Introduction: Why ask the question

Draft · 1,784 words · 7 October 2026

In the spring of 2026 I rode up Sa Calobra faster than I ever had. The climb on the north coast of Mallorca is nine and a half kilometres at about seven per cent, with twenty-six hairpins and nowhere to recover, and I took two minutes and eleven seconds off my best time.

I knew why, or thought I did. I had spent the winter in my garage on a smart trainer, four or five times a week, riding for 45 minutes to an hour at a heart rate of about 142 beats per minute. It was the dullest training I had ever done. Nothing hurt, nothing was worth posting, and my ride history turned into a column of identical flat grey lines. Then I went to Mallorca, rode the effort I had learned by heart all winter, and found there was a lot more power under it than before.

That winter was Zone 2 training, and I wrote about it with the enthusiasm of a convert. I told readers of my website that easy riding was the single most effective way to build mitochondria, the structures in muscle cells that use oxygen to make energy. I believed it. I had heard it from people who sounded as if they knew.

A few months later I sat down to read the research properly, and that sentence didn't survive. The largest summary of the evidence, a review of 353 training studies published in 2024, found that mitochondrial content rose by similar amounts whether people did steady continuous training (about 23%), high-intensity intervals (27%) or all-out sprint intervals (27%). Per hour of training, the sprints did almost four times as much [1]. Zone 2 had done something real for me. It just hadn't done it for the reason I had been told, or not only for that reason.

This book grew out of that gap: between what people confidently say about endurance training and what the studies actually show.

A short history of training fashions

Endurance training goes through fashions, and they tend to follow the same pattern. Someone finds something real in a study. It gets explained with a mechanism that sounds convincing. Then it spreads faster than the evidence behind it, until the claims have little to do with what was found.

In 1996 a Japanese research group led by Izumi Tabata had seven people do a few minutes of very hard 20-second efforts on a bike, with 10 seconds of rest between each, five days a week for six weeks. Both their aerobic fitness and their capacity for short, hard efforts improved [2]. It was a small study of a punishing protocol. Within a few years "Tabata" had become a name for almost any four-minute workout in a gym class, most of them nothing like what the study's subjects did.

Ten years later Martin Gibala's lab in Canada compared two small groups of active young men over two weeks. One group did short sessions of 30-second all-out sprints. The other did long steady rides, about four times as much training in total. Both groups improved by similar amounts [3]. It was a careful study with a striking result, and it launched a decade of articles promising the benefits of endurance training in a fraction of the time. Fewer of those articles mentioned that the study lasted two weeks, involved 16 people, and measured early changes in muscle, not what happens over a season.

Around the same time, the Norway-based physiologist Stephen Seiler was describing how the world's best endurance athletes actually trained. Across sports, he found, they did roughly four easy sessions for every hard one, with very little in between [4]. That observation became "80/20" and "polarised training", and for a while every training plan had to be polarised.

Then came Zone 2. Its rise owed a lot to podcasts, in particular a 2019 conversation between the physician Peter Attia and the physiologist Iñigo San-Millán, who worked with professional cycling teams [5]. San-Millán's research on how well athletes and patients burn fat and clear lactate was real and interesting. Repeated across thousands of episodes and social media posts, it became something larger: a training intensity with special powers over mitochondria, metabolism and even lifespan.

None of these fashions was simply wrong. High-intensity intervals do work, elite athletes do mostly train easy, and easy riding does build an aerobic base. What each fashion got wrong was its confidence, and its silence about who was studied, for how long, and what was actually measured.

What this book tries to do

There is no shortage of training advice. What's in short supply is a way of judging it. So this book has two aims, and every chapter works towards both.

The first is to explain the mechanism. For each training claim, I want you to understand the biology underneath it: what happens in a muscle, a heart or a bloodstream when you ride easy or ride hard, and why that might make you faster. Mechanisms matter because they tell you why something should work. They are also where fashions start, because a plausible mechanism is far easier to find than evidence that the training actually improves performance. "Easy riding burns the most fat, so it must build the most mitochondria" is a mechanism argument. Whether it's true is a different question, and the answer turned out to be no.

The second aim is to help you judge research yourself. Each chapter includes a short section called Reading the research, which takes one study from that chapter and uses it to practise a single skill: telling correlation from cause, spotting a sample too small to trust, noticing when "a trend" means "not significant", or asking who was actually studied. By the end of the book you should be able to read a training study, or a confident claim about one, and decide for yourself how much weight it can bear. The next fashion is already on its way, and this book won't be there when it arrives.

To keep the book honest about its own claims, the important ones carry a grade:

  • A: several randomised trials, or a well-conducted meta-analysis, pointing the same way.
  • B: consistent findings, but from small or short studies, a single lab, or one good trial.
  • C: observational data, mechanism, case studies or expert opinion.

A grade C claim isn't necessarily wrong. Most of what coaches know started as grade C, and some of it will become grade A. But you should know which is which, and so should anyone selling you a training plan.

How the book is organised

The book is built around one question, which is what actually limits how fast you can ride for an hour, or for five. Chapter 1 answers it with a model that physiologists have used for decades. Endurance performance depends on how much oxygen you can use at most (your VO₂max), how close to that maximum you can ride for a long time, and how efficiently you turn that oxygen into power. Recent research adds a fourth factor: how well those three hold up when you're tired. Almost every training question in this book comes down to which of those four a session changes, and how strong the evidence is.

Part I, The engine, sets out the model, the biology of how bodies adapt to training, how to read a training study, and how to define and find your training intensities. Part II, The easy part, covers what low-intensity riding does to the body, why elite athletes do so much of it, durability, and the claims about health and longevity. Part III, The hard part, covers intervals: why time spent close to VO₂max seems to matter, how to design a session, and how many hard sessions a week make sense. Part IV puts it together into a training week, deals with special cases such as masters riders, women and riding indoors, and looks at how to tell whether any of it is working. The last chapter answers the title's question.

You can read the chapters in order or dip into the ones you care about. If you skip anything, don't skip Chapters 2 and 3. The biology and the research skills in those two chapters are what the rest of the book builds on.

Who this is for

This book is written for cyclists who want to understand their training rather than just follow it. It gets technical where the science needs it to, and it names researchers and studies throughout, because "studies show" is exactly the habit the book is trying to break. You don't need a science background. You do need some patience with numbers.

Most of the research is in cyclists, and where it isn't, I say so. Plenty of the best training studies were done in runners, rowers and cross-country skiers, and I use them where the physiology carries across.

This is not a training plan, and it isn't medical advice. If you have a heart condition or another medical reason to be careful with hard exercise, talk to a doctor before you change how you train.

Chapter 1: What limits endurance

Draft · 3,405 words · 7 October 2026

In 1923 the physiologist Archibald Hill and his colleague Hartley Lupton described something that every endurance athlete now takes for granted. As exercise gets harder, the body takes up more oxygen, until it reaches a ceiling. Beyond that point the work can continue for a while, but the extra energy has to come from somewhere other than oxygen, and it can't come from there for long [1]. That ceiling became known as maximal oxygen uptake, or VO₂max, and for most of the following century it was the number physiologists reached for first when they wanted to explain why one athlete was faster than another.

It explains less than you might think. Two riders can have the same VO₂max and finish an hour-long time trial minutes apart. A rider with a modest VO₂max can beat one with a much bigger engine. To see why, you need the rest of the model, and that model is what this chapter is about. It is also the framework for the whole book. Every training claim in later chapters comes down to a simple question: which part of this model does it change?

The question

Strip away tactics, aerodynamics and the weather, and endurance performance comes down to one thing: how much power you can sustain for the length of the event. For a 40-minute effort or a two-hour climb, that power comes almost entirely from aerobic metabolism, the process in which muscle cells use oxygen to release energy from fat and carbohydrate.

The real question, then, is what sets the rate at which you can produce energy aerobically, and how much of that energy ends up turning the pedals.

In 2008 the physiologists Michael Joyner and Edward Coyle set out the answer that is still the standard one [2]. It has three parts:

  1. VO₂max, the maximum rate at which you can take up and use oxygen.
  2. The fraction of VO₂max you can sustain, which depends on where your lactate threshold sits.
  3. Efficiency, the share of the energy you produce that ends up as power at the pedals.

Multiply the first two and you get what Joyner and Coyle call your performance VO₂, the rate of oxygen use you can hold for the duration of a race. Multiply that by your efficiency and you have your sustainable power. The rest of this chapter takes each part in turn, then adds a fourth that the original model left out.

How it works: from air to muscle

Before the three factors make sense, it helps to follow oxygen from the air to the muscle.

You breathe it into your lungs, where it crosses into the blood and binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Your heart pumps that blood out to the body. The amount it can pump each minute, the cardiac output, is your heart rate multiplied by the volume of blood ejected with each beat, the stroke volume. In the working leg muscles, blood flows through a dense mesh of tiny vessels called capillaries, and oxygen moves out of the blood into the muscle fibres. Inside each fibre it reaches the mitochondria, small structures that use it to break down fuel and make ATP, the molecule that powers muscle contraction.

Any link in that chain could, in principle, set the ceiling. In practice, the evidence points mostly at delivery: how much oxygen the heart and blood can bring to the muscle. Bassett and Howley's influential review set out three lines of evidence [3]. When oxygen delivery is changed, by blood doping, by breathing thin air at altitude, or by drugs that slow the heart, VO₂max changes with it. When people train, the rise in VO₂max comes mainly from a bigger maximum cardiac output, not from muscles extracting more oxygen from each litre of blood. And when a small muscle group is given more blood than it would normally get, it can consume oxygen at a far higher rate than it does during whole-body exercise, which suggests the muscle could use more oxygen if only it were delivered. A

Joyner and Coyle identify the size of the heart's stroke volume as the most important single difference between champions and everyone else, alongside a larger blood volume, more capillaries and more mitochondria in the trained muscles [2]. Those last two matter for a different reason, covered in the next section: they don't raise the ceiling much, but they change how close to it you can ride.

The mechanics of how training changes the heart, the blood and the muscle are the subject of Chapter 2. For now, the important point is that there are two broad sites of adaptation: the delivery system, which mostly sets VO₂max, and the muscle itself, which mostly sets how much of it you can use.

VO₂max: the ceiling

Champion endurance athletes have VO₂max values of roughly 70 to 85 millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute, about 50 to 100% higher than healthy, untrained young adults [2]. Elite women's values tend to be about 10% lower than elite men's, which Joyner and Coyle attribute mainly to lower haemoglobin concentration and a higher proportion of body fat.

A high VO₂max is close to a requirement for elite endurance performance. You won't find a Tour de France winner with an average one. But among athletes who are already good, VO₂max becomes a poor predictor of who wins, because the riders in a professional peloton all have high values. Once the ceiling is high enough, the other two factors decide most of the differences that remain.

VO₂max is also only partly trainable. It rises quickly in untrained people and much more slowly in those who have trained for years, and part of it is set by genetics. That matters later in the book, when we look at how much of the case for high-intensity intervals rests on VO₂max, and how much of the case for easy riding rests on everything else.

The fraction you can sustain

Nobody can ride at 100% of their VO₂max for long. The question is how close they can get for an hour, and the answer depends on what happens to lactate in the blood as effort rises.

At low intensities, blood lactate stays close to its resting level. As intensity rises, it reaches a point where lactate starts to accumulate, and above a second, higher point it keeps rising until the effort has to stop. The first of these points is the lactate threshold that Chapter 4 explores in detail. For now, the useful fact is where it sits. In untrained people, lactate begins to rise noticeably at around 60% of VO₂max. In elite endurance athletes it can sit at 75 to 90% [2]. A

That shift is one of the largest effects of endurance training, and it has little to do with the heart. It comes mainly from changes in the muscle: more mitochondria, more of the enzymes that use oxygen, better capacity to burn fat, and so less reliance on producing lactate at a given rate of work [3]. Bassett and Howley argue that the lactate threshold is the best single physiological predictor of endurance performance, because it combines all three factors in one number: how big the engine is, how much of it you can use, and how economically you use it.

This is where the case for large volumes of easy riding begins. If easy training builds mitochondria and capillaries, it should raise the fraction of VO₂max you can sustain, even if it does relatively little for VO₂max itself. That's a mechanism argument. Whether the training studies bear it out is a question for Part II.

Efficiency: turning oxygen into watts

The third factor is the easiest to overlook. Of the energy your muscles release from oxygen and fuel, only a fraction ends up as power at the cranks. The rest becomes heat. In cyclists, that fraction, the gross efficiency, typically ranges from about 18.5% to 23.5% at 300 watts [2]. Joyner and Coyle estimate that efficiency varies by 20 to 30% between cyclists, which is a large range for something that rarely appears in training discussions.

Taking the two ends of that range, a rider with a gross efficiency of 23% produces about a quarter more power from the same oxygen than a rider at 18.5% [2]. No training block raises VO₂max by a quarter in a trained cyclist.

The best-known evidence on why some riders are more efficient than others comes from Edward Coyle's lab in 1992. In 19 highly trained cyclists, the proportion of slow-twitch (type I) fibres in the thigh muscle ranged from 32% to 76%, and riders with more of them were markedly more efficient: the correlation between type I fibre percentage and one measure of efficiency was 0.85 [4]. Type I fibres are the ones built for sustained, aerobic work, and they seem to produce force with less energy wasted as heat. B

Whether training can increase efficiency much, and how quickly, is less clear. Joyner and Coyle suggest it may improve by 1 to 3% a year with continued endurance training [2]. The most famous evidence for that idea is a single case study, and it is worth looking at closely.

Reading the research: what one athlete can and can't tell us

In 2005 Coyle published a paper following one cyclist from age 21 to 28 [5]. Over those seven years, the paper reported, the rider's VO₂max stayed at about 6 litres a minute, but his muscular efficiency rose by 8%, and together with losing body fat before each race season, his steady-state power per kilogram at a given rate of oxygen use rose by 18%. The paper suggested the improvement probably reflected changes in muscle proteins after years of training intensely for three to six hours on most days. The rider was described as a six-time consecutive champion of the Tour de France. He was Lance Armstrong.

The paper was widely cited as evidence that efficiency improves with years of training. It also drew immediate challenges. In the same journal and the same year, a published exchange asked whether Armstrong's efficiency had improved at all [6]. In 2008, a letter from four researchers was published under the blunt title "Delta efficiency calculation in Tour de France champion is wrong" [7]. And in 2012 the United States Anti-Doping Agency found that Armstrong had doped, and his Tour de France results from 1999 onwards were disqualified [8].

The specific dispute about the calculation is technical. The general lesson isn't, and it applies far beyond this one paper. A case study follows one person. It can't separate the effect of training from everything else that changed in that person's life over the same period: equipment, testing methods, illness, weight, and, in this case, drugs that alter blood and muscle. Measurements taken years apart, on different occasions, may not be comparable at all. Case studies are valuable for raising questions. They can't answer them.

When you see a training claim resting on one athlete, however famous, treat it as grade C at best. Efficiency may well improve over years of training. This paper can't show it.

One study, up close: when efficiency makes up for a smaller engine

In 2002 Alejandro Lucía and colleagues in Madrid tested 11 world-class professional road cyclists [9]. Their average VO₂max was 72 mL/kg/min, firmly in the elite range. Each rode a ramp test to find their VO₂max, then 20 minutes at a steady power equal to 80% of it, which averaged 385 watts. From the second test, the researchers calculated each rider's efficiency, which averaged 24.5%.

The striking result was the relationship between the two. Riders with a higher VO₂max tended to be less efficient, and riders with a lower VO₂max tended to be more efficient. The correlations were around −0.65 to −0.72, depending on how VO₂max was scaled for body size. The authors concluded that, at the top of the sport, high efficiency seems to compensate for a relatively low VO₂max.

It's a small study, and it describes riders who had already reached the top. That matters. If you only study successful professionals, you only see combinations that worked: a rider with a modest VO₂max and modest efficiency wouldn't be in the sample, because they wouldn't be a world-class professional. The study can't tell us that training trades one factor for the other, and it doesn't claim to. What it shows is that there is more than one route to the same performance, and that VO₂max alone would have ranked these riders wrongly. B

Putting the three together

Because the three factors multiply, a change in any of them changes sustainable power in proportion. Take two riders, as an illustration. Both have the same VO₂max. One can sustain 80% of it for an hour and the other 75%. All else equal, the first rider's sustainable power is about 7% higher, simply because 80 is about 7% more than 75. Now make the second rider 7% more efficient, and the two are level again.

That multiplication explains several things that puzzle riders. It's why VO₂max alone ranks experienced riders poorly. It's why a training block that leaves your VO₂max unchanged can still make you faster, if it raises your threshold or your efficiency. And it's why arguments about whether easy riding or hard intervals are "better" often talk past each other: they may be arguing about different factors. Broadly, and with the details still to come, the case for high-intensity training rests heavily on VO₂max, and the case for large amounts of easy training rests on the fraction you can sustain and, possibly, on efficiency.

Joyner and Coyle described elite performance as the product of these factors working together, which is why an elite cyclist can hold around 400 watts for an extended effort [2]. They also warned that this physiological picture leaves things out: the nervous system's role in fatigue, motivation and the realities of racing. Their model explains a great deal, and it doesn't claim to explain everything.

The power–duration curve and critical power

There's another way to describe what limits endurance, and it's the one that power meters have made familiar. Ask a rider to produce as much power as possible for 10 seconds, then for a minute, five minutes, twenty and an hour. Plot the results, and you get a curve that falls steeply at first and then flattens out. Riders who use training software know it as their power curve.

In 1965 the French physiologists Hugues Monod and Jean Scherrer noticed something about that curve's shape [10]. For efforts lasting from a couple of minutes to the best part of an hour, the relationship between power and the time it can be held follows a simple mathematical form, a hyperbola, which flattens towards a particular power. They called that power the critical power.

The idea has held up remarkably well. A 2016 review led by David Poole and Andrew Jones describes the hyperbolic power–duration relationship as consistent across species, types of exercise and individual muscles [11]. For cycling it reduces to two numbers. Critical power is the boundary: the highest power at which the body's responses, including oxygen uptake and lactate, can still settle to a steady state. Above it, they can't, and the effort will end. The second number, known as W′ (pronounced W prime), is the fixed amount of work you can do above critical power before that happens. Ride 50 watts above your critical power and you'll use up W′ in a predictable time. Ride 100 watts above it and you'll use it up roughly twice as fast.

Critical power also divides exercise into the intensity domains that will come up throughout this book. Below the lactate threshold is the moderate domain. Between the lactate threshold and critical power is the heavy domain. Above critical power is the severe domain. Martin Burnley and Andrew Jones showed that fatigue behaves differently in each [12]. In the moderate domain it develops slowly and seems to come mainly from the nervous system. In the heavy domain, both the nervous system and the muscles fatigue, often in step with falling muscle glycogen. In the severe domain, the muscle's internal chemistry is progressively disrupted until it can't continue, and that happens faster the further above critical power you ride. They also make a point every cyclist has felt: the range of power you can actually sustain is remarkably narrow. {grade A for the shape of the curve; grade B for the details of fatigue in each domain}

Critical power isn't a fourth factor alongside the other three. It's a different view of the same physiology, because where critical power sits depends on all three: how much oxygen you can use, how much of it you can sustain without losing control of your muscle chemistry, and how efficiently you turn it into power. Its advantage is practical. You can estimate it from a power meter, and it gives researchers a way to measure how the whole system holds up when you're tired.

The fourth factor: durability

Every number in this chapter so far is usually measured on a rested rider. Laboratory tests are done in the morning, after an easy day, with fresh legs. Races aren't.

In 2021 a group of physiologists led by Ed Maunder argued that this was a serious gap. The attributes measured in routine testing aren't fixed: they shift during prolonged exercise, and models that ignore those shifts will misjudge how athletes perform and how they should pace [13]. They proposed measuring durability: when, and by how much, those characteristics deteriorate as exercise goes on.

The size of the problem is now becoming clear. In a 2019 study from Andrew Jones's group, 16 people did a three-minute all-out test fresh, and again after 40 minutes, 80 minutes and two hours of hard cycling. Their critical power held up for the first 80 minutes, but after two hours it was 9% lower [14]. In a 2024 review, Jones described critical power falling by about 10% on average after two hours of heavy-intensity cycling, with individual changes ranging from less than 1% to about 32% [15]. He argued that this resilience to fatigue should be treated as a fourth dimension of endurance performance, alongside the three in Joyner and Coyle's model. B

That range is the important part. Two riders who look identical in a fresh lab test could be almost a third apart after two hours. For anyone training for long events, that may matter more than a few per cent of VO₂max. Chapter 8 looks at durability in detail: how it's measured, what the professional peloton shows, and what, if anything, trains it.

What this means for training

The model gives every training question in this book the same shape. A session can make you faster by raising your VO₂max, by raising the fraction of it you can sustain, by improving your efficiency, by making all three hold up better late in a ride, or by some combination. Arguments about training methods usually turn out to be arguments about which of these a method changes, and by how much.

The rest of the book takes those questions one at a time. Chapter 2 looks at the biology of adaptation, the chain of events by which a training session, repeated often enough, becomes a bigger heart, more capillaries or more mitochondria. Chapter 3 is about judging the studies. Then Part II asks what easy riding does, and Part III asks the same of hard intervals.

Carry one thing forward from this chapter. Each of the four factors can be trained, but they respond to different stimuli, on different timescales and to different degrees in different people, and some of them respond slowly. A training method that claims to improve everything, for everyone, is claiming more than physiology allows.

175 kilometres alone

The final stage of the 2026 Tour de Luxembourg ran 177 km from Mersch to the Limpertsberg district of Luxembourg City. Mathieu van der Poel rode clear in the opening kilometres and stayed clear to the line. He said afterwards that it hadn't been the plan. He had found himself alone and assumed the chasers would come back. With 45 km to go, when the bunch swept up the riders who had been pursuing him, he still had three and a half minutes in hand, and he won by 1:04 from Mikkel Honoré [1].

Tadej Pogačar has made a habit of the same thing from shorter range. At Strade Bianche in March 2024 he attacked on the Monte Sante Marie gravel sector with 81 km left and won by 2:44. At the World Championships in Zürich that September he rode the last 50 km alone and held off Ben O'Connor by 34 seconds [2].

It is tempting to read these rides as proof of a bigger engine, but a result on its own can't prove that. Van der Poel's lead came down by almost two and a half minutes in the last 45 km [1]. That could be a rider fading or a bunch finally racing for the finish, and nothing public tells us which. His power in the first hour and the last hasn't been released, so nobody outside his team can say how much he faded.

What the rides do is pose a question that the model in Chapter 1 leaves open. VO₂max, the fraction of it you can sustain, and efficiency are all measured on a rider who is fresh. Races are decided four or five hours in. How much of what you had at the start do you still have at the end?

Most of us meet that question well below the level of a World Championship.

From the saddle. In 2025 I rode a tour of the Pyrenees. On the fourth day I was really struggling, while one of my friends in particular seemed to be getting stronger. He's an ex-professional golfer and spends a lot of his time on the golf course.

I can't tell you why he got stronger while I got weaker, and the research can't yet either. Most of it concerns a single long ride, and little of it reaches a fourth day in a row. But the thing I was short of that day now has a name and a growing set of measurements behind it.

What durability is, and what to call it

In 2021 a group led by Ed Maunder in Auckland argued that physiologists had been profiling athletes in the wrong state. Stephen Seiler, whose work on training intensity runs through this book, was among the authors. Lab tests are done rested. The qualities those tests measure shift during long exercise, and they shift by different amounts in different people. Maunder and his colleagues proposed the word durability for this, and defined it as the time of onset and the size of the deterioration in an athlete's physiological characteristics during prolonged exercise [3].

Three years later Andrew Jones, the Exeter physiologist whose lab worked with Nike's two-hour marathon project, made the stronger claim. He argued that this quality belongs in the Joyner and Coyle model as a fourth factor alongside the original three, and he called it physiological resilience: the ability to resist functional decline under stress [4]. The two terms describe the same thing. This book uses durability, because that is the word cyclists and coaches have adopted.

The idea is easier to hold once you see how it is measured. There are three main ways.

The first is to test a rider fresh, tire them, and test them again. The clearest examples come from Jones's own lab. In one study, 16 men did a three-minute all-out test fresh, and again after 40 minutes, 80 minutes and two hours of steady riding [5]. The test estimates critical power, the boundary described in Chapter 1 above which effort can't be sustained, and W′, the small reserve of work available above it. Critical power was 260 W fresh, 268 W after 40 minutes and 260 W after 80 minutes. After two hours it was 236 W, a fall of 9%. W′ went sooner: it was down 18% by 80 minutes and 23% by two hours. So the reserve you draw on for attacks and short climbs drains first, and the power you can hold for an hour stays put for a surprisingly long time and then drops.

Two details of that study deserve more attention than they usually get. The riders were competitive amateurs with an average VO₂peak of 52.5 mL/kg/min, much like the readers of this book and nothing like van der Poel. And the two-hour ride that cost them 9% of their critical power averaged 164 W, which began at about 64% of their VO₂peak [5]. That is a steady endurance ride just above the top of Zone 2. Nobody was being broken.

Riders differed far more than the average suggests. Across three studies from the same lab, the fall in critical power after two hours averaged about 10%, but individual riders ranged from under 1% to about a third [4]. B On a fresh critical power of 260 W, that is the difference between losing a watt or two and losing more than 80.

The second way to measure durability needs no lab. Researchers with access to professional riders' power files look for each rider's best efforts over set durations, then look again at the best efforts produced after a given amount of work has already been done that day. The work is counted in kilojoules per kilogram of body weight, the same kilojoules a bike computer shows. For a 70 kg rider, 45 kJ/kg is 3,150 kJ, roughly four and a half hours at an average of 195 W. Most of what is known about durability in professionals comes from this method, and a later section looks at what it shows.

The third way is to watch what drifts during a long steady effort: heart rate climbing at constant power, or the oxygen cost of that power creeping up. This is the version you can observe on your own rides, and the thresholds that define your training zones drift too.

In 2022 Maunder's group took 14 trained cyclists and triathletes, people training about nine hours a week, and measured the power at their first ventilatory threshold (VT1), which marks the top of Zone 2. Then the riders did two hours at 90% of that power, an average of 194 W, and the threshold was measured again [6]. It had fallen from 217 W to 196 W, a drop of 10%. Individual falls ranged from about 9 W to 44 W. The heart rate at the threshold had moved the other way, from 142 to 151 bpm.

After two hours of riding in Zone 2, then, the top of Zone 2 was 21 W lower and 9 bpm higher than the fresh test said [6]. A rider holding fixed power drifts towards the threshold from below. A rider holding fixed heart rate sees their power sink. One caution: the riders had fasted overnight and drank only water, and the authors expect the shift to be smaller in someone who has eaten and is taking in carbohydrate. There was also no control trial in which the riders simply sat for two hours between tests, although it is hard to see why sitting would lower a threshold by 10%. B

How it works

The reviews of this field are candid that nobody can yet fully explain why two hours of steady riding lowers the power you can sustain [4,7]. There are several candidate mechanisms, each with some evidence, and none of them yet explains why one rider loses 1% and another 30%. What follows is the current best account, with the gaps marked.

Start with what critical power is. Below it, oxygen uptake rises when you start riding and then settles. Above it, oxygen uptake keeps climbing until it reaches VO₂max and you have to stop. Critical power is the highest power at which uptake can still settle. It follows that anything which raises the oxygen cost of each watt, or lowers the amount of oxygen the body can deliver, will lower critical power, without anything having gone wrong in the usual sense of the word.

Both happen during a long ride.

Take the cost first. In the two-hour rides from Jones's lab, the power was held constant and oxygen uptake was not. In one study the same power went from 64% to 68% of VO₂peak over the two hours [5]; in another, from 65% to 72% [8]. The rider's computer shows the same number throughout while the body works progressively harder to produce it.

Part of this is fuel. As a ride goes on and carbohydrate stores run down, the muscles burn proportionally more fat. In the second of those studies, carbohydrate use fell from 1.77 to 1.17 grams a minute while fat use rose from 0.68 to 1.08 [8]. Fat is the larger store by far, but it needs more oxygen than carbohydrate does to produce the same amount of ATP [7]. Jones and his colleague Brett Kirby estimate that the change in fuel accounts for roughly 30% of the rise in oxygen uptake over two hours. So fuel is part of the explanation and the smaller part.

A second contributor is which muscle fibres are doing the work. Chapter 1 introduced type I fibres, the slow, efficient ones. They are recruited first and do most of the work at moderate power. As those fibres tire or run low on glycogen, the nervous system has to call on fibres further up the recruitment order to keep the power constant. Those type II fibres can produce the force, but they use more ATP and more oxygen to do it [7]. The power on the screen is unchanged while a more expensive set of fibres is producing a growing share of it. The reviews favour this as the explanation for much of the rest of the rising oxygen cost. It is well supported as a general principle of how muscle is recruited. How much of the fall in critical power it accounts for in any particular rider has not been measured.

Delivery is the other side. Over a long ride you lose fluid as sweat, which lowers blood volume. Your core temperature rises, and blood is diverted to the skin to shed heat, where it competes with the working muscles. The heart fills a little less with each beat and beats faster to compensate. This is cardiovascular drift, the slow rise in heart rate at constant power that everyone who rides with a heart rate monitor has seen. In the Auckland study heart rate rose 8.2% over two hours at constant power [6]. If the drift goes far enough, the maximum amount of oxygen the heart can deliver falls, and VO₂max, the thresholds and critical power can fall with it [7].

Heat makes all of this worse, and it is the mechanism I have felt most directly.

From the saddle. My power dropped massively during the heatwave of 2026, when the training room was 37 degrees.

Riding indoors in the heat is taken up properly in a later chapter. The point here is that part of what feels like fading legs is the circulation.

Then there is glycogen. The oldest explanation for fading late in a ride is that the muscles run out of stored carbohydrate, and nobody doubts that glycogen matters in long exercise. What Jones's lab found is that it doesn't neatly explain the fall in critical power. They took muscle biopsies from 14 men before and after two hours of riding. Glycogen fell by about 65% and critical power by about 11%, but the riders who lost the most glycogen were not the riders who lost the most critical power: the correlation between the two was 0.19, which is close to none [8].

That result is often repeated as "it's not about glycogen", which goes further than the data. The authors themselves point out that a biopsy samples a tiny piece of one muscle, and that a real relationship could be hidden by that alone. A study of 14 people is also too small to detect a relationship of moderate size reliably, a problem the Reading the research box in this chapter returns to. The fair summary is that in these data, glycogen loss did not predict who faded most. The same study found that the rise in oxygen cost did not predict it either. The two most obvious explanations both fail to account for the differences between riders, and the question is open. C

Carbohydrate during the ride is a clearer story. When the riders in the earlier study repeated the two-hour ride drinking 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour, their critical power afterwards was 254 W, against 236 W on a placebo and 260 W fresh [5]. Carbohydrate removed most of the fall. It did so without sparing muscle glycogen, which dropped by almost the same amount in both trials. Blood glucose and the rate of carbohydrate burning late in the ride were higher with the drink, and the authors' reading is that a steady supply of glucose from the blood kept the working fibres going. Fuelling gets its own section later in this chapter, with a larger and more recent trial.

Put together, the picture is of a rider whose watts become more expensive as the hours pass, whose oxygen supply slowly tightens, and whose fuel is shifting towards the kind that costs more oxygen to burn. Each change is modest. They compound, because critical power sits where supply and cost meet.

This also suggests why training might improve durability, and here the chapter moves from evidence to inference. If the trouble starts when type I fibres tire and more costly fibres take over, then anything that lets type I fibres carry the load for longer should delay it: more capillaries around them, more mitochondria inside them, a greater capacity to burn fat. Those are among the adaptations to endurance training described in Chapter 2. It is a plausible chain of reasoning, and it is the reasoning behind the long easy ride. Whether training actually changes durability, and which training, is a question for trials, and the rest of this chapter looks at how few there are.

Durability in the wild

It would be satisfying to say that van der Poel or Pogačar has exceptional durability, and plenty of commentary does. Here is what can be known from outside.

The results give distances, times and gaps. For the Luxembourg stage that means 177 km, a lead of 3:30 with 45 km left and 1:04 at the line [1]. They don't give power. A rider's speed on a long climb can be turned into an estimate of power, but the estimate rests on a guess at his weight, the wind and how long he sat in someone's slipstream.

The published science has the opposite limitation. The largest study of professional power files covers 112 riders and reports them as two anonymous groups [9]. It can tell you what WorldTour riders as a class do after 45 kJ/kg of work, and nothing about any one of them.

That is why this chapter makes no claim about van der Poel's physiology or Pogačar's. Their rides are here because they are what durability looks like from the roadside. Everything the chapter concludes comes from riders who were measured.

What the professionals show

Professional cyclists are the best-studied group in this field, for a practical reason: they race with power meters, their teams keep every file, and a season of racing contains maximal efforts made at every stage of fatigue.

The study with the most riders comes from a Spanish group led by Manuel Mateo-March, using data collected between 2013 and 2021 from 112 male professionals: 66 from the WorldTour, the sport's top division, and 46 from ProTeams, the division below [9]. For each rider they found the best power ever recorded for durations from 10 seconds to two hours. Then they found the best power for the same durations among efforts made after 15, 25, 35 and 45 kJ/kg of work that day.

Power fell at every duration as the work mounted up. After 15 kJ/kg the losses were between 1.6% and 3.0%, depending on the duration. After 45 kJ/kg they were between 6.0% and 9.7% [9].

The comparison between divisions is what made the study widely cited. Fresh, the WorldTour riders and the ProTeam riders were hard to tell apart: their best power did not differ consistently. After 45 kJ/kg the WorldTour riders had lost 4.7% to 8.8% and the ProTeam riders 7.6% to 11.6%, with the gap showing mainly in efforts of five minutes and longer [9]. On these figures, what separates the top division from the second is less what riders can do in the first hour than what they can still do in the last.

Other groups have found the same pattern lower down the ladder. Among 53 climbers studied by Gabriele Gallo and colleagues, the fade after 20 to 50 kJ/kg was the only measure that separated the best professionals from the best under-23 riders, although not every comparison in that study reached statistical significance [10]. During the 2020 Vuelta a España, eight WorldTour and seven ProTeam riders were compared week by week. Fresh, the WorldTour riders were better only over 30 minutes. After 35 kJ/kg they were better over 30 seconds, one minute, five minutes, 20 minutes and 30 minutes [11]. That study is as close as the research comes to my fourth day in the Pyrenees, and with 15 riders it is a small one. B

Before taking these numbers home, it helps to see how far away they are. In a lab study of trained amateurs described later in this chapter, three hours at 169 W came to 24 kJ/kg [12]. The professionals' 45 kJ/kg is nearly double that. It describes the closing stages of a long professional race, a state few amateurs ever ride themselves into.

The method has a weakness, and it is worth understanding because power-file studies are becoming common. The "fatigued" figures are the best efforts a rider happened to make late in a ride. Nobody asked him to make them. A rider produces a maximal five-minute effort after 45 kJ/kg only if the race puts him in a position where it is worth doing, which means he is in the front group on the last climb. A domestique who has finished his work for the day rides in at whatever pace gets him to the bus. Better riders are more often in the finale, so they have more chances to record a high number late on. Some of the gap between divisions may be opportunity. This is my own reading of the method, and I have not seen the size of the effect estimated.

A second complication is that kilojoules are a blunt measure of how tired someone is. Two thousand kilojoules ridden steadily in the bunch and 2,000 that include repeated attacks are different days. A group led by Peter Leo counted only the work done above critical power and found that in under-23 riders, power for efforts of a minute or longer fell significantly after 5 kJ/kg of it, while professionals held on until 7.5 kJ/kg [13]. A 2026 systematic review of 21 studies reached the same conclusion more generally: hard work does more damage per kilojoule than easy work, with losses of 10% to 20% reported after as little as 2.5 to 15 kJ/kg of high-intensity riding [14]. For anyone comparing their own files, the lesson is to compare like with like. Power after three steady hours and power after a three-hour chain gang are answers to different questions.

Semi-professionals look somewhat different again. A Dutch-led group tested 16 of them three times across a season, fresh and after about 38 kJ/kg. One-minute power fell when they were tired. Ten-minute power did not [15]. That sits awkwardly beside the professional data, where the differences showed mainly in longer efforts, and I don't have an explanation for it beyond the obvious ones: a different level of rider, a lab protocol in place of race files, and 16 people.

Whether durability can be read off an ordinary lab test is disputed. In the amateur riders tested in Jones's lab, the fall in critical power was unrelated to VO₂max or to critical power when fresh [7]. In 10 young professionals studied by James Spragg and colleagues, the change in critical power after a set of hard efforts correlated very strongly with VO₂max, threshold power and efficiency, with correlations between 0.74 and 0.89 [16]. Ten riders is a very small group for claims of that kind, and the Reading the research box below explains why those numbers deserve less trust than they seem to. For now the fair statement is that nobody knows whether durability is a separate quality or mostly a reflection of being fit.

What trains it

This is the part of the chapter with the weakest evidence, and the people who know the field best say so. Jones and Kirby's 2025 review, the most thorough attempt to answer the question, describes the data as "scant" and states that no long-term study has yet set out to find the training that best improves durability [7]. What exists is one 10-week training trial in beginners, one study of strength training in trained cyclists, and a set of comparisons between groups: trained against untrained, faster against slower, professionals against under-23s.

Take the comparisons first. Trained runners drift less than active adults who don't run over an hour at the same relative intensity: in one study of 20 people the relative intensity rose by 2.6% in the trained group and 8.3% in the others [17]. Faster runners lose less running economy over 90 minutes than slower ones, 2.4% against 4.5% in a study of 44 men [18]. The same study, as Jones and Kirby describe it, found that among runners of similar ability, those who regularly ran for longer than 90 minutes held their economy better than those who didn't [7]. Professionals fade less than under-23s. Each comparison points the same way, and none can separate cause from effect. People who are naturally durable may find long sessions easier and so do more of them.

Spragg's group came closer by following 30 under-23 professionals through a season. Riders who spent more time below the first threshold improved their two-minute power when fatigued, and riders whose training became more polarised improved their fatigued 12-minute power [19]. The correlations were around 0.4. A correlation of that size is loose, and it is still observation: nobody was assigned to ride more easy hours.

The one trial that assigned people to easy or hard riding comes from Finland. Pekka Matomäki and colleagues took 35 sedentary or recreationally active adults and gave them 10 weeks of either low-intensity riding, averaging 6.8 hours a week, or high-intensity intervals, averaging 1.6 hours a week [20]. Before and after, each did a three-hour ride at an easy fixed power while the researchers tracked how much heart rate, energy cost and other measures drifted, and when the drift began.

Durability improved in both groups, and by about the same amount. In the low-intensity group the average drift went from 7.7% to 6.3% and its onset moved from 106 to 131 minutes, changes that fell just short of statistical significance. In the interval group the drift went from 8.8% to 5.4% and the onset from 108 to 137 minutes, both significant [20]. There was no significant difference between the groups.

Read with care, that trial says three things. Training of either kind makes beginners more durable. Four times the hours of easy riding bought no more durability than the intervals did. And the changes were small. It can't tell you what happens in a rider with five years of training behind them, or over a year instead of 10 weeks, and the reviews' main suggestion is precisely that durability comes from volume accumulated over years [7]. A 10-week trial cannot test that idea. B for the claim that training improves durability in untrained people.

The study in trained cyclists was of strength training. Bent Rønnestad's group in Norway had 11 well-trained cyclists add heavy leg strength training twice a week for 12 weeks while nine carried on with endurance training alone [21]. Afterwards each rode for just over three hours at an easy pace and then did a five-minute all-out effort. The strength group's power in that effort rose from 371 W to 400 W. The control group's didn't change. It is one study of 20 riders, but it is an intervention in trained cyclists, measured late in a long ride, and the thing that worked was heavy weights. Strength training is covered in a later chapter. It belongs here as a reminder that the long ride is not the only candidate.

That leaves the long easy ride. The case for it is the mechanism described earlier, the cross-sectional comparisons, and Spragg's season of data. Against it stands nothing. No trial has shown that long rides fail to improve durability. There is simply no trial in trained riders that has tested them. I think the long ride is a reasonable bet. I would not tell you it has been shown to work. C

There is one more observation to add, and it is only an observation. My friend in the Pyrenees, the one getting stronger on day four, spends a lot of his time on the golf course. If he walks his rounds, that is many hours a week of very low-intensity exercise, kept up for years. It fits the volume-over-years idea neatly, and a single golfer proves nothing.

Fuelling is a different matter, because there the trials exist.

From the saddle. One thing I try to do is out-fuel the riders I'm riding with. I can be a bit of a greedy pig, but that often helps keep me at the front of the group and gives me a strong finish in sportives.

The study from Jones's lab described earlier found that 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour removed most of the fall in critical power over two hours [5]. A larger and more recent trial tested whether more is better.

One study, up close: carbohydrate dose and critical power

In 2026 a group at Liverpool John Moores University recruited 16 trained cyclists and triathletes, 15 men and one woman, with an average age of 35 and an average VO₂max of 51.8 mL/kg/min. All trained more than five hours a week [12]. They are a fair match for the readers of this book.

Each rider's critical power was measured fresh with a three-minute all-out test. Then, on three separate days at least six days apart, each rode for three hours at 95% of the power at their gas exchange threshold, which is the top of Zone 2 by another name. It averaged 169 W [12]. Straight afterwards they did the three-minute test again. On one day they drank only water, on another a drink supplying 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour, and on the third 120 grams an hour, as a mix of maltodextrin and fructose. The order was randomised and balanced, so that any effect of doing the rides first or last was spread evenly across the three conditions. This is a crossover design: every rider is compared with themselves, which removes the differences between people that swamp small studies.

The riders were well fed going in. For 24 hours before each ride they ate a supplied diet containing 8 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight, and they had a set breakfast [12]. Nobody started short of glycogen.

Fresh, critical power averaged 277 W. After three hours on water it was 236 W, a fall of about 15%. With 60 grams an hour it was 257 W, a fall of about 7%. With 120 grams an hour it was 266 W, a fall of about 4%. Each step was statistically significant. W′, the reserve above critical power, dropped by a similar amount whatever the riders drank [12].

Three things in the details matter. The first step did most of the work: going from nothing to 60 grams an hour saved 20 W, and doubling it saved 9 W more. Stomach complaints were mild at every dose, though fullness and cramping scored higher at 120 grams than on water. And during the three-hour rides themselves, oxygen cost and efficiency did not differ between the drinks, so the carbohydrate was not working by making each watt cheaper. The riders on the highest dose could simply get closer to their VO₂max in the final test [12].

What the study can't tell us is just as specific. The paper describes no attempt to disguise which drink was which, so the riders presumably knew when they were on water, and a three-minute all-out test depends on how hard someone is willing to go. Fifteen of the 16 were men. Carbohydrate was supplied by a supplement company, although the funding came from a public research foundation. Critical power was estimated from a single short test, and the authors themselves think the method may have understated the fall. And it measured one all-out effort after one ride. So it shows carbohydrate protecting late-ride power on the day, and leaves open whether fuelling well in training makes you more durable in the long run. B

Reading the research: correlation and small samples

Two studies in this chapter reported correlations, and they show two different ways a correlation can mislead.

Spragg's season-long study found that riders who spent more time below the first threshold improved their fatigued two-minute power, with a correlation of 0.43 [19]. Squaring a correlation gives the share of the variation it accounts for. Here that is 18%, which leaves 82% to everything else. Then there is the direction. Easy riding might build durability. Or riders who are already durable, or who are simply healthy and uninjured that year, might be the ones able to pile up easy hours. Or a third thing, such as a coach who prescribes both more volume and better fuelling, might drive both. An observational study can't choose between these, however carefully it is done. Only assigning riders to different training can.

Spragg's lab study found correlations as high as 0.89 between riders' lab measures and the change in their critical power [16]. That looks close to proof. It came from 10 riders. With a sample that small, the true value could plausibly lie anywhere from about 0.6 to 0.97, by the standard calculation of a 95% confidence interval. For the weakest of the reported correlations, 0.74, the plausible range runs from about 0.2 to 0.93. The same arithmetic applies in the other direction to the glycogen result earlier in the chapter. A correlation of 0.19 from 14 riders is compatible with a true value anywhere between about −0.4 and 0.65, which covers everything from a moderate negative relationship to a strong positive one [8]. With 10 or 14 people, chance alone can produce a large correlation or hide a real one.

When a paper reports a correlation, look for the number of people before the size of r. Below about 30, treat a striking correlation as a reason to run a bigger study. Chapter 3 goes through the reasoning in full.

What to do with it

Start by finding out how much you fade, because the range between riders is so wide that the average tells you little about yourself.

The simplest test needs a power meter and two rides. On the first, after a warm-up, ride as hard as you can for a fixed time: Spragg's group used two, five and 12 minutes [19], and five or 12 suits most amateurs. A week or so later, do the same effort at the end of a ride of two and a half to three hours at your usual endurance pace, eating and drinking as you normally would. The percentage you lose is a rough measure of your durability. Keep the route, the fuelling and the temperature as similar as you can, and expect the result to move by a few per cent from one attempt to the next for no reason at all.

You can also watch drift without a hard effort. On a long ride at steady power, compare your average heart rate in the last half hour with the half hour after you warmed up. In the Finnish trial, untrained people drifted by 5% to 9% over three hours at an easy pace [20]; in the Auckland study, trained riders who had not eaten drifted 8.2% in two [6]. Heat and dehydration push the number up on their own, so compare cool day with cool day.

For Zone 2 training, the Auckland result has a direct consequence. If you ride at the very top of your zone by power, the top comes down to meet you, and after two hours you may be above it. If you hold a heart rate ceiling set from a fresh test, your power will sink further than it needs to, because the heart rate at the threshold rises as you tire [6]. Neither method is right late in a long ride. My own rule of thumb is to set power 10 to 15 W under the top of the zone for rides over two hours, and to eat.

Eat on anything longer than about 90 minutes, which is roughly where critical power began to slide in Jones's lab [5]. Sixty grams of carbohydrate an hour gets you most of the protection in the trials above. More helps a little further if your stomach accepts it, and that is worth practising in training before trying it in an event [12].

Then there is the long ride itself. Keep doing it. The mechanism is plausible, the comparisons of durable and less durable athletes point to accumulated volume, and nothing else on offer has better evidence apart from fuelling and, on one study, the weights room. But hold the belief at its proper strength. If the next few years bring a trial showing that an hour of intervals builds as much durability in trained riders as it did in the Finnish beginners, that should change your mind, and it will change this chapter.

Ice vests on the start line

The 2026 Tour de France began in Barcelona in temperatures above 30 °C and got hotter. Stage 3, from Granollers over the border to Les Angles, was raced in 36 °C while wildfires burned near Perpignan. Tadej Pogačar won it and called the day "a logistic nightmare", by which he meant the quantity of water and ice his team had to get to its riders. The next stage, out of Carcassonne, had up to 40 °C forecast. Riders that week waited for the start in frozen vests. Ineos had its riders stand with their forearms in iced water. Alpecin handed out carbohydrate gels that had been frozen solid. Ice socks, stockings packed with ice and pushed down the back of a jersey, are banned once the racing starts and were everywhere before and after it [1]. Four days later the organisers cut 30 km from stage 9, between Malemort and Ussel, after the Corrèze was put on a red heatwave alert [2].

Cooling a rider on the day is one answer to heat. The other is to send him to the race already adapted to it. In January 2025 Jeroen Swart, a sports scientist who has spent six years at UAE Team Emirates, described part of how the team had prepared Pogačar the previous season. They had been running training sessions at 35 °C and 90% humidity. Altitude training, he explained, thickens the blood by shrinking its liquid part, and heat does the opposite: plasma volume, he is quoted as saying, "can be increased by up to 25 per cent" [3].

The practice has spread well beyond one team. A group of Danish researchers who work with professional riders wrote in 2024 that heat training had become widespread among them, and that riders were using it to go faster in ordinary temperatures as well as to prepare for hot races [4]. The equipment has followed. A Swiss company sells a sensor the size of a matchbox that clips to a heart rate strap and shows an estimate of core body temperature on a bike computer, and its maker has announced partnerships with 10 professional teams [5].

Heat training is sold for two purposes, and only one of them is well supported.

The first is preparing for heat. If your event will be hot, training in the heat beforehand makes you faster on the day. Nobody disputes this. In one study, two weeks of training at about 35 °C improved cyclists' time-trial performance in the heat by 16% [6]. In another, 10 days improved it by 10% [7]. A Cooling on the day has good evidence too, and a later section covers the vests and the slushies.

The second is using heat the way riders use altitude: as a stimulus that makes you fitter everywhere, including on a cool day. That is the claim behind the sensors and the heat suits, and it is the subject of this chapter. It also makes a good test of the two questions this book keeps asking. What is the mechanism, and has the effect been shown or only inferred from it?

Where the idea came from

The modern interest dates from a single paper. In 2010 Santiago Lorenzo and Christopher Minson at the University of Oregon took 12 trained cyclists and had them ride for 10 days at an easy intensity, about half of VO₂max, in a room at 40 °C. Eight matched controls did the same riding at 13 °C [8].

When the heat group was tested afterwards in the cool room, their VO₂max had risen by 5%, from 66.8 to 70.2 mL/kg/min [8]. The work they could do in a time trial had risen by 6%, and their power at the lactate threshold by 5%. The control group had not changed. Plasma volume in the heat group was up 6.5%, and the heart's maximum output had risen with it.

For 10 days of easy riding in cyclists who were already trained, that is a very large effect, and people noticed.

Other labs tried to repeat it, and the first attempts did not go well. The two-week study mentioned earlier tested its cyclists in the cool as well as the heat. After two weeks their VO₂max had gone from 4.79 to 4.82 L/min, and their power in a 43 km outdoor time trial from 300 W to 302 W [6]. The 10-day study used a crossover, in which the same seven men did 10 days of training at 38 °C and, separately, 10 days at 18 °C. The heat block raised their VO₂max and time-trial power in a hot room by about 10% each. In a room at 18 °C, both were unchanged [7].

By 2016 the disagreement was formal. The Journal of Physiology ran one of its set-piece debates, with Minson and James Cotter arguing that heat acclimation does improve performance in the cool, and Lars Nybo and Carsten Lundby arguing that it does not [9,10]. Even the proposers hedged. Their summary describes the benefit as unresolved and possibly "modest or absent in some settings and individuals", and they pointed out that nobody had yet tested truly elite athletes or run the heat for longer than a couple of weeks.

Length turned out to matter, because the blood adapts to heat in two stages.

How it works

The first stage is fast. Within about a week of daily heat exposure, the liquid part of the blood expands. The studies in this chapter put the increase at 6% to 7% [8,7,11], which is a long way short of the 25% in Swart's interview, although he did say "up to". With more plasma, the heart fills more fully on each beat and has blood to spare for the skin. Sweating starts sooner and becomes more copious and more dilute: in one five-week study, riders' sweat rate rose by 0.44 litres an hour [4]. Core temperature at a given workload comes down. All of this is what makes a rider faster in the heat, and it is why the benefit there is so reliable.

For performance in the cool, the same change cuts both ways. Chapter 1 described VO₂max as mostly a matter of oxygen delivery, which is the amount of blood the heart can pump multiplied by the oxygen each litre carries. Extra plasma helps the first and hurts the second, because the same number of red cells is now spread through more liquid. The Copenhagen group suggested that the two roughly cancel [11]. That would explain why 10 days of heat does so much in a hot room and so little in a cool one.

The second stage is slow, and it is the reason for the current fashion. If heat exposure continues for several weeks, the total amount of haemoglobin in the body starts to rise. Haemoglobin is the protein in red cells that carries oxygen, and its total mass, measured in grams, sets how much oxygen the blood can carry in all. Raising it is the whole point of altitude training.

Why heat should do this is not established. The leading idea is that the kidney, which makes the hormone that drives red cell production, responds to how concentrated the blood is. Dilute the blood with extra plasma for long enough and the kidney reads it as a shortage of red cells and orders more [4]. It is a neat explanation, and the evidence for it is indirect. When the Copenhagen researchers measured the relevant hormones during five and a half weeks of heat training, they found no change in them [12]. The haemoglobin went up anyway.

So the mechanism predicts two things that can be checked. Short heat blocks should do little for cool-weather performance, which is what the 2015 studies found. And blocks of four or five weeks should raise haemoglobin mass, and performance with it. Since 2019, a series of trials has tested the second prediction.

What the five-week trials show

There are six of them, and their designs are similar enough to compare. Riders add about five hour-long sessions a week of easy riding in the heat, for five weeks, and are compared with a control group doing the same training in normal temperatures. The heat comes either from a chamber at 38 to 40 °C or from a heat suit worn while riding in an ordinary room.

On haemoglobin mass the trials agree. In 23 elite male cyclists it rose by 42 g, from 893 to 935 g, against 6 g in the controls [13]. In a second trial from the same lab it rose 2.6% with a heat suit and 2.4% in a chamber, while the controls lost 0.7% [14]. In 25 elite cross-country skiers it finished 30 g higher than in the controls [15]. In a study of 20 elite women and 27 elite men it rose 4.0% and 3.5% [16]. In 20 elite male cyclists in a separate trial it was up 3% after three weeks and 4% after five [4]. Every trial points the same way, and the size of the effect, 30 to 40 g or 3% to 4%, is about what an altitude camp delivers. One study made that comparison directly in seven riders who did both: 5.4% from five weeks of heat, 3.5% from three weeks at 3,000 metres [17]. B

On performance they do not agree.

The first of these trials found that VO₂max rose no more in the heat group than in the controls. Threshold power and 15-minute power both improved more with heat, by amounts the authors describe as small to intermediate, and neither difference was statistically significant [13]. The skiers showed no difference from controls in VO₂max, in economy or in a 15-minute performance test, despite their extra 30 g of haemoglobin [15]. The second cycling trial found better threshold and one-minute power in the heat-suit group but not in the chamber group; only when the two were combined, and three measures merged into one index, did heat clearly beat control, by 4.9% against 1.7% [14]. The study of women and men reports that threshold, VO₂max and 15-minute power all improved with heat [16]. The riders in the 2024 trial gained 12 W of peak power in a ramp test, with a change in VO₂max that fell short of significance [4].

Among these elite trials, two say yes, one says yes after pooling and two say no. A meta-analysis that gathered 28 heat studies of all lengths put the effect on cool-weather VO₂max, compared with control training, at 0.30 standard deviations, which is conventionally called small, with a confidence interval running from 0.06 to 0.54 [18]. My reading is that there is probably a real effect on performance in elite riders, that it is small, and that it is much less certain than the effect on haemoglobin. B

Three caveats belong with that.

Carsten Lundby, who argued the "no" side of the 2016 debate, is an author on every one of these five-week trials. It is to his credit that he tested a position he had argued against, and it means the evidence comes from one connected group of labs in Copenhagen and Lillehammer. Nobody outside that group has yet repeated it.

The riders were nearly all elite, and the men among them had VO₂max values in the mid-70s [13,16]. An elite rider has little room left to improve by ordinary training, so a 3% rise in haemoglobin is worth chasing. Whether the same applies to a rider with far more room is a separate question, and the one trial that tested it is described in the next section.

And the gain does not last. In the 2024 trial, haemoglobin mass was back to its starting value two weeks after the heat sessions stopped [4]. Rønnestad's group found that three sessions a week were enough to hold it [14]. Heat training is therefore something you keep doing or time carefully, like a taper.

One study, up close: the Copenhagen amateurs

In 2019 Nybo and Lundby's group published the first trial long enough to test the haemoglobin idea, and it remains the only one in riders who resemble the readers of this book [11,12].

They recruited 24 male cyclists with at least three years of riding behind them, an average age of 38 and an average VO₂max of about 58 mL/kg/min. These were good club riders [11]. Twenty-one finished: 12 randomly allocated to heat and nine to control. For five and a half weeks the heat group replaced part of their normal training with an hour at 60% of VO₂max in a chamber at 40 °C, five days a week. The control group did all their riding below 15 °C. Total training time was similar in the two groups, about eight and a half hours a week in the heat group and nine and a half in the controls.

These sessions were harder than "an hour at 60%" sounds. Rectal temperature at the end of every one was above 39 °C: 39.9 °C in the first session, falling to 39.4 °C by the last as the riders adapted [11]. The riders were also fully acclimated by every standard test. Their sweat became more dilute, their heart rate in the heat fell, and they lasted longer in a hot endurance test.

Before and after, everyone did a 15 km time trial at 14 °C. The heat group's average power went from 298 W to 315 W. The control group's went from 294 W to 311 W. Both improved by 17 W. VO₂max in the heat group was 60.0 before and 59.8 after [11].

Haemoglobin mass rose by 34 g in the heat group and 2 g in the controls. The difference between groups fell just short of statistical significance, at p = 0.061 [12]. That is a similar gain to the one the elite trials later found, and here it bought nothing.

The study has limits. Twenty-one riders is few, and a real performance difference of a few watts could be hidden in a group that size. The controls improved by 17 W over five weeks, which suggests the whole group was coming into form after winter, and a rising tide of that kind can swamp a small effect [11]. Only men took part. But the central result is hard to argue with for an amateur weighing this up. Riders much like you gave five of their eight and a half weekly hours to sitting in a 40 °C chamber, and were no faster than the riders who trained normally. C for the claim that heat training improves cool-weather performance in amateurs.

Reading the research: a marker is not a performance

Every trial in this chapter measured two kinds of thing. Haemoglobin mass is a marker: a quantity inside the body that is believed to lead to performance. Time-trial power is the performance itself. Fashions tend to be built on markers, because markers respond more consistently and can be measured more precisely than a tired rider's willingness to suffer in a time trial.

The heat trials show the gap clearly. The marker rose in all six, though in one the difference from the control group fell just short of significance. The skiers gained 30 g of haemoglobin and nothing measurable in performance [15]. The Copenhagen amateurs gained 34 g and matched their controls to the watt [11,12]. A headline that says "heat training increases haemoglobin mass" is accurate for both studies, and a reader would reasonably assume it means the athletes got faster.

When a study reports a marker, look for the performance measure in the same paper. If it is missing, the study has shown that a mechanism was engaged. If it is present and didn't move, that is the finding, whatever the title says.

A second habit is worth watching for in the same literature. In the trial where the heat-suit group improved and the chamber group did not, the positive result came from combining the two groups and merging three tests into a single index [14]. There can be good reasons to do that. But each choice of that kind, made after the data are in, raises the odds of finding something, and a result that appears only after pooling deserves less weight than one that was there in the first comparison. Chapter 3 returns to why analyses should be decided in advance.

The sensor

The device most riders mean is called CORE, made by a Swiss company, greenTEG. It doesn't measure core temperature, which can only be measured from inside the body. It measures the temperature of the skin on your chest and the rate at which heat is flowing out through it, optionally adds heart rate, and uses an algorithm to estimate what the temperature deep in the body must be [19].

I don't own one, so what follows is from the published tests and not from my own riding. I found three independent studies that compared it with a direct measurement.

One group tested it against a rectal probe during indoor cycling. It was repeatable: when 12 men did the same ride twice, the sensor's readings on the two days differed by an average of 0.02 °C. Against the probe it did less well. At 19 °C it read 0.23 °C high on average, and only 51% of its readings were within 0.3 °C of the true value. At 31 °C it read 0.10 °C low on average, with 45% of readings within 0.3 °C. Most individual readings fell somewhere between 0.6 °C below the probe and 0.7 °C above it [19]. The authors concluded that their results did not support the maker's claim of a valid measure.

A second group tried it on 19 international hockey players at a heat camp before the Tokyo Olympics, against a temperature pill the players had swallowed. Agreement was rated poor in all four sessions. The sensor read low, by up to 0.34 °C on average, and the error grew as the players got hotter. Between 41% and 60% of readings were more than 0.3 °C out [20]. A third study, of a 5 km run at 30 °C found an average error of 0.47 °C and a sensor that lagged behind the real rise [21].

A 2024 study of a different wearable, the Calera Research Sensor, found much closer agreement with a swallowed pill in cyclists riding at 32 °C: no average bias, and limits of about 0.36 °C either side [22]. So the approach can work better than the early tests suggested. I found no independent test of the newer, smaller CORE 2.

For heat training, an error of half a degree matters a good deal. The sessions in the Copenhagen trial ended with core temperatures between 39.4 and 39.9 °C [11]. Heat illness is usually associated with core temperatures above 40 °C [23]. The useful range is about one degree wide, and the sensor's error in the published tests is about half a degree in either direction, with a tendency in two of the three studies to read low when the body is hottest. A reading of 38.8 °C could mean you are not yet in the range, or that you are at the top of it.

It is also worth knowing that none of the trials in this chapter used the sensor. The Copenhagen group used rectal probes. Rønnestad's group prescribed time, a suit or a room temperature, and an easy intensity. The haemoglobin results came from riders who were simply made hot for 50 minutes, five times a week [14].

None of this makes the sensor useless. A repeatable number that is somewhat wrong can still show you a trend from one session to the next, if you treat it as your own scale and not as a temperature. But it is an optional extra for a practice whose benefits were demonstrated without it.

Cooling on the day

The ice on those start lines has more trial evidence behind it than most of this chapter. A 2015 meta-analysis pooled 28 trials of cooling, all in men exercising at above 30 °C. Cooling before the effort improved performance by an average of 5.7%, and cooling during it by 9.9%; the difference between those two was not statistically significant. During exercise, ice vests were the most effective method. Beforehand, combining several methods worked best [24]. A

The slushy has studies of its own. Ice absorbs a great deal of heat as it melts, so a drink that is partly ice takes more heat out of the body than the same drink served cold. In one study, 10 men drank either an ice slurry or cold water before running to exhaustion at 34 °C. The slurry lowered their rectal temperature by 0.66 °C before they started, against 0.25 °C for the water, and they ran for 50 minutes instead of 41 [25]. In 10 cyclists, taking a sports drink as a slushy through 90 minutes of riding at 32 °C brought the time for a time trial straight afterwards down from 20:24 to 18:28 [26].

That cycling study had a third arm, in which the riders swilled the slushy round their mouths and spat it out. They finished in 19:45, between the other two. Some of the benefit of cold is in the sensation, with nothing swallowed. The meta-analysis points the same way: across its 28 trials, how far a method lowered core temperature did not predict how much it improved performance [24]. Feeling cooler lets you work harder. In the running study the men who had drunk the slurry stopped at a higher rectal temperature than they reached after cold water, 39.4 °C against 39.1 °C [25]. The ice had bought them room to go further into the heat, and they used it.

Cooling and acclimation have been compared directly. Thirteen triathletes did a 20 km time trial at 35 °C before and after an eight-day training camp in the heat, each time with and without an ice vest beforehand [27]. Before the camp the vest was worth 10 W. The camp was worth 28 W. After the camp the vest added 4 W, an effect the authors called unclear, although the riders still felt more comfortable with it. So a rider who has adapted gets less from the ice, and a rider who hasn't had the chance to adapt gets the most. For an amateur flying to a hot sportive from a British spring, that second case is the usual one.

What to do with it

If your target event will be hot, heat training is worth doing, and the case is as strong as any in this book. One to two weeks of regular riding in the heat before the event is what the 2015 studies used, with gains of 10% to 16% in hot conditions [6,7]. The sweating and cardiovascular adaptations were largely complete within three weeks in the 2024 trial, and they were gone two weeks after stopping [4], so the block needs to run up close to the event. On the day itself, cool down before the start and keep the coldest, iciest drink you can carry.

If the aim is to be faster in ordinary temperatures, my advice for most readers is not to bother. The dose that raises haemoglobin is about five hours a week for five weeks. For a professional training 25 hours a week, five easy hours in a heat suit are a small part of the programme. For a rider with eight hours, they are most of it, and they are hours at low power in conditions that make quality work impossible. The one trial in riders at that level found no advantage [11]. The time is better spent on the things the earlier chapters covered.

From the saddle. George: the 2026 heatwave, when your training room reached 37 °C. How long did it last, did you keep riding through it, and did you notice anything in the weeks afterwards? That was accidental heat training, and whatever you saw, or didn't, belongs here.

If you want to try it anyway, perhaps because you are short of ways to add load without adding hours on the road, the trials give a template. Ride indoors at a genuinely easy intensity for 50 to 60 minutes, with no fan and enough clothing to make you sweat heavily, four or five times a week for five weeks, and then two or three times a week to hold what you have gained [14]. Expect your power at a given heart rate to be well down in these sessions. Keep your hard sessions cool, as the riders in the trials did.

Be careful. The sessions in the trials were supervised, the riders were young and screened, and their core temperatures were being measured properly. Riding to a core temperature above 39 °C alone in a spare room is a different undertaking. Drink, stop if you feel dizzy, confused or chilled, and don't do it at all without medical advice if you have a heart condition or take medication that affects sweating or blood pressure.

Heat training for cool-weather speed is a fair example of how a fashion forms. It began with one striking study that later, shorter studies could not repeat. It was rescued by a better mechanism and longer trials, which showed that the mechanism is real. What those trials have not yet shown is that an amateur who engages the mechanism gets faster, and the single time anyone checked, they did not.

Ninety-six finishers

The 1998 Tour de France started in Dublin on 11 July with 189 riders. Three days earlier a car belonging to the Festina team had been stopped at the French-Belgian border carrying EPO, steroids and other drugs. The team was expelled after the sixth stage. Six more teams left the race before the end, and 96 riders reached Paris [1,2].

Fourteen years later the United States Anti-Doping Agency published its decision on the rider who won the next seven Tours. It described a team-run doping programme, banned Lance Armstrong for life and struck out his results [3]. He admitted it the following January. No winner is listed for those seven races.

This chapter is about that era: what the riders were trying to do to their bodies, what it was worth, what it risked, and what it has to teach anyone who wants to judge a training claim. It ends in the present, so I should say where I stand. I assume today's riders are clean. Nothing here is meant to suggest otherwise about anyone, and nobody is connected with doping in this chapter unless an official body has found it or the rider has admitted it. What I do think is that professional teams look for every advantage the rules allow, and that the rules keep having to catch up with them. The last section is about that.

What they were trying to achieve

The commission that investigated cycling's doping history in 2015 summarised decades of it in one sentence. The aims, it wrote, had stayed the same throughout: to raise the blood's capacity to carry oxygen, and to build muscle and speed recovery [4]. The drugs changed as new ones reached the market and as tests caught up with old ones, while those two targets stayed fixed.

The second aim covers testosterone, growth hormone and the corticosteroids, which riders told the commission were used both to dull pain and to strip weight. The first is the one that defined the era, and it maps directly onto the model in Chapter 1. Among well-trained riders, VO₂max is limited mainly by how much oxygen the heart and blood can deliver to the muscles. Training raises delivery slowly, over years, and by amounts that shrink as a rider approaches their ceiling. A drug that raises it by a tenth in a month is acting on the one link in the chain that an elite athlete can no longer improve much by working harder.

There were two ways to do it. One was to transfuse blood: a rider had a bag or two of their own blood taken out and stored, waited some weeks for the body to replace it, and had the red cells put back before a race. The other, from about 1990, was a drug that made the body produce the extra red cells itself.

How it works: the biology of EPO

Erythropoietin, EPO for short, is a hormone that everyone makes. Its job is to keep the number of red blood cells matched to the body's need for oxygen.

It is made mainly in the kidneys, by cells in the outer layer called the cortex [5]. Those cells contain a sensor for oxygen. The sensor is a protein, hypoxia-inducible factor or HIF, which the cell manufactures continuously and, as long as oxygen is plentiful, destroys almost as fast as it makes it. The enzymes that mark HIF for destruction need oxygen to work. When the oxygen reaching the kidney falls, because you have lost blood, or moved to altitude, or become anaemic, those enzymes slow down, HIF survives, and it switches on the gene for EPO.

EPO then travels in the blood to the bone marrow, where red cells are made. The marrow constantly produces red cell precursors, many of which die before maturing. EPO is the signal that lets them live. More EPO means more precursors survive, multiply and mature into red cells, each packed with haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen [5]. As the red cell count rises, more oxygen reaches the kidney, HIF is destroyed again, and EPO production falls back. It is a thermostat for the blood.

Two things you have met elsewhere in this book work through the same thermostat. Altitude training lowers the oxygen in the blood, so the kidney makes more EPO for as long as you stay high. Heat training, on one theory, dilutes the blood until the kidney reads it as short of red cells. Both are slow and both stop when the stimulus stops.

People whose kidneys fail lose the ability to make EPO and become severely anaemic. In the 1980s the human gene was inserted into cultured cells to manufacture the hormone, and recombinant EPO was approved in the United States as a treatment for the anaemia of kidney failure in June 1989 [6]. For those patients it replaced regular blood transfusions, and it remains one of the most useful drugs biotechnology has produced.

Injected into a healthy person, the same drug overrides the thermostat. The marrow receives a signal to make red cells that the body doesn't need. What happens next was measured in 2007 by Carsten Lundby, who appears in the heat training chapter, in eight healthy volunteers given EPO for 15 weeks [7]. Their red cell volume rose from 2,933 mL to about 3,200 mL. Their plasma volume fell, from 3,645 mL to about 3,270 mL. Total blood volume barely changed. The body, in other words, made room for the new red cells by removing liquid, and between 38% and 54% of the rise in the blood's oxygen content came from that concentration, not from the extra cells.

That finding explains two features of the era. It is the reverse of what heat does, which is why a team scientist in the heat training chapter describes altitude and heat as opposites. And it is why the measure everyone talked about was haematocrit, the percentage of the blood's volume taken up by red cells. Riders told the 2015 commission that their natural levels were in the low to middle 40s [4]. EPO pushed the number up from both directions at once, by adding cells and by taking away plasma, and blood with that many cells in it is thicker and harder to pump.

How much it was worth

In the laboratory the effect is large. In a second study from the same group, eight healthy non-athletes given EPO raised their VO₂max by 12.6% in four weeks, and the time they could ride at 80% of it rose by 54% [8]. A 2025 review of 10 studies in well-trained athletes found the same direction in all of them: haemoglobin up, VO₂max up, peak power usually up [9]. A for the claim that EPO raises VO₂max.

Transfusion works too, and it takes very little blood. In a double-blind study, highly trained men who were given back about 135 mL of their own red cells, roughly half of what one donated bag contains, improved their power in a time trial from 213 W to 223 W, about 5%. After a sham transfusion nothing changed [10].

What these numbers meant in a professional race is harder to establish, because nobody can run a trial in the Tour. The commission's report says it "was told" that gains had been 10% to 15% in the years of unrestricted use, and perhaps 3% to 5% from the small doses riders moved to once blood values were being tracked [4]. Those figures are testimony from people inside the sport. They aren't measurements, and the report doesn't say who offered them.

Race speeds give indirect support. One analysis took the fifth-placed finisher in each Tour, Giro and Vuelta from 1990 to 2009, to avoid depending on any one champion. Average speed rose by 0.16 km/h a year until 2004 and then fell by 0.22 km/h a year [11]. Its author read the turn as compatible with anti-doping efforts taking hold. But a second analysis, of the winning times of 181 Grand Tours going back to 1947, found no sharp break in the 1990s and concluded that the effect of EPO on results may have been overestimated [12]. Speed depends on the route, the weather, the tactics and the tarmac as well as the riders, and two careful people looking at similar data reached different conclusions.

One randomised trial has tested EPO on the road.

One study, up close: EPO up Mont Ventoux

In the spring of 2016 a clinical research unit in Leiden recruited 48 male amateur cyclists aged 18 to 50 [13]. To qualify they had to produce more than 4 W/kg in a ramp test and not hold a licence that made them subject to anti-doping rules. They trained five to six hours a week and had an average VO₂max of 55.6 mL/kg/min. Half were randomly assigned to a weekly injection of EPO for eight weeks and half to an injection of salt water that looked identical. Neither the riders nor the staff testing them knew who was getting which.

The dose was adjusted rider by rider to raise haemoglobin by 10% to 15%, and held back for a week if haematocrit reached 52%. It averaged 6,000 units a week, which the authors describe as consistent with known practice in professional cycling. Haemoglobin rose by 12% and the EPO group's haematocrit reached an average of 50%, the old racing limit [13].

In the laboratory the drug did what it was expected to. Compared with placebo, VO₂max was about 5% higher and peak power in a ramp test about 4% higher, 352 W against 341 W. The riders also did a 45-minute test at the highest power they could hold, designed to resemble a time trial. The EPO group averaged 283 W and the placebo group 277 W, and that difference was not statistically significant [13].

Then, on 19 June 2016, about 12 days after the last injection and with haemoglobin still raised, the riders rode 110 km through Provence as a group and raced the 21.5 km from Bédoin to the summit of Mont Ventoux. Forty-four started and 40 finished. The EPO group's average time was 1 hour 40 minutes 32 seconds and the placebo group's 1 hour 40 minutes 15 seconds. Average power on the climb was 3.03 W/kg against 3.09 [13].

Afterwards the riders were asked which treatment they thought they had been given. Of the 23 who had completed the course of EPO, nine thought so. Six of the 24 on placebo thought they had been on the drug [13].

The authors concluded that EPO improved a maximal laboratory test and did not improve the submaximal test or the road race. That is easily shortened to "EPO doesn't work", and the trial does not show that.

Reading the research: when a trial finds nothing

A result of "no difference" can mean that there is no effect, or that the study could not have seen one. The way to tell is the confidence interval, the range of true effects the data are compatible with.

In their planning, the authors worked out the size of effect that would matter: a gain in sustained power of a little over 3%, which they estimated was worth about two minutes on a climb like Ventoux [13]. Now look at what the race could detect. The difference in time between the groups was 0.3%, with a 95% confidence interval from 8.3% faster on EPO to 9.6% slower. On a 100-minute climb that is a range of roughly eight minutes either way. A two-minute effect is well inside it. So is a five-minute one.

The paper itself explains why the race was so imprecise. The wind at the summit that afternoon was blowing at 85 km/h and the temperature there was 5 °C. Four riders gave up from exhaustion. Each rider did the climb once, at the end of a long day [13].

The 45-minute laboratory test was far better controlled, and it is the stronger evidence. Its result was a difference of 5.9 W in favour of EPO, with an interval from 0.9 W the wrong way to 12.7 W the right way. As a percentage, that runs from about zero to about 4.5%. The best estimate is a gain of 2%. The commission was told that micro-dosing was worth 3% to 5%. The trial's own laboratory time trial does not rule that out [13,4].

Who was studied matters too. These riders trained five to six hours a week, which the authors acknowledge is far below a professional's load. For an amateur, a 2% change in sustained power is lost among everything else that varies from one rider to the next on the day. For a professional racing against riders who have all spent years removing those other differences, the same 2% is a decisive margin.

What the trial does show is narrower and still useful. A recreational rider who took a professional's dose of EPO for two months came out with a measurably higher VO₂max, a gain in sustained power too small to distinguish from nothing, no advantage on a mountain, and in most cases no idea he had been on it. Keep that in mind for the passage on amateur racing below. B for the claim that EPO improves sustained performance in the laboratory; C, resting on testimony and race speeds, for its size on the road in professionals.

The risks

The risk everybody knows about is that EPO thickened riders' blood until their hearts stopped in the night. The story usually comes with a number: 18 Dutch and Belgian cyclists dead between 1987 and 1990.

The 2015 commission repeats it, and its wording is careful. "The media speculated", it says, that the deaths of 18 European cyclists were related to EPO [4].

In 2011 a Spanish academic, Bernat López, went looking for the evidence behind the number [14]. He collected 61 texts that mention the deaths, 36 of them academic. Only two cited anything resembling an original source, and both of those were newspaper articles. The rest cited one another, or nobody. The details changed from text to text: the number of dead ran from "half a dozen" to "around 40", the nationalities from Dutch and Belgian to Scandinavian, and the dates across two decades. One paper put the deaths between 1997 and 2000 [14].

López then searched newspaper archives for every sudden death of a competitive cyclist he could find. Between 1987 and 1992 there were 17, nine of them Dutch and six Belgian, which is about one a year in each country. Several had retired before EPO was available, some died in the off-season, and seven were amateurs with little access to an expensive new drug. He was left with two cases that could even plausibly involve it. In one of the best-known cases, a Dutch professional who died in his sleep in 1990, the rider's widow said the post-mortem had found nothing linking his death to the drug. Studies of sudden death in young athletes, which López also reviewed, attribute about nine in 10 to underlying heart conditions [14].

His paper has an argument to make. He calls the story anti-doping propaganda, and he concedes that his own count is not watertight and that nothing he found proves EPO played no part. But the central finding stands on its own: a claim repeated in dozens of scholarly works had no traceable evidence behind it.

That doesn't make the drug safe. In one large trial that López cites, dialysis patients with heart disease whose haematocrit was pushed up to 42% with EPO had more heart attacks than those held at 30% [14]. Those were sick people, and the finding can't simply be transferred to athletes, but it shows the concern about thick blood was not invented. Riders described to the commission how they got up in the night to turn the pedals on a stationary bike and keep their blood moving [4]. Whether that fear was justified by the physiology or by the rumour is hard to know now. What the episode shows is how a plausible mechanism, repeated often enough, comes to be treated as an observed fact, and that is the thing this book keeps warning about.

The sport's own response is instructive. In 1997 the governing body brought in a rule that any rider with a haematocrit above 50% would be stood down for 15 days. It was presented as a health check, because there was then no test for EPO itself. The commission thought it probably prevented deaths [4]. It was also told what the rule did to behaviour. A rider whose natural level was 43% now had seven points of headroom, and riders described carrying centrifuges to stay inside it. One former rider said the rule "was perceived by riders as legalising EPO up to a certain limit" [4]. Its best-known application came in June 1999, when Marco Pantani, the winner of that 1998 Tour, was removed from a Giro d'Italia he was leading by more than five minutes after a reading of 52% [15]. Because the rule was a health measure, that was a two-week stand-down and not a doping sanction.

In the Leiden trial, with weekly blood checks and the dose held back whenever haematocrit reached 52%, there was no difference in adverse events between the groups and no sign of clotting. Two blood markers involved in clot formation did rise in the EPO group, and the authors point out that a study of 48 people could not have detected a rare event such as a stroke [13]. That is the fairest summary of the medical risk I can give: small when a doctor is controlling the dose, unknown when a rider is doing it alone with a centrifuge, and, by my reasoning, greater again with transfusions, where blood has to be drawn, stored, transported and re-infused away from any hospital.

The costs that are fully documented are of a different kind. Careers ended in bans and some in court. Seven Tours have no winner. And for every rider caught there were others who didn't dope and finished where nobody remembers, in a generation of results that can't be read.

What the era teaches

The first lesson is that tests catch a small fraction of doping. A review of prevalence studies across elite sport estimated that between 14% and 39% of athletes had doped intentionally, during years in which 1% to 2% of tests came back positive [16]. The German blood-doping ring was broken in 2019 by police acting on a skier's confession, and the cyclists who then admitted using it had not been caught by a test [17]. A clean test record, on its own, shows little.

The second is that the best tool the sport has can be beaten too, and that it helped anyway. Since January 2008 professional riders have had a biological passport, a running record of their blood values against which each new sample is compared; in its first two years the programme covered more than 850 riders and almost 20,000 samples [18]. The idea is to catch the effect of doping on the blood when the drug itself has gone. In 2011 researchers gave 10 volunteers small, frequent doses of EPO for up to 12 weeks. Their haemoglobin mass rose by 10%, about the equivalent of two bags of blood, and the passport software flagged none of them [19]. The 2015 commission named micro-dosing as its biggest concern for that reason. It also made the point that matters more for the sport: because riders had to use far less to stay inside their own profile, the gain shrank, and a clean rider could win again [4].

The third is the one the 50% rule teaches. A limit tells people where the limit is. Any threshold, however well meant, becomes a target for those willing to ride up to it. That applies to the rules discussed at the end of this chapter as much as to haematocrit.

The fourth is about evidence, and it cuts both ways. A performance can't convict a rider. Climbing times depend on the wind, the pace into the foot of the climb, the tactics and the road surface, and the review of prevalence studies concluded that performance data cannot be used to estimate how much doping there is [16]. By the same reasoning, a performance can't acquit one either, and nor can a clean test record. The commission asked people inside the sport how much doping remained. One "respected cycling professional" said 90% of the peloton. Another said 20%. Many said they didn't know [4]. That spread, from people far better placed than any fan, is the true state of knowledge.

The last lesson concerns the readers of this book more than the professionals. The commission wrote that doping in amateur cycling was "becoming endemic", and that masters races were "said to have middle-aged businessmen winning on EPO" [4]. It blamed cheap drugs, easy access through gyms and the internet, and the near absence of testing at that level. Set that beside the Leiden trial, in which amateurs took EPO for two months and gained nothing measurable on a mountain. A man risking his health and a ban for an age-group result may be buying mostly a number on a lab report.

Getting faster without it

People who follow the sport ask how today's riders can be as fast as they are without drugs. It is a fair question, and it has better answers than it did 20 years ago. The useful way to approach it is to take each legal explanation in turn and ask whether it has evidence behind it and how large an effect it could plausibly have. Several are in this book.

Fuelling is the best supported. The research on carbohydrate intakes above 90 grams an hour is recent, and I have not found a reliable figure for what riders ate during races in the 1990s. In the trial described in the Durability chapter, trained cyclists who drank water for three hours finished with a critical power of 236 W, and the same riders on 120 grams of carbohydrate an hour finished with 266 W [20]. That is a difference of about 13% in what they could do at the end of a long ride. The gain claimed for unrestricted EPO was 10% to 15%. Nobody in the 1990s raced on water alone, so the real difference between then and now is smaller than 13%. But a climb at the end of a long mountain stage is where late-ride power decides the time, and the size of the fuelling effect is of the same order as the drug's.

Haemoglobin can be raised legally. Three weeks at 3,000 metres raised it by 3.5% in elite cyclists, and five weeks of heat training by about the same or more [21]. Those are smaller than the 10% a course of EPO micro-doses produced in the Australian study, they fade within a fortnight, and they have to be timed. They are also available to every team.

Equipment I can't put a number on from the sources I have read for this book, and I would rather say so than quote a manufacturer. Tyres roll more easily and frames and clothing move less air than they did, and on any climb ridden at over 20 km/h some of the rider's power still goes into the air. Training has changed too, in the ways the rest of this book describes.

Added together, these make a clean explanation of modern speeds plausible. They can't make it proven, for the reason given in the last section: nothing can. The fuelling data are what persuade me most. A legal change in what riders eat has an effect of the same size as the drug that defined an era.

The edge of the rules

The line between clean and doped is drawn by a list, and the list moves.

Tramadol is an opioid painkiller. For years it was legal, and the 2015 commission was told it was "used widely" in the peloton for that reason [4]. In one trial, cyclists produced 220 W in a 20-minute time trial on tramadol against 209 W on a placebo, although a second experiment in the same paper found no difference [22]. Cycling banned it in competition from March 2019 and the World Anti-Doping Agency followed in 2024. A rider who took it in 2018 broke no rule. The same act six years later is a doping offence.

Carbon monoxide is a more recent case. Inhaling a small, measured amount is the standard way to measure haemoglobin mass, and it is how the heat training studies in the previous chapter got their numbers. In 2024 it was reported that professional teams owned the equipment, which they said they used for measurement. Used once, it is a measuring tool. Used repeatedly, in theory, it could starve the body of a little oxygen and prompt the kidney to make more EPO, which would be altitude training from a cylinder. In February 2025 the sport's governing body banned possession of the equipment outside a medical facility, allowed a single supervised measurement with a second no sooner than two weeks later, gave rider health as its reason, and asked the World Anti-Doping Agency to rule on it [23]. I know of no evidence that any rider used it for anything except measurement. The rule was written because the possibility existed.

Ketone drinks are legal. In October 2025 the governing body said it could find "no compelling evidence" that they help and recommended that riders not take them, without banning them [24].

These three sit in the same place the 50% rule once did. Each was permitted, each was adopted by people whose job is to find advantages, and each was then questioned. That is all I mean by teams looking for every advantage, and it is not an accusation. Each of these was within the rules when it was used, and finding legal gains is what a professional team is for. The discomfort is that "legal" describes the rulebook on a given date, and the 50% rule showed how easily a limit is taken for permission.

For anyone riding below that level the conclusion is simpler. The gains this chapter has described as certain are the legal ones: eating enough on the bike, training consistently for years, and for those with the time, altitude or heat. The one trial of the era's signature drug in riders like us found nothing on the road. Whatever is true of the professionals, there is nothing in that pharmacy for an amateur.
