## chapters/doping/draft.md

#### One study, up close: EPO up Mont Ventoux

In the spring of 2016 a clinical research unit in Leiden recruited 48 male amateur cyclists aged 18 to 50 [@heuberger2017]. To qualify they had to produce more than 4 W/kg in a ramp test and not hold a licence that made them subject to anti-doping rules. They trained five to six hours a week and had an average VO₂max of 55.6 mL/kg/min. Half were randomly assigned to a weekly injection of EPO for eight weeks and half to an injection of salt water that looked identical. Neither the riders nor the staff testing them knew who was getting which.

The dose was adjusted rider by rider to raise haemoglobin by 10% to 15%, and held back for a week if haematocrit reached 52%. It averaged 6,000 units a week, which the authors describe as consistent with known practice in professional cycling. Haemoglobin rose by 12% and the EPO group's haematocrit reached an average of 50%, the old racing limit [@heuberger2017].

In the laboratory the drug did what it was expected to. Compared with placebo, VO₂max was about 5% higher and peak power in a ramp test about 4% higher, 352 W against 341 W. The riders also did a 45-minute test at the highest power they could hold, designed to resemble a time trial. The EPO group averaged 283 W and the placebo group 277 W, and that difference was not statistically significant [@heuberger2017].

Then, on 19 June 2016, about 12 days after the last injection and with haemoglobin still raised, the riders rode 110 km through Provence as a group and raced the 21.5 km from Bédoin to the summit of Mont Ventoux. Forty-four started and 40 finished. The EPO group's average time was 1 hour 40 minutes 32 seconds and the placebo group's 1 hour 40 minutes 15 seconds. Average power on the climb was 3.03 W/kg against 3.09 [@heuberger2017].

Afterwards the riders were asked which treatment they thought they had been given. Of the 23 who had completed the course of EPO, nine thought so. Six of the 24 on placebo thought they had been on the drug [@heuberger2017].

The authors concluded that EPO improved a maximal laboratory test and did not improve the submaximal test or the road race. That is easily shortened to "EPO doesn't work", and the trial does not show that.


## chapters/heat-training/draft.md

#### One study, up close: the Copenhagen amateurs

In 2019 Nybo and Lundby's group published the first trial long enough to test the haemoglobin idea, and it remains the only one in riders who resemble the readers of this book [@mikkelsen2019; @oberholzer2019].

They recruited 24 male cyclists with at least three years of riding behind them, an average age of 38 and an average VO₂max of about 58 mL/kg/min. These were good club riders [@mikkelsen2019]. Twenty-one finished: 12 randomly allocated to heat and nine to control. For five and a half weeks the heat group replaced part of their normal training with an hour at 60% of VO₂max in a chamber at 40 °C, five days a week. The control group did all their riding below 15 °C. Total training time was similar in the two groups, about eight and a half hours a week in the heat group and nine and a half in the controls.

These sessions were harder than "an hour at 60%" sounds. Rectal temperature at the end of every one was above 39 °C: 39.9 °C in the first session, falling to 39.4 °C by the last as the riders adapted [@mikkelsen2019]. The riders were also fully acclimated by every standard test. Their sweat became more dilute, their heart rate in the heat fell, and they lasted longer in a hot endurance test.

Before and after, everyone did a 15 km time trial at 14 °C. The heat group's average power went from 298 W to 315 W. The control group's went from 294 W to 311 W. Both improved by 17 W. VO₂max in the heat group was 60.0 before and 59.8 after [@mikkelsen2019].

Haemoglobin mass rose by 34 g in the heat group and 2 g in the controls. The difference between groups fell just short of statistical significance, at p = 0.061 [@oberholzer2019]. That is a similar gain to the one the elite trials later found, and here it bought nothing.

The study has limits. Twenty-one riders is few, and a real performance difference of a few watts could be hidden in a group that size. The controls improved by 17 W over five weeks, which suggests the whole group was coming into form after winter, and a rising tide of that kind can swamp a small effect [@mikkelsen2019]. Only men took part. But the central result is hard to argue with for an amateur weighing this up. Riders much like you gave five of their eight and a half weekly hours to sitting in a 40 °C chamber, and were no faster than the riders who trained normally. {grade C} for the claim that heat training improves cool-weather performance in amateurs.


## chapters/elites/draft.md

#### One study, up close: four ways to train for nine weeks

The largest of the early trials was run by Stöggl and Sperlich in Salzburg and published in 2014 [@stoggl2014]. They recruited 48 competitive runners, cyclists, triathletes and cross-country skiers, among them members of the Austrian cross-country ski team. The athletes were 31 years old on average, with a VO₂peak of 62.6 mL/kg/min, and were used to training 10 to 20 hours a week. Each was randomly assigned to one of four programmes. The trial ran for nine weeks, and every session was controlled by heart rate.

The high-volume programme was six sessions a week: five easy ones, two of them lasting two and a half to four hours, and one of intervals at the lactate threshold. The threshold programme had three threshold sessions a week, one session of varied pace and two easy ones. The interval programme packed 12 sessions of four four-minute efforts at 90 to 95% of peak heart rate into each of two 15-day blocks. The polarised programme had two of those interval sessions a week, two long easy sessions of two and a half to four hours with a few five-second sprints, and two easy sessions of 90 minutes [@stoggl2014].

Forty-one athletes finished. VO₂peak rose by 11.7% in the polarised group, from 60.6 to 67.4 mL/kg/min, and by 4.8% in the interval group. It did not change significantly in the other two. Power or speed at a blood lactate of 4 mmol/L rose by 8.1%, 5.6%, 1.4% and 1.2% in the polarised, interval, threshold and high-volume groups. Time to exhaustion in a ramp test rose by 17.4%, 8.8%, 6.2% and 8.0%, and on that measure the groups did not differ significantly. The interval group lost 3.7% of its body weight [@stoggl2014].

The paper's title says that polarised training has a greater impact on endurance than the three alternatives. The full text supports something narrower.

The distributions are counts of sessions. The polarised group is described as 68 / 6 / 26, which means that 14 of its 54 sessions were interval sessions. Each held 16 minutes of hard effort. By my arithmetic from the paper's table, that is under four hours of intervals in 104 hours of training, less than 4% of the time [@stoggl2014].

The groups did not train for the same number of hours. The polarised group trained for 104 hours, the high-volume group for 102, the threshold group for 84 and the interval group for 66 [@stoggl2014]. Only the comparison with the high-volume group is free of that difference.

That comparison has a problem of its own. Before the trial every athlete had been training the high-volume way, with at most two threshold sessions a week, and none had been doing intervals regularly. The high-volume group therefore carried on as before, and the authors say that this may be why it did not improve. The polarised group kept its volume and was given something new. What the trial shows most securely is that adding two interval sessions a week to a high-volume programme raised VO₂peak in athletes who had not been doing intervals. It does not show that a ratio was responsible.

Nothing was raced. Every outcome was a laboratory test, and the authors list the absence of a time trial as a limitation.

Seven athletes dropped out, four of them from the threshold group, and among those who stayed in that group the change in VO₂peak ran from a gain of 4% to a loss of 20% [@stoggl2014]. A fifth of a trained athlete's VO₂peak does not disappear in nine weeks of training three threshold sessions a week. Illness or a bad test is a likelier explanation, and in a group that had lost four of its members one such result moves the average a long way.

The headline number is also very large. The authors themselves wrote that an 11% rise in nine weeks in athletes of this standard "has to be put in perspective" [@stoggl2014], and Chapter 4 explained why the largest results from the smallest groups are the ones most likely to shrink when the experiment is repeated.
