# Introduction: Why ask the question

In the spring of 2026 I rode up Sa Calobra faster than I ever had. The climb, on the north coast of Mallorca, is nine and a half kilometres at about seven per cent, with twenty-six hairpins and nowhere to recover. I took two minutes and eleven seconds off my best time.

I knew why, or thought I did. I had spent the winter in my garage on a smart trainer, four or five times a week, riding for 45 minutes to an hour at a heart rate of about 142 beats per minute. It was the dullest training I had ever done. Nothing hurt, nothing was worth posting, and my ride history turned into a column of identical flat grey lines. Then I went to Mallorca, rode the effort I had learned by heart all winter, and found there was a lot more power under it than before.

That winter was Zone 2 training, and I wrote about it with the enthusiasm of a convert. I told readers of my website that easy riding was the single most effective way to build mitochondria, the structures in muscle cells that use oxygen to make energy. I believed it. I had heard it from people who sounded as if they knew.

A few months later I sat down to read the research properly, and that sentence didn't survive. The largest summary of the evidence, a 2025 review of 353 training studies, found that mitochondrial content rose by similar amounts with steady training (about 23%) and with intervals, whether high-intensity efforts or all-out sprints (27%). Per hour of training, the sprints did almost four times as much {shadow @molmen2025}. In 2025 four Canadian researchers made the same point at length, in a review they called "Much Ado About Zone 2" [@storoschuk2025]. Zone 2 had done something real for me. It just hadn't done it for the reason I had been told, or not only for that reason.

This book grew out of that gap: between what people confidently say about endurance training and what the studies actually show.

## A short history of training fashions

Endurance training goes through fashions, and they tend to follow the same pattern. Someone finds something real in a study. It gets explained with a mechanism that sounds convincing. Then it spreads faster than the evidence behind it, until the claims have little to do with what was found.

In 1996 a Japanese research group led by Izumi Tabata had seven people do a few minutes of very hard 20-second efforts on a bike, with 10 seconds of rest between each, five days a week for six weeks. Both their aerobic fitness and their capacity for short, hard efforts improved {shadow @tabata1996}. It was a small study of a punishing protocol. Within a few years "Tabata" had become a name for almost any four-minute workout in a gym class, most of them nothing like what the study's subjects did.

Ten years later Martin Gibala's lab in Canada compared two small groups of active young men over two weeks. One group did short sessions of 30-second all-out sprints. The other did long steady rides, about four times as much training in total. Both groups improved by similar amounts {shadow @gibala2006}. It was a careful study with a striking result, and it launched a decade of articles promising the benefits of endurance training in a fraction of the time. Fewer of those articles mentioned that the study lasted two weeks, involved 16 people, and measured early changes in muscle, not what happens over a season.

Around the same time, the Norway-based physiologist Stephen Seiler was describing how the world's best endurance athletes actually trained. Across sports, he found, they did roughly four easy sessions for every hard one, with very little in between [@seiler2010]. That observation became "80/20" and "polarised training", and for a while every training plan had to be polarised.

Then came Zone 2. Its rise owed a lot to podcasts, in particular a 2019 conversation between the physician Peter Attia and the physiologist Iñigo San-Millán, who worked with professional cycling teams [@attia2019]. San-Millán's research on how well athletes and patients burn fat and clear lactate was real and interesting. Repeated across thousands of episodes and social media posts, it grew into something larger: the idea of a training intensity with special powers over mitochondria, metabolism and even lifespan.

None of these fashions was simply wrong. High-intensity intervals do work, elite athletes do mostly train easy, and easy riding does improve aerobic fitness. What each fashion got wrong was its confidence, and its silence about who was studied, for how long, and what was actually measured.

## What this book tries to do

For each training claim, I want you to understand the biology underneath it: what happens in a muscle, a heart or a bloodstream when you ride easy or ride hard, and why that might make you faster. A plausible mechanism is far easier to find than evidence that the training actually improves performance. "Easy riding burns the most fat, so it must build the most mitochondria" is a mechanism argument. The training studies didn't bear it out.

I also want you to be able to judge research yourself. The *Reading the research* sections use individual studies to practise skills such as telling correlation from cause, spotting a sample too small to trust, or noticing when "a trend" means "not significant". You should be able to apply those skills to the next confident training claim you hear.

To keep the book honest about its own claims, the important ones carry a grade:

- **A**: several randomised trials, or a well-conducted meta-analysis, pointing the same way.
- **B**: consistent findings, but from small or short studies, a single lab, or one good trial.
- **C**: observational data, mechanism, case studies or expert opinion.

A grade C claim isn't necessarily wrong. Most of what coaches know started as grade C, and some of it will become grade A. But you should know which is which, and so should anyone selling you a training plan.

## How the book is organised

Chapter 1 asks what limits how fast you can ride for an hour, or for five. Endurance performance depends on how much oxygen you can use at most (your VO₂max), how close to that maximum you can ride for a long time, and how efficiently you turn that oxygen into power. Recent research adds a fourth factor: how well those three hold up when you're tired. The training chapters ask which of those four a session changes, and how strong the evidence is.

Part I, *The engine*, sets out the model, the biology of how bodies adapt to training, what the brain and nerves contribute, how to read a training study, and how to define and find your training intensities. Part II, *The easy part*, covers what low-intensity riding does to the body, why elite athletes do so much of it, durability, and the claims about health and longevity. Part III, *The hard part*, covers intervals: why time spent close to VO₂max seems to matter, how to design a session, and how many hard sessions a week make sense. Part IV, *Other ways to get faster*, weighs what else is credited with speed besides ordinary training: fuelling, strength training, heat training and drugs. Part V, *Putting it together*, builds a training week, deals with special cases such as masters riders, women and riding indoors, and looks at how to tell whether any of it is working. The last chapter answers the title's question.

If you skip anything, don't skip Chapters 2 and 4. The biology and the research skills in those two chapters are what the rest of the book builds on.

## Who this is for

This book is written for cyclists who want to understand their training rather than just follow it. It gets technical where the science needs it to, and it names researchers and studies throughout, because "studies show" is exactly the habit the book is trying to break. You don't need a science background. You do need some patience with numbers, and with the word *mitochondria*, which appears more often in this book than the word *bike*.

Most of the research is in cyclists, and where it isn't, I say so. Plenty of the best training studies were done in runners, rowers and cross-country skiers, and I use them where the physiology carries across.

This is not a training plan, and it isn't medical advice. If you have a heart condition or another medical reason to be careful with hard exercise, talk to a doctor before you change how you train.

## About me, and a disclosure

I am not a sports scientist. I am a quantitative researcher in finance, where I use behavioural analytics to help portfolio managers improve the way they invest, and I read a lot of finance research as part of the job. Before that I studied maths and worked at the European Space Agency and in banking. So I come to training studies as someone who judges quantitative claims for a living, in a field where a persuasive story with weak evidence behind it costs money.

I am also a rider short of time. I raced mountain bikes in my teens with my two older brothers. These days I have two children and a busy life, I ride about three sportives a year, and I go on a couple of cycling holidays with other dads from my children's school. I wanted to know what training would do the most for someone like that.

I should also be clear about an interest. I built an app, JustZone2, that controls a smart trainer to hold your heart rate in a target zone. I built it for my own winter training and other riders now use it. It gets one mention at the end of the book and none in between, and it has no bearing on what the evidence says. If anything, writing this book has made me more cautious about some of the claims I used to make for easy riding. Where the research says Zone 2 is overrated, this book says so.
