# Introduction: Why ask the question

In the spring of 2026 I rode up Sa Calobra faster than I ever had. The climb on the north coast of Mallorca is nine and a half kilometres at about seven per cent, with twenty-six hairpins and nowhere to recover, and I took two minutes and eleven seconds off my best time.

I knew why, or thought I did. I had spent the winter in my garage on a smart trainer, four or five times a week, riding for 45 minutes to an hour at a heart rate of about 142 beats per minute. It was the dullest training I had ever done. Nothing hurt, nothing was worth posting, and my ride history turned into a column of identical flat grey lines. Then I went to Mallorca, rode the effort I had learned by heart all winter, and found there was a lot more power under it than before.

That winter was Zone 2 training, and I wrote about it with the enthusiasm of a convert. I told readers of my website that easy riding was the single most effective way to build mitochondria, the structures in muscle cells that use oxygen to make energy. I believed it. I had heard it from people who sounded as if they knew.

A few months later I sat down to read the research properly, and that sentence didn't survive. The largest summary of the evidence, a review of 353 training studies published in 2024, found that mitochondrial content rose by similar amounts whether people did steady continuous training (about 23%), high-intensity intervals (27%) or all-out sprint intervals (27%). Per hour of training, the sprints did almost four times as much [@molmen2025]. Zone 2 had done something real for me. It just hadn't done it for the reason I had been told, or not only for that reason.

This book grew out of that gap: between what people confidently say about endurance training and what the studies actually show.

## A short history of training fashions

Endurance training goes through fashions, and they tend to follow the same pattern. Someone finds something real in a study. It gets explained with a mechanism that sounds convincing. Then it spreads faster than the evidence behind it, until the claims have little to do with what was found.

In 1996 a Japanese research group led by Izumi Tabata had seven people do a few minutes of very hard 20-second efforts on a bike, with 10 seconds of rest between each, five days a week for six weeks. Both their aerobic fitness and their capacity for short, hard efforts improved [@tabata1996]. It was a small study of a punishing protocol. Within a few years "Tabata" had become a name for almost any four-minute workout in a gym class, most of them nothing like what the study's subjects did.

Ten years later Martin Gibala's lab in Canada compared two small groups of active young men over two weeks. One group did short sessions of 30-second all-out sprints. The other did long steady rides, about four times as much training in total. Both groups improved by similar amounts [@gibala2006]. It was a careful study with a striking result, and it launched a decade of articles promising the benefits of endurance training in a fraction of the time. Fewer of those articles mentioned that the study lasted two weeks, involved 16 people, and measured early changes in muscle, not what happens over a season.

Around the same time, the Norway-based physiologist Stephen Seiler was describing how the world's best endurance athletes actually trained. Across sports, he found, they did roughly four easy sessions for every hard one, with very little in between [@seiler2010]. That observation became "80/20" and "polarised training", and for a while every training plan had to be polarised.

Then came Zone 2. Its rise owed a lot to podcasts, in particular a 2019 conversation between the physician Peter Attia and the physiologist Iñigo San-Millán, who worked with professional cycling teams [@attia2019]. San-Millán's research on how well athletes and patients burn fat and clear lactate was real and interesting. Repeated across thousands of episodes and social media posts, it became something larger: a training intensity with special powers over mitochondria, metabolism and even lifespan.

None of these fashions was simply wrong. High-intensity intervals do work, elite athletes do mostly train easy, and easy riding does build an aerobic base. What each fashion got wrong was its confidence, and its silence about who was studied, for how long, and what was actually measured.

## What this book tries to do

There is no shortage of training advice. What's in short supply is a way of judging it. So this book has two aims, and every chapter works towards both.

The first is to explain the mechanism. For each training claim, I want you to understand the biology underneath it: what happens in a muscle, a heart or a bloodstream when you ride easy or ride hard, and why that might make you faster. Mechanisms matter because they tell you *why* something should work. They are also where fashions start, because a plausible mechanism is far easier to find than evidence that the training actually improves performance. "Easy riding burns the most fat, so it must build the most mitochondria" is a mechanism argument. Whether it's true is a different question, and the answer turned out to be no.

The second aim is to help you judge research yourself. Each chapter includes a short section called *Reading the research*, which takes one study from that chapter and uses it to practise a single skill: telling correlation from cause, spotting a sample too small to trust, noticing when "a trend" means "not significant", or asking who was actually studied. By the end of the book you should be able to read a training study, or a confident claim about one, and decide for yourself how much weight it can bear. The next fashion is already on its way, and this book won't be there when it arrives.

To keep the book honest about its own claims, the important ones carry a grade:

- **A**: several randomised trials, or a well-conducted meta-analysis, pointing the same way.
- **B**: consistent findings, but from small or short studies, a single lab, or one good trial.
- **C**: observational data, mechanism, case studies or expert opinion.

A grade C claim isn't necessarily wrong. Most of what coaches know started as grade C, and some of it will become grade A. But you should know which is which, and so should anyone selling you a training plan.

## How the book is organised

The book is built around one question, which is what actually limits how fast you can ride for an hour, or for five. Chapter 1 answers it with a model that physiologists have used for decades. Endurance performance depends on how much oxygen you can use at most (your VO₂max), how close to that maximum you can ride for a long time, and how efficiently you turn that oxygen into power. Recent research adds a fourth factor: how well those three hold up when you're tired. Almost every training question in this book comes down to which of those four a session changes, and how strong the evidence is.

Part I, *The engine*, sets out the model, the biology of how bodies adapt to training, how to read a training study, and how to define and find your training intensities. Part II, *The easy part*, covers what low-intensity riding does to the body, why elite athletes do so much of it, durability, and the claims about health and longevity. Part III, *The hard part*, covers intervals: why time spent close to VO₂max seems to matter, how to design a session, and how many hard sessions a week make sense. Part IV puts it together into a training week, deals with special cases such as masters riders, women and riding indoors, and looks at how to tell whether any of it is working. The last chapter answers the title's question.

You can read the chapters in order or dip into the ones you care about. If you skip anything, don't skip Chapters 2 and 3. The biology and the research skills in those two chapters are what the rest of the book builds on.

## Who this is for

This book is written for cyclists who want to understand their training rather than just follow it. It gets technical where the science needs it to, and it names researchers and studies throughout, because "studies show" is exactly the habit the book is trying to break. You don't need a science background. You do need some patience with numbers.

Most of the research is in cyclists, and where it isn't, I say so. Plenty of the best training studies were done in runners, rowers and cross-country skiers, and I use them where the physiology carries across.

This is not a training plan, and it isn't medical advice. If you have a heart condition or another medical reason to be careful with hard exercise, talk to a doctor before you change how you train.
