# Chapter 5: Intensity, properly defined

In 2006 Stephen Seiler and Glenn Kjerland published the training records of 11 nationally competitive junior cross-country skiers in Norway. Over 32 days the skiers had logged 384 sessions, and the researchers sorted the endurance sessions into three zones by heart rate. Three-quarters fell in zone 1 and 17% in zone 3. Zone 2 got 8%. The paper's summary ended by remarking that elite endurance athletes seem to "train surprisingly little" at that middle intensity [@seilerkjerland2006].

In 2025 a panel of 14 sport scientists and coaches published a paper called *What is "Zone 2 training"?* It set out how Zone 2 sessions should be done and what they should be expected to achieve, for a kind of training that many people now say should make up a large share of an endurance athlete's week [@sitko2025]. Seiler was one of the authors.

He had not changed his mind. The zone 2 of the 2006 paper lies between two physiological thresholds. The Zone 2 of the 2025 paper lies below the lower of them. A coach who tells you to stay out of zone 2 and a podcast that tells you to live in Zone 2 may be describing the same week of training.

Most arguments about training intensity have this problem somewhere in them. "Threshold" has at least two meanings and dozens of ways of being measured. "Easy" is often set as a percentage of a maximum heart rate that was itself guessed from the rider's age. Before Part II can ask what easy riding does, the words need fixing to something in the body.

## The three intensity domains

Chapter 1 introduced the idea that exercise intensity divides into domains: moderate below the first threshold, heavy between the first threshold and critical power, and severe above critical power. The boundaries are there because the body behaves differently on each side of them, and a 2017 experiment at the University of Exeter shows the difference more clearly than any definition [@black2017].

Eleven recreationally active men rode to exhaustion at a constant power in each domain, on separate days, with muscle biopsies taken from most of them. In the moderate domain, at 20 W below their first threshold (113 W on average), they lasted three and a half hours. Their blood lactate barely moved from its starting level. When they stopped, the glycogen in their thigh muscle was down by 83%. The likeliest reason they stopped is that they had run out of fuel [@black2017].

In the heavy domain, at an average of 231 W, they lasted 43 minutes, and the individual times ran from 20 minutes to 67. Twice the power had cut the time by four-fifths [@black2017].

In the severe domain they did several rides at different powers, and lasted between 2 and 14 minutes. Every rider reached his maximum oxygen uptake on every one of those rides. The chemistry inside the muscle at the point of stopping (its acidity, its lactate, its store of phosphocreatine) did not differ measurably whether the ride had taken 2 minutes or 14. Above critical power the riders stopped at a fixed chemical limit, and how hard they were riding decided only how soon they reached it [@black2017]. {grade B}

Martin Burnley and Andrew Jones had described the same pattern from earlier work {shadow @burnleyjones2018}, and the review led by David Poole and Jones calls the boundaries consistent across species and kinds of exercise {shadow @poole2016}. That there are three domains, with different fatigue, different fuel use and different limits, is well established. What is unsettled is how to find the two boundaries in a given person, and what to call them.

## How it works: lactate, and why breathing changes

Muscle breaks glucose down in two stages. The first, glycolysis, is fast, needs no oxygen and ends in a molecule called pyruvate. The second takes place in the mitochondria, uses oxygen, and extracts most of the energy. When glycolysis runs faster than the mitochondria take up pyruvate, the surplus is converted to lactate. This happens all the time, at rest included. Lactate then moves out of the fibre that made it and into the blood, and other fibres, the heart and the liver take it up and burn it or turn it back into glucose. The physiologist George Brooks, who spent a career establishing this, calls the traffic the lactate shuttle: lactate is a fuel being passed between cells, and a signalling molecule as well {shadow @brooks2018}.

The concentration in the blood is therefore a balance. It is the rate at which lactate appears minus the rate at which it is removed, and it says little about either on its own {shadow @brooks1985}. At rest and during easy riding the two are equal and the level sits near its baseline, around 1 mmol/L in most people. At the first threshold, production begins to outrun removal. The level rises and then settles at a new, higher balance. Push further and you reach an intensity at which it can no longer settle: lactate climbs for as long as the effort lasts.

Breathing follows. Lactate leaves the muscle together with hydrogen ions, which would make the blood more acidic if nothing absorbed them. Bicarbonate in the blood absorbs them, and that reaction releases carbon dioxide. The extra carbon dioxide has to be breathed out, so above the first threshold breathing rises faster than oxygen uptake does. That is the first ventilatory threshold, and it is why holding a conversation gets harder at about the point where lactate begins to rise. At the second threshold the buffering falls behind, the blood starts to acidify, and breathing rises again, now faster even than carbon dioxide output. A laboratory with a gas mask can see both breakpoints without taking any blood.

The man who connected breathing to lactate was Karlman Wasserman, in a 1964 paper with a colleague on detecting what its title called the threshold of anaerobic metabolism in cardiac patients {shadow @wasserman1964}. His explanation was that the working muscle had run short of oxygen and switched to making energy without it. The method survived and the explanation did not. Lactate rises because glycolysis speeds up as the work gets harder, and muscle that has all the oxygen it needs produces it [@poole2021]{shadow @brooks1985}. A 2021 review of the argument, with Brooks and Poole among its authors, carries the subtitle "50+ years of controversy" [@poole2021]. The name is still in everyday use. When you see "anaerobic threshold", read it as a threshold of some kind, and find out which.

One more change starts at the first threshold, and it matters more to a rider than lactate does. Below it, a given power costs a fixed amount of oxygen, and heart rate and breathing level off within a few minutes. Above it, the oxygen cost of holding the same power creeps upward for many minutes. Physiologists call this the slow component of oxygen uptake. It reflects the muscle becoming less efficient as it tires and as it brings in fast-twitch fibres that use more oxygen for the same work {shadow @jones2011}. A steady effort above the first threshold therefore costs more than its power suggests, and costs more the longer it goes on.

## The first threshold

It has many names: first lactate threshold (LT1), first ventilatory threshold (VT1), gas exchange threshold, aerobic threshold. They describe the same transition seen through different instruments, and where studies have used both lactate and breathing on the same riders, the instruments agree reasonably well. In 14 well-trained cyclists the power at the lactate threshold and at VT1 did not differ significantly. The average gap was about 14 W, though, and the correlation between the two was 0.72, which leaves room for larger gaps in individuals {shadow @pallares2016}. In 50 trained cyclists tested in Germany, in a study this chapter returns to, VT1 came at 200 W on average and the first rise in lactate at 199 W [@meixner2025].

There is more than one way to read each instrument. A 2009 review found 25 separate lactate threshold concepts in the literature and sorted them into three families: fixed concentrations, the first rise above baseline, and methods aimed at the highest steady state {shadow @faude2009}. The first-rise family alone includes "0.5 mmol/L above baseline", "1.0 above baseline" and several curve-fitting methods, and they give different answers from one set of blood samples [@jamnick2018].

Where the first threshold sits is a personal matter. Danilo Iannetta and colleagues in Calgary tested 100 men and women and found it at anything from 45% to 74% of VO₂max, from 60% to 90% of maximum heart rate, and from 23% to 57% of the peak power reached in a ramp test [@iannetta2020]. Seventy per cent of maximum heart rate was in the moderate domain for some of those people and in the heavy domain for others. Their conclusion was that prescriptions based on fixed percentages of a maximum "do not adequately control the metabolic stimulus". {grade B}

The threshold also moves. Training raises it: Chapter 1 gave the figures, from around 60% of VO₂max in untrained people to 75–90% in elite athletes {shadow @joynercoyle2008}. And it falls during a long ride. In a study from Auckland that Chapter 9 describes in full, two hours of easy riding lowered the power at VT1 from 217 W to 196 W {shadow @stevenson2022}. A number from a fresh laboratory test describes the first hour of a ride better than the fourth.

## The second threshold

The upper boundary has more names and less agreement: second lactate threshold (LT2), second ventilatory threshold (VT2), respiratory compensation point, maximal lactate steady state, onset of blood lactate accumulation, critical power, functional threshold power, and, again, anaerobic threshold.

The oldest practical version is a fixed number. In the 1970s and 1980s physiologists, most of them German, took 4 mmol/L of blood lactate as the mark of the highest sustainable intensity [@jones2019]. In 1979 Wilfried Kindermann's group had seven national-level cross-country skiers run on a treadmill for at least 30 minutes at the speed that an earlier test had matched to 4 mmol/L. Their lactate rose to nearly that level and then stayed there for the rest of the run {shadow @kindermann1979}. A rule that is still in use rested, at the start, on seven skiers.

It didn't hold up as a universal number. The lactate level at which a person's steady state tops out varies widely between individuals and between sports [@jones2019]. So the definition moved from a concentration to a procedure. The maximal lactate steady state (MLSS) is found by riding for 30 minutes at a constant power, on four or five separate days at different powers. The threshold is the highest power at which blood lactate rises by less than 1 mmol/L between the tenth minute and the thirtieth.

A 2018 study from Victoria University in Melbourne shows how far the shortcuts miss [@jamnick2018]. Seventeen trained cyclists had their MLSS measured the long way: it averaged 264 W. They also did stepped tests with stages of four different lengths, and the researchers calculated 14 different lactate thresholds from each. The fixed 4 mmol/L rule was 14 W to 41 W too high. The same 17 riders had 56 lactate thresholds between them, depending on the method and the length of the stages, and the authors judged many of the traditional methods not valid.

MLSS has its own critics. Andrew Jones and colleagues argued in 2019 that it underestimates the true boundary by construction [@jones2019]. If the trial powers are 30 W apart and you take the highest one that passed, you will be about 15 W low on average. A lactate meter has an error of 0.2 to 0.4 mmol/L, in a test that turns on a difference of 1.0.

Their candidate for the real boundary is critical power, which Chapter 1 described: the power the curve of your best efforts flattens towards. In the critical power model the boundary is defined by performance, and the physiology follows: just below it oxygen uptake and lactate stabilise, and just above it they climb to their maximum. A 2020 review of every common method concluded that there is some evidence for the first lactate and ventilatory thresholds as the lower boundary. For the upper one it found little evidence for anything except critical power [@jamnick2020]. The authors of the 2019 paper are the researchers most associated with critical power, and their opponents point out that a rider can hold their measured critical power for only 20 to 30 minutes, which is short for something described as sustainable [@jones2019].

Most cyclists meet the second threshold as functional threshold power, or FTP. The term comes from Andrew Coggan, who defined it as the highest power a rider can maintain in a quasi-steady state for about an hour. He offered a field test: ride as hard as you can for 20 minutes and take 95% of the average [@allencoggan2010]. It is a performance measure standing in for a physiological one, and whether it stands in well has been tested.

### Reading the research: agreement on average is not agreement for you

Two studies have compared FTP with the maximal lactate steady state, and they reached opposite conclusions.

| | Brazil, 2019 [@borszcz2019] | Canada, 2020 [@inglis2020]
| Riders | 15 trained cyclists | 18 people
| Average difference | FTP 1.4% from the power at MLSS | MLSS at 88.5% of 20-minute power, so FTP about 7% too high
| Authors' conclusion | Trained cyclists can use FTP as a practical alternative to the laboratory measure | Using FTP to represent MLSS "would not be advisable"
:::

The Brazilian paper also reported a correlation of 0.91 between the two, which it called nearly perfect. The Canadian group brought ten of its riders back after seven months of training. Their MLSS had risen by 12 W and their FTP had not risen [@inglis2020].

The two papers are closer than their conclusions. The Brazilian abstract reports a third number beside the 1.4%, the 95% limits of agreement, which were plus or minus 9.2%. Limits of agreement describe individuals. They say that for 95% of riders like these, FTP will fall within about 9% of their MLSS in either direction. For a rider whose steady state is truly at 250 W, that is an FTP anywhere from roughly 230 W to 275 W. Among the less trained riders in the group the limits were plus or minus 11.8% [@borszcz2019].

A correlation of 0.91 doesn't contradict this. Correlation asks whether riders with a high MLSS also have a high FTP, and across a group that runs from modest club riders to very fit ones, they will. It would still be 0.91 if every rider's FTP were 30 W too high.

When a paper says a cheap test is valid against an expensive one, the average difference tells you whether the cheap test is biased, the limits of agreement tell you how wrong it can be for one person, and the correlation tells you least.

## Three zones, five zones, seven

Once there are two boundaries, the simplest scheme has three zones: below the first, between the two, above the second. The framework goes back at least to a 1980 paper by James Skinner and Thomas McLellan, which described three phases in the transition from easy to maximal work {shadow @skinner1980}. Seiler adopted it for his studies of how athletes distribute their training, and most research on the subject has used it since [@seiler2010]. In this scheme zone 2 is the middle band, the heavy domain.

Coaches wanted finer divisions. The scale used by the Norwegian Olympic federation has five zones. Its zones 1 and 2 both lie below the first threshold, zone 3 is the band between the thresholds, and zones 4 and 5 lie above the second [@seiler2010]. Its Zone 2 is given as 72–82% of maximum heart rate, or a blood lactate of 1.5 to 2.5 mmol/L [@meixner2025]. Most heart-rate zone systems in apps and bike computers have five zones of this general kind, and this is the Zone 2 of the podcasts.

Coggan's power levels, widely used in power-meter software, number seven and are all set as percentages of FTP. Level 2, "endurance", is 56–75% of FTP. Above it come "tempo", "lactate threshold" at around FTP itself, and three more levels [@allencoggan2010]. These zones are easy to calculate, which is why software uses them. But the first threshold appears nowhere in the calculation. The top of Level 2 is wherever 75% of your FTP happens to be. In Iannetta's 100 volunteers the first threshold ranged from 23% to 57% of peak ramp power and the upper steady state from 44% to 71% [@iannetta2020]. With ranges that wide the gap between a person's two thresholds is unlikely to be a fixed proportion. A zone system built from one anchor has to assume that it is.

When Seiler reported that elite athletes do about 80% of their training at low intensity, he was counting sessions. A session with 20 minutes of hard intervals in it counts as a hard session, though most of its minutes are easy. In 2014 his group measured how much this matters, using 570 sessions by 29 elite cross-country skiers at a training camp [@sylta2014]. Counted by session, 87% were zone 1, 11% zone 2 and 2% zone 3. Counted by minutes of heart rate in each zone, the same training was 96%, 3% and 1%. Someone who trains "80/20" by sessions and someone who trains "95/5" by time may be doing the same thing.

## What Zone 2 means now

The 2025 panel was convened because the term had spread without a definition. Its 14 members agreed that Zone 2 training "should preferably be performed at intensities located immediately below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold", and that it can be done as continuous, variable or interval sessions [@sitko2025]. They expected a broad range of adaptations from it, and added that these "might not be unique" to Zone 2 and could come from riding slightly harder or slightly easier. The definition is expert opinion and carries no more weight than that, but it is the nearest thing to an agreed meaning, and this book starts from it. {grade C}

The panel left one choice open, between lactate and breathing [@sitko2025], and the book makes it. From here on the top of Zone 2 is the first lactate threshold: the last intensity before blood lactate rises 0.5 mmol/L above the rider's own baseline, in a test with stages of at least four minutes. Lactate is what the case for Zone 2 was built on, a rider can measure it with a handheld meter, and a rise above one's own baseline travels between people better than a fixed figure such as 2 mmol/L. The breathing threshold is its closest relative and on average falls close to it. Chapter 6 gives the reasons in full, along with the evidence that the choice is a practical one: no study shows lactate to be the better boundary of the two.

In practice the label is attached to many other things. The Canadian review that criticised the Zone 2 fashion listed the definitions it found in use: below the first lactate threshold, taken as 2.0 mmol/L; below the first ventilatory threshold; below the intensity of peak fat burning; under 45% of VO₂max {shadow @storoschuk2025}. It also pointed out how far the same definition travels between people. The top of Zone 2 can be 24% of VO₂max in a sedentary person, which is a walk, and 80% in an endurance athlete, which may be 300 W.

### One study, up close: nine markers, fifty cyclists

In 2025 Benedikt Meixner and colleagues, working from the University of Würzburg, asked what happens when you apply the common definitions of Zone 2 to the same riders [@meixner2025].

They recruited 50 cyclists, 30 men and 20 women, all with more than three years of regular riding. Their average VO₂peak was 54 mL/kg/min: trained amateurs [@meixner2025]. Each came to the laboratory twice within a week. The first visit was a ramp test to find maximum oxygen uptake and peak power. The second was a step test, starting at 100 W and rising by 25 W every three minutes, with a mask measuring breathing gases and a drop of blood taken from the earlobe at the end of each stage. From the step test the researchers calculated the power at which each rider reached nine markers: three lactate concentrations, a rise of 0.5 mmol/L above baseline, VT1, 65% of VO₂peak, peak fat burning, and 72% and 82% of maximum heart rate, the edges of the Norwegian Zone 2.

Drawn from Table 2 of Meixner and colleagues [@meixner2025].](figures/zone2-markers.svg)

On average the markers ran from 125 W to 224 W. Three of them agreed: VT1 at 200 W, the first rise in lactate at 199 W, and 82% of maximum heart rate at 202 W. A lactate of 2.0 mmol/L came at 211 W and 2.5 mmol/L at 224 W, both above the first threshold for the average rider. Peak fat burning came far lower, at a median of 125 W, and the authors put it at roughly a quarter below VT1 as a share of peak power. A rider who sets Zone 2 by fat burning and one who sets it by 2 mmol/L of lactate are 86 W apart on these averages, and the second is no longer below the first threshold [@meixner2025].

The Norwegian scale's two definitions disagree with each other. Its heart-rate band, 72–82% of maximum, ran from 148 W to 202 W in these riders. Its lactate band, 1.5 to 2.5 mmol/L, ran from 194 W to 224 W. The two bands share 8 W, and the lower lactate figure comes from only 41 riders. The other nine never showed a lactate below 1.5 mmol/L at any point in a test that started at 100 W, so for them the lactate version of the zone has no lower edge [@meixner2025].

For individuals the picture is looser still. VT1 sat at 81% of maximum heart rate on average, with a standard deviation of 3.9 percentage points. Roughly a third of riders were therefore outside the range 77.5–85.3%. The authors note that a difference of five percentage points is about 10 beats a minute [@meixner2025].

The study's headline finding needs care. The abstract reports "substantial variability", with coefficients of variation between riders of 6% to 29% depending on the marker. Those coefficients were calculated on raw watts and raw beats per minute. Riders differ in fitness, and in this group peak power itself varied by 19% and maximum heart rate by 5.7%. A marker that sat at an identical percentage of every rider's maximum would still show variation of about that size. Most of the 6–29% is the spread of fitness in the sample, and it says little about whether the markers are good or bad. The stronger evidence is in the table of averages and in the agreement analysis, which the paper shows only as charts [@meixner2025].

Nobody in the study did any training. It shows that the markers land in different places, and it can't show which of them makes the better ceiling for an easy ride. It was one laboratory, one step protocol and riders mostly in their twenties and thirties. The authors declared no funding and no conflicts of interest. It does establish that the common definitions of Zone 2 pick out different intensities in the same rider. {grade B}

## Power, heart rate and feel

A zone can be written in watts, in beats per minute or as a feeling, and each is a different kind of measurement. Power is what you put in. It responds instantly and takes no account of the day you are having. Heart rate is one of the body's responses. It lags behind a change in effort, and it drifts upward over a long ride for reasons Chapter 9 explains. The domains are defined by the body's response, so neither reading is the thing itself.

The Auckland study shows the two readings coming apart. After two hours the power at the first threshold had fallen by 21 W, and the heart rate at the threshold had risen from 142 to 151 beats per minute {shadow @stevenson2022}. A rider who set out at the threshold power from the fresh test would have been 21 W above the threshold by the end. A rider who held the heart rate from the fresh test would have finished further below the threshold than intended.

Perceived effort does better than its reputation. In the 2006 study the skiers also rated each session on a scale of perceived exertion of the kind Gunnar Borg introduced {shadow @borg1982}. Sorted by those ratings, the sessions came out within two percentage points of the heart-rate figures in every zone [@seilerkjerland2006]. Over a month of training, the athletes' own ratings and their heart rate monitors gave the same account. Only the monitors needed batteries. Chapter 6 covers the talk test, which turns the same sense into a method.

## The terms as this book uses them

From here on the book holds each word to one meaning.

- **The first threshold** is the first lactate threshold (LT1): the intensity at which blood lactate first rises above the rider's own baseline, taken as a rise of 0.5 mmol/L in a test with stages of at least four minutes. Breathing first rises out of proportion to oxygen uptake at about the same point, the first ventilatory threshold (VT1). Where a study measured the threshold from breathing, the text says so.
- **The second threshold** is the highest intensity at which the body can still reach a steady state. Where a study measured critical power, MLSS or FTP, the text names the measure, because they are not interchangeable.
- **Easy**, or low intensity, means below the first threshold: the physiologists' moderate domain and zone 1 of the three-zone model. Physiologists call this intensity moderate and riders call it easy, and the book uses the riders' word.
- **Zone 2**, capitalised, means the upper part of that easy range, immediately below the first threshold, as the 2025 panel defined it. When a study uses the three-zone model, its zone 2 appears here as "between the thresholds".
- **Between the thresholds** is the heavy domain. Riders call parts of it tempo, sweet spot and threshold; the book uses those words only for a session a study itself gave that name.
- **Hard**, or high intensity, means above the second threshold, in the severe domain.

Whenever a study, a coach or an app uses one of these words, two questions are worth asking. Which of the two boundaries is meant, and on which map? And how was the boundary found for the person doing the training: measured, or calculated as a percentage of something else? Several of the studies in Part II set intensity as a percentage of a maximum, and Iannetta's figures mean that some of their volunteers were probably not training in the domain the paper's title names.
