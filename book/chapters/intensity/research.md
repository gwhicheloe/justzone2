# Intensity, properly defined: research notes

Written 11 October 2026. Tags: [FT] full text; [Abs] abstract; [Rep] reported by a source I read; [GK] general knowledge, unchecked; [Own] my arithmetic or inference.

**How far each source was read.** Full text (open access, saved as text in `papers/`): Meixner 2025 (all of it), Black 2017 (results and discussion), Jones 2019 (MLSS and critical power sections), Jamnick 2018 (abstract, threshold definitions, Table 3). Stevenson 2022 and Storoschuk 2025 were read in full for earlier chapters. Everything else: abstract only. **Not read at all:** Wasserman 1964, Skinner & McLellan 1980 (no abstracts exist), Allen & Coggan's book, Seiler 2010 beyond its abstract.

## The two Zone 2s (opening)

- Seiler & Kjerland 2006 [Abs]: 11 male junior cross-country skiers, "nationally competitive"; 384 sessions (37 strength, 347 endurance) in 32 days; 318 endurance sessions analysed. Heart rate: 75 ± 3% zone 1, 8 ± 3% zone 2, 17 ± 4% zone 3. Session RPE: 76 / 6 / 18. Lactate in 60 sessions: 71% ≤2.0 mM, 7% 2 to 4, 22% over 4. "Elite endurance athletes train surprisingly little at the lactate threshold intensity." That the skiers were in Norway, and Kjerland's first name (Glenn): [GK].
- Sitko 2025 [Abs]: panel of 14 applied sport scientists and professional coaches; "zone 2 training should preferably be performed at intensities located immediately below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold through continuous, variable, or interval-type sessions"; expected adaptations "might not be unique to zone 2 and could also be induced with sessions performed at slightly higher and lower intensities". Says many propose Zone 2 for "a large proportion" of training. Seiler is the eleventh of 14 listed authors [FT of the author list]. **I assume the 14 authors are the panel; the abstract doesn't say.** How consensus was reached (vote, Delphi rounds) is not in the abstract.

## Domains

- Black 2017 [FT]: 11 healthy recreationally active men, age 21.8, University of Exeter; 8 volunteered for biopsies, one of those withdrew after the severe trials. Moderate: 20 W below gas exchange threshold, 113 ± 19 W, time to the limit 211 ± 57 min (range 180 to 360); VO₂ 52% of peak; blood lactate "largely unchanged"; muscle glycogen −83%; authors: glycogen depletion likely involved. Heavy: 231 ± 56 W, 43.5 ± 16.2 min (20.5 to 67.4). Severe: 4 to 5 tests, 2.2 to 13.9 min, VO₂peak reached "in all cases"; muscle pH, PCr, lactate at the limit not different across three severe work rates.
- "Twice the power, a fifth of the time": 231/113 = 2.04; 43.5/211 = 0.21 [Own].
- Burnley & Jones 2018, Poole 2016: as in the What limits endurance notes.

## Lactate and breathing (How it works)

- Brooks 2018 [Abs]: lactate "forms continuously under aerobic conditions"; energy source, gluconeogenic precursor, signalling molecule; the lactate shuttle.
- Brooks 1985 [Abs]: blood lactate is the result of appearance and removal and "provides minimal information about the rate of lactate production in muscle"; lactate is produced "for reasons other than an O₂ limitation"; ventilatory threshold does not always track lactate.
- Poole 2021 [Abs]: Wasserman's scheme (limited oxygen → glycolysis → lactate and H⁺ → buffered by bicarbonate → more CO₂ → more ventilation); "insufficient O₂ is not the primary basis for lactataemia"; critical power is the threshold for a sustained non-oxidative contribution; gas exchange threshold "remains important".
- Wasserman & McIlroy 1964: title only. What it argued is from Poole 2021 [Rep].
- Jones 2011 [Abs]: VO₂ slow component above the lactate threshold; "progressive loss of skeletal muscle contractile efficiency"; associated with recruitment of type II fibres and with fatigue.
- [GK], standard textbook physiology, not from a source read for this chapter: glycolysis and pyruvate; resting lactate around 1 mmol/L; lactate leaving muscle with hydrogen ions; the second ventilatory threshold as the point where ventilation outruns CO₂ output; heart rate and VO₂ levelling off in minutes below the first threshold. **A physiology textbook citation would firm these up.**

## First threshold

- Pallarés 2016 [Abs]: 14 trained male cyclists (VO₂max 62.1). Workload at lactate threshold vs VT1: p = 0.147, r = 0.72, bias −13.6 (watts, sign not explained). The draft says "about 14 W".
- Meixner 2025 [FT]: VT1 200 ± 45 W, baseline + 0.5 mmol/L 199 ± 45 W; authors describe "relatively small limits of agreement and minimal bias" but give no numbers in the text.
- Faude 2009 [Abs]: 25 threshold concepts, three categories.
- Jamnick 2018 [FT]: baseline + 0.5, + 1.0, + 1.5 are separate methods.
- Iannetta 2020 [Abs]: 100 people (46 women); LT 45 to 74% VO₂max, 23 to 57% peak work rate, 60 to 90% HRmax; MLSS 69 to 96%, 44 to 71%, 75 to 97%. Calgary: [GK]. "70% of HRmax is moderate for some and heavy for others" follows from LT at 60 to 90% and MLSS at 75% or more [Own].
- Stevenson 2022 [FT]: see Durability notes.

## Second threshold

- Jones 2019 [FT]: MLSS "may perhaps be attributed to" Mader and Heck in the 1980s; first taken as a fixed 2.2 or "more often" 4 mmol/L; lactate at MLSS varies between individuals and modes. Protocol: typically 4 to 5 bouts of 30 min on separate days; <1 mmol/L rise between 10 and 30 min. With 30 W steps MLSS is underestimated by 15 W on average. Analyser error 0.2 to 0.4 mmol/L. CP sustainable "no more than approximately 20–30 min". Their reply to the "fatigueless task" argument (read 26 Oct 2026): Tlim = W′/(P − CP) is undefined at CP, and a CP estimate has a "bandwidth" of about ±3–5% even with careful protocol (example: CP 300 W with a 2% standard error lies between 294 and 306 W), so a rider set to ride at exactly CP has "a 50% chance" of being above it. Time to the limit at CP in the studies they cite: "approximately 15 to 40 min or occasionally up to ~60 min". No conflict declared. "Most of them German" (the 4 mmol physiologists): Mader, Heck, Kindermann are German [GK]; Sjödin is not.
- Kindermann 1979 [Abs]: seven national-level cross-country skiers, at least 30 min at the speed (and separately the heart rate) of the 4 mmol/L threshold; lactate "initially rose to values of nearly 4 mmol/l and then remained essentially constant"; heart rate rose slightly throughout, averaging above 170. First name Wilfried: [GK].
- Jamnick 2018 [FT]: 17 trained male cyclists, VO₂peak 61.0, MLSS 264 ± 39 W. Fixed 4.0 mmol/L from tests with 3-, 4-, 7- and 10-min stages: 306, 291, 281, 279 W; mean differences −41.3, −26.3, −16.8, −14.2 W. Baseline + 0.5: 235, 229, 228, 225 W. 14 calculations × 4 tests = 56. "Many of the traditional LT methods were not valid."
- Jamnick 2020 [Abs]: see sources.yaml.
- Coggan / FTP: definition ("highest power a rider can maintain in a quasi-steady state for approximately one hour"), 95% of 20-min power, and level boundaries (L1 <55%, L2 56–75%, L3 76–90%, L4 91–105%, L5 106–120%, L6 121–150%, L7 maximal): **[GK]. Check against the book.** Borszcz 2019's abstract confirms "95% of the average power during a 20-min time trial".
- Borszcz 2019 [Abs]: 15 cyclists (7 trained, 8 well trained), VO₂max 62.3. FTP vs MLSS: bias ± 95% limits of agreement 1.4% ± 9.2%; typical error 4.7%; r = 0.91 ("nearly perfect"). Trained: 1.4% ± 11.8%; well trained 1.3% ± 7.4%. Brazilian: [GK]. **Direction of the bias isn't stated**, so the draft says "differed by 1.4%" and "within about 9% in either direction".
- Worked example [Own]: 250 W ± 9.2% is 227 to 273 W; shifted by +1.4% it is 230.5 to 276.5. The draft says "roughly 230 W to 275 W".
- Inglis 2020 [Abs]: 18 participants; MLSS = 88.5 ± 4.8% of 20-min power, 93.1 ± 5.1% of FTP; 1/0.931 = FTP 7.4% above MLSS [Own]. 10 retested after 7 months: MLSS +12 ± 8 W, no increase in FTP (P = .75). Canadian: [GK].

## Zone models

- Three-phase model from Skinner & McLellan 1980: [GK]; paper not read. First names James and Thomas: [GK].
- Norwegian five-zone scale and its mapping to three zones (zones 1 and 2 below the first threshold, 3 between, 4 and 5 above): **[GK], attributed to Seiler 2010, which I have as an abstract only.** Zone 2 as 72 to 82% HRmax and 1.5 to 2.5 mmol/L: Meixner 2025 [FT], citing the Norwegian Olympic Federation.
- "Most heart-rate zone systems in apps have five zones": [GK].
- Iannetta's ranges imply the gap between thresholds is not a fixed proportion: [Own]; the ranges alone don't prove it.
- Sylta 2014 [Abs]: 29 elite cross-country skiers, 570 sessions, ~14-day altitude camp. Time in zone 96.1 / 2.9 / 1.1%; session goal 86.6 / 11.1 / 2.4%.
- Seiler's 80% counts sessions: Seiler 2010 [Abs] and the website evidence review.

## What Zone 2 means; Meixner close-up

- Storoschuk 2025 [FT]: definitions and the 24% to 80% range; see `chapters/easy-riding/research.md`.
- Meixner 2025 [FT], Table 2 (mean ± SD power; % HRmax): FatMax 125 ± 37 W (median ± MAD), 72.3%; HR72% 148 ± 41; 65% VO₂peak 183 ± 49, 78.2%; 1.5 mmol/L 194 ± 56, 78.1% (n = 41); baseline + 0.5 199 ± 45, 81.2%; VT1 200 ± 45, 81.4 ± 3.9%; HR82% 202 ± 46; 2.0 mmol/L 211 ± 53, 83.4%; 2.5 mmol/L 224 ± 52, 86.0%. Peak: 387 ± 73 W (ramp), HRmax 179 ± 10.
- Participants: 30 men, 20 women, >3 years' cycling, age 31.2 ± 7.8, VO₂peak 54.1 ± 8.1. Two visits within 7 days. Step test from 100 W (three riders from 75 W), 25 W per 3 min. No significant difference among baseline + 0.5, 1.5 mmol/L, VT1 and HR82%, or between HR72% and FatMax; all other pairs differed.
- FatMax "generally ~25% lower than VT1": the authors' words; as % of peak ramp power 38.7 vs 51.5 [FT], which is 25% lower [Own]. The 125 W median and the 38.7% mean don't describe the same average (38.7% of 387 W is 150 W), so the draft gives the median watts and the authors' quarter.
- Bands overlap by 8 W (148–202 against 194–224); 211 − 125 = 86 W; a third of riders outside 81.4 ± 3.9 assumes a normal distribution: all [Own].
- CVs: reported for absolute values; maxima have CVs of 21.7% (VO₂), 5.7% (heart rate), 18.9% (ramp power) [FT]. The reading that most of the 6–29% is fitness spread is **mine** [Own].
- One author lists a professional cycling team as an affiliation; conflicts "none", funding "none" [FT]. Not mentioned in the draft beyond the declaration.
