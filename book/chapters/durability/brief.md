## The question

Why do some riders fade badly after three hours while others with the same numbers when fresh hardly fade at all, and what can you do about it?

## The argument (to approve)

1. The classic model of endurance performance (VO₂max, the fraction of it you can sustain, efficiency) describes a rider who is fresh. Real events are ridden tired. Two riders with identical lab results can perform very differently at hour four.
2. Researchers have started measuring this directly, under the names *durability* and *physiological resilience*: how much power, thresholds and efficiency deteriorate during prolonged work. Maunder and colleagues made the case in 2021; Andrew Jones has since argued it is a fourth determinant of performance in its own right.
3. In professional cyclists it varies a lot between riders and separates the best from the rest: their power after thousands of kilojoules of work falls off less. *(To verify: size of the differences, and whether this holds below World Tour level.)*
4. What trains it is the weakest part of the evidence. Most data are observational: riders who train more, or do more long riding, tend to be more durable, which may be cause or effect. There is at least one training study suggesting both easy and hard training improve it. Fuelling also plays a part.
5. The practical case for the long easy ride rests here. It is plausible and supported by observation, but not yet proven by trials. **Expected grade: C to B.**

The chapter should leave readers understanding why "fresh" numbers mislead, how to measure their own durability roughly with a power meter, and why the long ride is still a reasonable bet.
