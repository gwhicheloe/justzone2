## Definitions

- **Durability** (Maunder et al. 2021, [Abs]): the time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in physiological-profiling characteristics over time during prolonged exercise. Their argument: profiling is done on rested athletes, but the measured attributes shift during long exercise, which matters for pacing, training-load monitoring and predicting performance. Review/opinion paper with supporting field data. `@maunder2021`, status abstract.
- **Physiological resilience** (Jones 2024, [Abs]): Jones's term for the same family of ideas, proposed as a "fourth dimension" alongside VO₂max, efficiency and fractional utilisation. `@jones2024`, relevant sections read [FT] (published version, via Figshare).
- Terminology note: the field uses both words. Zanini, Jones & Nybo (2025) is a short piece on defining the two; abstract not available yet. **To do:** read it before writing the terminology section, then pick one term for the book and say why.

## The size of the fade, and how much riders differ

- **The range, resolved.** Jones 2024 [FT, p. 4]: across the three Clark studies (2018, 2019a, 2019b) critical power fell about **10% on average** after 2 h of heavy-intensity cycling, with individual falls of **0.4% to 32%** (the abstract rounds this to "<1% to ~32%"). Jones & Kirby 2025 [FT] give the same finding as "1% to 33%". **Use "from under 1% to about a third", cite Jones 2024.** The range is pooled individual data reported by Jones; it does not appear as a stated range in the Clark abstracts. Jones & Kirby add that the size of the fall was not correlated with VO₂max or fresh critical power [FT]. The participants were not elite, which both papers flag.
- **Clark et al. 2018** (MSSE 50:1658–1668) [Abs]: the first of the series. Study 1 (6 cyclists): the 3-minute test after 2 h gave repeatable results (273 against 276 W; r = 0.99). Study 2 (9 cyclists): end-test power **306 W fresh, 282 W after 2 h (8% lower)**; W′ estimate **18.3 against 14.7 kJ (20% lower)**. `@clark2018`
- **Clark et al. 2019a** (J Appl Physiol 127:726–736) [FT, accepted manuscript]: **16 men**, age 34 ± 6, VO₂peak **52.5 ± 7.3 mL/kg/min**; competitive but not elite (two runners, six cyclists, four triathletes, three CrossFit athletes, one squash player). 3-minute all-out test fresh and after 40 min, 80 min and 2 h of "heavy" cycling. End-test power (their estimate of critical power) **260 W fresh, 268 W after 40 min, 260 W after 80 min, 236 W after 2 h (9% lower, significant)**. W′ **17.9 kJ fresh, 16.1 after 40 min (not significant), 14.7 after 80 min, 13.8 after 2 h** (the discussion gives these as falls of 10%, 18% and 23%). The two parameters deteriorate on different time courses: W′ falls early, critical power holds for about 80 minutes and then drops. `@clark2019a`
  - **Worth telling readers:** "heavy" here was only **164 ± 28 W**, a quarter of the way from the gas exchange threshold (132 W) to fresh critical power, and about 63% of VO₂peak at the start. That is just above the top of Zone 2 for these riders, not a hard ride. Over the 2 h the same power drifted from **64% to 68% of VO₂peak** and heart rate rose [FT, Results].
- **Clark et al. 2019b** (Am J Physiol 317:R59–R67) [FT, accepted manuscript]: **14 men**, age 31 ± 10, VO₂peak 54.7 ± 5.4 mL/kg/min; 2 h heavy-intensity cycling, water only. Critical power **~11% lower** (287 W fresh against 256 W fatigued, the same by the 3-minute test and by conventional time-to-exhaustion trials). W′ **18.7 kJ fresh; 14.6 kJ (3-minute test, 22% lower) or 15.3 kJ (conventional, 17% lower)**; the abstract's "~20%" is the middle of these. The same power cost more oxygen as the ride went on: **65% → 72% of VO₂peak**, with respiratory exchange ratio falling 0.86 → 0.79 (more fat, less carbohydrate burned: 1.77 → 1.17 g/min carbohydrate, 0.68 → 1.08 g/min fat). Muscle glycogen fell about 65% (639 → 226 mmol/kg dry weight). The fall in glycogen did **not** correlate with the fall in critical power (r = 0.19, p = 0.52) or W′ (r = 0.07). Nor did the rise in oxygen cost correlate with either. Only 5 of 14 could ride 30 minutes just *below* their fatigued critical power. `@clark2019b`
  - **Authors' own caveat [FT, Experimental Considerations]:** a biopsy samples a small part of the working muscle, so the missing correlation does not rule glycogen out. With n = 14 a real but moderate relationship could easily be missed too (our point, for the Reading the research box). **So the text must say "not explained by glycogen in these data", not "nothing to do with glycogen".**
  - Clark 2019a adds a pooled analysis of 28 participants [FT]: glycogen *level* after 2 h correlated with fatigued critical power (r = 0.43) and the percentage fall in glycogen with the fall in W′ (r = 0.44), but the *change* in glycogen still did not correlate with the change in critical power (r = 0.09 to 0.11). Weak-to-moderate correlations; mixed picture.

## Thresholds move too: does your Zone 2 shift during a long ride?

- **Stevenson et al. 2022** (Eur J Appl Physiol 122:2673–2682; open access, PMC9488873) [Abs]: 14 trained cyclists and triathletes (13 men, 1 woman; VO₂peak 59.9 ± 6.8 mL/kg/min). Step test before and after **2 h at 90% of VT1 power** (a proper Zone 2 ride). Power at the first threshold fell: **VT1 217 → 196 W** (p < 0.0001), lactate-based **212 → 190 W** (p = 0.004), roughly 10%. Part was lost efficiency (about 8 W), the larger part a lower rate of energy expenditure at the threshold (about 14 W). The **heart rate at the threshold rose: 142 → 151 bpm** (VT1), 140 → 150 bpm (lactate). `@stevenson2022`
  - **Full text read 5 October [FT]:** training volume 9 ± 3 h/week; the 2 h averaged **194 ± 41 W**; fall in VT1 power **21 ± 12 W, 10.0 ± 5.8%**, individual range about **9 to 44 W**; heart rate at VT1 up **6.3%**; heart rate during the 2 h at constant power up **8.2 ± 2.7%**, with oxygen uptake up 3.4% (p = 0.08, not significant). Riders were **fasted overnight and drank only water**: the authors expect a smaller shift when fed and taking carbohydrate. No control (rest) trial; sample-size target was 15 (one dropout). The fall in threshold was not related to peak fat oxidation, VO₂peak, sweat loss or dehydration.
  - **For the book:** this is the durability finding closest to the reader. After two hours, the top of Zone 2 sits about 20 W lower and about 9 bpm higher. A rider holding fixed power drifts towards (or over) the threshold; a rider holding fixed heart rate sees power fall. Directly relevant to the app's heart-rate targeting, but the app is not mentioned outside the intro. Also belongs in Finding your zones and Knowing it's working. Jones 2024 [FT, p. 4] describes the same study as a fall of "the order of 10%" with large individual differences.

## Professional cyclists: durability separates levels

- **Mateo-March et al. 2022** (IJSPP 17:926–931) [Abs]: 112 professional men (46 ProTeam, 66 WorldTour), data 2013–2021. Maximal mean power for 10 s to 120 min, fresh and after 15, 25, 35 and 45 kJ/kg of work. All values declined progressively: **1.6% to 3.0% lower after 15 kJ/kg, 6.0% to 9.7% lower after 45 kJ/kg**. WorldTour riders declined less (after 45 kJ/kg: **4.7% to 8.8% WorldTour against 7.6% to 11.6% ProTeam**). Fresh values didn't differ consistently between the two levels; the differences appeared under fatigue, mostly for efforts of 5 minutes or longer. `@mateomarch2022` *Candidate for the figure: fresh vs fatigued declines by level, if the full text gives per-duration numbers.*
- **Muriel et al. 2022** (Eur J Sport Sci 22:1797–1804) [Abs]: 8 WorldTour and 7 ProTeam riders in the 2020 Vuelta. Fresh, WorldTour riders were only better for 30-minute efforts in week 1; after 35 kJ/kg the gap widened to 30 s, 1, 5, 20 and 30-minute efforts. Overall durability differences reached significance only when analysed by week. Repeatability (number of efforts above 95% of maximal mean power) didn't differ. Small sample. `@muriel2022`
- **Leo et al. 2024** (IJSPP 19:545–549) [Abs]: 49 men (11 under-23, 13 ProTeam, 24 WorldTour). Work done **above critical power**, from 0 to 7.5 kJ/kg. Under-23 riders' power for efforts of 1 minute or longer dropped significantly after 5 kJ/kg above critical power; ProTeam and WorldTour riders' power didn't drop significantly until 7.5 kJ/kg. They call this "high-intensity durability". `@leo2024`
- **Spragg et al. 2023a** (MSSE 55:133–140) [Abs]: 10 young professional riders (age about 19, VO₂max about 74 mL/kg/min). Critical power measured fresh and after five 8-minute efforts at 105–110% of critical power. The change in critical power correlated with relative peak power (r = 0.89), relative VO₂max (r = 0.84), relative power at VT1 (r = 0.83) and VT2 (r = 0.74), and gross efficiency at 300 W (r = 0.87); negatively with carbohydrate oxidation at 200 W (r = −0.70). Very small sample; correlations this high in n = 10 need a caution in the text. `@spragg2023a` *Candidate for "one study, up close": small enough to explain fully, and its weaknesses are instructive.*
- **Spragg et al. 2023b** (Eur J Sport Sci 23:489–498) [Abs]: 30 under-23 professionals across a season. Fatigued profile = best 2, 5 and 12-minute power produced after 2,000 kJ of work. The fatigued profile varied more across the season than the fresh one and was lowest late in the season. Time below VT1 correlated with improvements in fatigued 2-minute power (r = 0.43 absolute, 0.38 relative); a shift towards a polarised distribution correlated with improvements in fatigued 12-minute power (r = 0.41). Observational: correlation, not proof that easy training causes durability. `@spragg2023b`

- **Gallo et al. 2022** (IJSPP 17:1094–1102) [Abs]: 53 male climbers (15 junior, 21 under-23, 17 professional), power data 2016–19. In under-23s and professionals, the higher-ranked riders lost less power after 20 to 50 kJ/kg of work. Between the best under-23s and the best professionals, the fade was the *only* measure that differed: professionals declined less in 1, 5 and 20-minute power. (Several of the reported p values are above 0.05, up to 0.099: effect sizes are large but not all comparisons are significant. Say so if used.) Jones 2024 leans on this study. `@gallo2022`
- **Voet et al. 2024** (IJSPP 19:809–819) [Abs]: 16 male semi-professionals tested three times in a season, fresh and after about 38 kJ/kg. One-minute power fell when fatigued; 10-minute power did not. Fatigued one-minute power improved more across the season than fresh. Closer to good amateurs than the WorldTour data, and a caution: at this level and this much prior work, 10-minute power held up. `@voet2024`
- **Leo 2024 full text:** the Universidad Europea repository lists the paper but does not release the file to the public (checked 5 October). Abstract only unless George can get it. Added to `papers-wanted.md`.

## What trains it

- **Matomäki et al. 2023** (Front Physiol 14:1128111) [FT]: randomised; 35 sedentary or recreationally active adults (16 men, 19 women) did 10 weeks of either low-intensity training (**6.8 h/week** on average) or high-intensity training (**1.6 h/week**). Durability tested with 3 h of cycling at 48% of pre-training VO₂max, from the size and onset of drifts in energy expenditure, heart rate, perceived exertion, ventilation and cardiac measures, plus "physiological strain". Durability improved similarly in both groups (no group difference, p = 0.42). Low-intensity group: average drift 7.7% → 6.3% (p = 0.09), onset 106 → 131 min (p = 0.08), strain improved (p = 0.01). High-intensity group: drift 8.8% → 5.4% (p = 0.03), onset 108 → 137 min (p = 0.03). VO₂max rose only in the high-intensity group. Authors' own limitations: nutrition not controlled during training, reliability of drift measures unknown, not powered for sex differences. **For the book:** the only randomised training study found so far; untrained people, low test intensity. It suggests both kinds of training help, at very different time costs, and that the effects were modest. Grade B at most for "training improves durability", grade C for "long easy rides specifically do". `@matomaki2023`
- **Jones & Kirby 2025** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 35:e70032) [FT, PMC11872681]: narrative review, and candid about its evidence. Their own words for the state of knowledge are that data are "scant" and that they rely partly on "anecdote and conjecture"; they state that no longitudinal study has yet set out to find the best training for resilience. What they suggest, with what each rests on: `@joneskirby2025`
  - *Years of consistent volume.* Rests on cross-sectional comparisons: professionals against under-23s (Gallo 2022), trained against untrained runners (Unhjem 2024), faster against slower runners (Zanini 2024, running). Cause and effect can't be separated: the authors say as much about the Zanini comparison. Proposed mechanism (speculative): slow conversion of type II to type I fibres over years.
  - *The long session.* Zanini 2024 (running): among runners matched for VO₂max and 10 km time, those who regularly ran longer than 90 minutes lost less running economy over a 90-minute run (**3.1% against 6.0%** rise in oxygen cost) [Rep: figure as given by Jones & Kirby; check the paper before using]. Cross-sectional, running. The nearest thing to direct support for the long ride, and it is observational.
  - *Hard efforts inside long sessions, or progressive long sessions.* Kipchoge's training is the example. The authors say outright that specific evidence is lacking.
  - *Heavy strength training.* The best-controlled evidence in the review: Rønnestad 2011 (below), Øfsteng 2018 (skiers), an unpublished thesis in runners.
  - *Interval training.* One study (Christensen 2024) in which adding three interval sessions a week at the same volume lowered strain during, and improved a time trial after, 2 h of moderate cycling. Not read; candidate.
  - Also: Matomäki 2023 summarised as "any type of endurance training develops resilience compared to remaining sedentary". Fuelling during exercise listed as a factor (Clark 2019a). Sex: women appear to fade less in marathons (11.7% against 15.6% second-half slowing; Deaner 2015), which may be pacing rather than physiology.
  - *Caution on attributions:* `lookup.py fulltext` strips reference numbers, so the studies named above were matched to claims from the reference list and context. Check each against the PMC page before citing a study by name via this review.
  - **For the book:** this review is the field's leading voice saying the long-ride case is inference. It supports the brief's grade (C to B) and George's chosen line. Don't quote the Kipchoge material beyond a sentence: the book is about cyclists.
- **Unhjem 2024** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 34:e14637) [Abs]: 10 trained runners against 10 active adults, 1 h at 70% of VO₂max. Relative intensity drifted up **2.6%** in the trained group against **8.3%** in the active group (p = 0.001): oxygen cost rose less and attainable VO₂max held in the trained runners. Cross-sectional, running, n = 20. `@unhjem2024`
- **Rønnestad et al. 2011** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 21:250–259) [Abs]: 20 well-trained cyclists; 11 added heavy strength training twice a week for 12 weeks, 9 continued endurance training only ("assigned": the abstract does not say randomised; check before calling it a randomised trial). After 185 minutes of easy riding (44% of maximal aerobic power), 5-minute all-out power rose **371 → 400 W** in the strength group, no change in controls; oxygen cost, heart rate and lactate in the last hour fell more in the strength group. `@ronnestad2011`
  - **For the book:** an intervention study in trained cyclists showing late-ride power improving, and the intervention was the gym, not the long ride. Worth a paragraph in "what trains it": it complicates the simple story usefully. Strength training itself belongs in another chapter (Special cases or The training week): note there.

## Fuelling

- **Clark et al. 2019a** [Abs]: with **60 g/h carbohydrate**, end-test power after 2 h was **254 W** against **236 W** on placebo (fresh 260 W): carbohydrate cancelled most of the drop in critical power but didn't protect W′ (13.5 kJ). [FT] confirms: the fall in muscle glycogen was the same with carbohydrate (238 mmol/kg) and placebo (254 mmol/kg), though blood glucose and carbohydrate oxidation late in the ride were higher with carbohydrate. The authors' reading is that the benefit came through blood glucose rather than spared muscle glycogen. W′ was 24% down with carbohydrate, 22% with placebo. Drink: 60 g/h, against a taste-matched placebo; same 16 men as above.
- **Norte et al. 2026** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 36:e70326) [FT]: randomised, fully counterbalanced crossover; 16 trained cyclists and triathletes (15 men, 1 woman; age 35 ± 9; VO₂max 51.8 ± 6.8 mL/kg/min; training >5 h/week). Diet standardised for 24 h (8 g/kg carbohydrate) plus a set pre-ride meal. 3 h at 95% of gas exchange threshold (moderate intensity), then a 3-minute all-out test. Critical power **277 W fresh; after 3 h: 236 W on water, 257 W with 60 g/h, 266 W with 120 g/h** (maltodextrin and fructose). Our arithmetic: drops of about **15%, 7% and 4%**. All three significantly below fresh; each step of carbohydrate significantly better than the one below. W′ fell similarly in all conditions. `@norte2026`
  - **For the book:** strong support for George's "out-fuel the group" habit, from riders much like the book's readers. Note it's one study, mostly men, and drink-based carbohydrate in a lab. Grade B.

## Mechanisms (for "How it works"), as far as the sources read so far go

From Jones 2024 [FT, pp. 4–5] and Jones & Kirby 2025 [FT]. Each is a candidate explanation; the reviews say the determinants are "not resolved" / "elusive".

1. **Rising oxygen cost.** The same power costs more oxygen as a long ride goes on (Clark: 64% → 68% and 65% → 72% of VO₂peak over 2 h). Since critical power is the highest power at which oxygen uptake can still settle, a higher oxygen cost per watt lowers it by definition.
2. **Fuel shift.** As carbohydrate runs down, more fat is burned, and fat needs more oxygen per unit of ATP. Jones 2024 estimates the fall in respiratory exchange ratio in Clark 2019b (0.86 → 0.79) would raise oxygen uptake by about **1.7%**; Jones & Kirby estimate about **30%** of the rise in oxygen uptake over 2 h (pooled, 36 participants) comes from the change in fuel. So fuel shift explains a minority.
3. **Fibre recruitment.** Fibres used first (mostly type I) run low on glycogen or tire; fibres higher in the recruitment order (type II), which cost more oxygen and ATP per unit of force, are brought in. Sources cited by the reviews: Krustrup 2004 (glycogen-depleting slow fibres raised fast-fibre activity and oxygen uptake during moderate cycling), Vøllestad & Blom 1985, Gollnick 1973. **Not read; candidates.** This is the bridge to George's question of why easy training might help (more, better-equipped type I fibres), and it is inference.
4. **Oxygen delivery.** Sweat loss lowers blood volume, skin competes with muscle for blood flow, core temperature rises, heart rate drifts up; VO₂max and thresholds can fall. (González-Alonso 2012; Coyle 1998, cited by Jones.) George's 37 °C room belongs here.
5. **Glycogen itself**, see the Clark findings above: clearly involved in fatigue during long exercise (Bergström 1967 is the classic), but individual falls in critical power were not predicted by individual falls in glycogen.
6. Clark 2019b found the rise in oxygen cost did **not** correlate with the fall in critical power either. So neither of the two obvious explanations accounts for who fades most. That open question is a good, true thing to tell readers.

Worked example to adapt (Jones & Kirby [FT]): a 7% rise in oxygen cost together with a 3% fall in VO₂max takes a ride from 75% to 83% of VO₂max without the power changing. Use our own numbers and wording.

Field measure for "What to do with it": heart rate decoupling. Smyth et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:2283–2295; open access) [Abs]: **82,303 recreational marathon runners**; the ratio of heart rate (% of maximum) to speed (relative to critical speed) was on average **16% higher at 35–40 km than at 5–10 km**, first detectable at **25 km**; the third of runners with the least decoupling finished faster (217 against 239 minutes for the highest third). Men decoupled more than women. Observational, running, and heart rate drift also reflects heat and dehydration (Jones 2024 makes this caveat). `@smyth2022`

## Added while drafting sections 4 to 7 (5 October 2026)

- **Norte 2026, further [FT] detail** (PMC13282445): Liverpool John Moores University. 18 recruited, 2 withdrew. Trials at least 6 days apart; six orderings, fully counterbalanced. The 3 h ride averaged **169 ± 14 W** (GET 178 W) and came to **24 ± 3 kJ/kg**. Falls in critical power as reported: **14.7%, 7.4%, 4.2%** (water, 60, 120 g/h). 60 against water: 20 W (p < 0.001); 120 against 60: 9 W (p = 0.006). W′ 15.5 kJ fresh; 12.0, 13.4, 12.7 kJ after (no differences between drinks). % of VO₂max reached in the final test: 90%, 94%, 97%. Oxygen cost, gross efficiency and relative intensity did not change with the drinks. Gut symptoms mild throughout; cumulative scores, fullness, cramps and regurgitation higher at 120 g/h than on water. Drinks were unflavoured maltodextrin and fructose (1:0.8) against plain water; **the paper describes no blinding**. Product supplied by Peak Supps; funding from the Portuguese Foundation for Science and Technology (first author's doctoral grant). Authors' limitation: the 3-minute test's fixed resistance, set fresh, may have understated the fall. They also cite Dudley-Rode et al. (60 g/h cut the fall in the first threshold from about 6% to about 3% after 150 min at 90% VT1): not read, candidate.
- **Zanini et al. 2024** (MSSE 56:2230–2240) [Abs]: 44 male runners, 90 min at the first lactate threshold. Oxygen cost rose **2.4%** in the faster subgroup against **4.5%** in the slower (p < 0.01). The long-run-habit comparison (3.1% against 6.0%) appears only in Jones & Kirby's account; the draft mentions it without the numbers. `@zanini2024`
- **Sánchez-Jiménez et al. 2026** (Eur J Appl Physiol 126:163–179; open access) [Abs]: systematic review, 21 studies. High-intensity prior work cuts power more per kilojoule than easy work: declines of **10% to 20% after 2.5 to 15 kJ/kg** of high-intensity work. Kilojoules alone are a poor measure of fatigue. `@sanchezjimenez2026`
- **Christensen 2024: do not use as Jones & Kirby describe it.** The abstract [Abs] reports 18 elite cyclists doing 7 weeks of intervals with maintained or reduced volume; the time trial after a 2 h preload was **not** significantly improved (p = 0.19). That does not match "improved time trial performance immediately following a 2 h bout". Either the attribution (inferred from the reference list) is wrong or the review is generous. Left out of the draft.
- **Our arithmetic, used in the draft and labelled as such there:**
  - 45 kJ/kg for a 70 kg rider = 3,150 kJ = about 4.5 h at 195 W. (A professional averaging 250 W gets there in about 3.5 h, so the draft avoids saying "the fifth hour".)
  - r = 0.43 squared = 0.18.
  - 95% confidence intervals by Fisher's transformation: r = 0.89, n = 10 → about 0.59 to 0.97; r = 0.74, n = 10 → about 0.21 to 0.93; r = 0.19, n = 14 → about −0.38 to 0.65.
  - 260 W less 0.4% is about 1 W less (draft says 2 W for "under 1%"); less 32% is 83 W less.
- **Claims in the draft that are our reasoning, not a source's:** the "opportunity" weakness of power-file studies (riders only make maximal late efforts when the race calls for it); "below about 30, treat a striking correlation as a reason for a bigger study"; "set power 10 to 15 W under the top of the zone on rides over two hours". Each is worded as George's view. **Check the first against the Mateo-March and Leo full texts if they arrive: the authors may address it.**
