## 175 kilometres alone

The final stage of the 2026 Tour de Luxembourg ran 177 km from Mersch to the Limpertsberg district of Luxembourg City. Mathieu van der Poel rode clear in the opening kilometres and stayed clear to the line. He said afterwards that it hadn't been the plan. He had found himself alone and assumed the chasers would come back. With 45 km to go, when the bunch swept up the riders who had been pursuing him, he still had three and a half minutes in hand, and he won by 1:04 from Mikkel Honoré [@skodatour2026].

Tadej Pogačar has made a habit of the same thing from shorter range. At Strade Bianche in March 2024 he attacked on the Monte Sante Marie gravel sector with 81 km left and won by 2:44. At the World Championships in Zürich that September he rode the last 50 km alone and held off Ben O'Connor by 34 seconds [@tourmagazin2025].

It is tempting to read these rides as proof of a bigger engine, but a result on its own can't prove that. Van der Poel's lead came down by almost two and a half minutes in the last 45 km [@skodatour2026]. That could be a rider fading or a bunch finally racing for the finish, and nothing public tells us which. His power in the first hour and the last hasn't been released, so nobody outside his team can say how much he faded.

What the rides do is pose a question that the model in Chapter 1 leaves open. VO₂max, the fraction of it you can sustain, and efficiency are all measured on a rider who is fresh. Races are decided four or five hours in. How much of what you had at the start do you still have at the end?

Most of us meet that question well below the level of a World Championship.

> **From the saddle.** In 2025 I rode a tour of the Pyrenees. On the fourth day I was really struggling, while one of my friends in particular seemed to be getting stronger. He's an ex-professional golfer and spends a lot of his time on the golf course.

I can't tell you why he got stronger while I got weaker, and the research can't yet either. Most of it concerns a single long ride, and little of it reaches a fourth day in a row. But the thing I was short of that day now has a name and a growing set of measurements behind it.

## What durability is, and what to call it

In 2021 a group led by Ed Maunder in Auckland argued that physiologists had been profiling athletes in the wrong state. Stephen Seiler, whose work on training intensity runs through this book, was among the authors. Lab tests are done rested. The qualities those tests measure shift during long exercise, and they shift by different amounts in different people. Maunder and his colleagues proposed the word *durability* for this, and defined it as the time of onset and the size of the deterioration in an athlete's physiological characteristics during prolonged exercise [@maunder2021].

Three years later Andrew Jones, the Exeter physiologist whose lab worked with Nike's two-hour marathon project, made the stronger claim. He argued that this quality belongs in the Joyner and Coyle model as a fourth factor alongside the original three, and he called it *physiological resilience*: the ability to resist functional decline under stress [@jones2024]. The two terms describe the same thing. This book uses durability, because that is the word cyclists and coaches have adopted.

The idea is easier to hold once you see how it is measured. There are three main ways.

The first is to test a rider fresh, tire them, and test them again. The clearest examples come from Jones's own lab. In one study, 16 men did a three-minute all-out test fresh, and again after 40 minutes, 80 minutes and two hours of steady riding [@clark2019a]. The test estimates critical power, the boundary described in Chapter 1 above which effort can't be sustained, and W′, the small reserve of work available above it. Critical power was 260 W fresh, 268 W after 40 minutes and 260 W after 80 minutes. After two hours it was 236 W, a fall of 9%. W′ went sooner: it was down 18% by 80 minutes and 23% by two hours. So the reserve you draw on for attacks and short climbs drains first, and the power you can hold for an hour stays put for a surprisingly long time and then drops.

![Critical power and W′ in 16 riders, tested fresh and after 40, 80 and 120 minutes of steady riding. Each is shown as a percentage of its fresh value. Drawn from the results of Clark and colleagues [@clark2019a].](figures/cp-decline.svg)

Two details of that study deserve more attention than they usually get. The riders were competitive amateurs with an average VO₂peak of 52.5 mL/kg/min, much like the readers of this book and nothing like van der Poel. And the two-hour ride that cost them 9% of their critical power averaged 164 W, which began at about 64% of their VO₂peak [@clark2019a]. That is a steady endurance ride just above the top of Zone 2. Nobody was being broken.

Riders differed far more than the average suggests. Across three studies from the same lab, the fall in critical power after two hours averaged about 10%, but individual riders ranged from under 1% to about a third [@jones2024]. {grade B} On a fresh critical power of 260 W, that is the difference between losing a watt or two and losing more than 80.

The second way to measure durability needs no lab. Researchers with access to professional riders' power files look for each rider's best efforts over set durations, then look again at the best efforts produced after a given amount of work has already been done that day. The work is counted in kilojoules per kilogram of body weight, the same kilojoules a bike computer shows. For a 70 kg rider, 45 kJ/kg is 3,150 kJ, roughly four and a half hours at an average of 195 W. Most of what is known about durability in professionals comes from this method, and a later section looks at what it shows.

The third way is to watch what drifts during a long steady effort: heart rate climbing at constant power, or the oxygen cost of that power creeping up. This is the version you can observe on your own rides, and the thresholds that define your training zones drift too.

In 2022 Maunder's group took 14 trained cyclists and triathletes, people training about nine hours a week, and measured the power at their first ventilatory threshold (VT1), which marks the top of Zone 2. Then the riders did two hours at 90% of that power, an average of 194 W, and the threshold was measured again [@stevenson2022]. It had fallen from 217 W to 196 W, a drop of 10%. Individual falls ranged from about 9 W to 44 W. The heart rate at the threshold had moved the other way, from 142 to 151 bpm.

After two hours of riding in Zone 2, then, the top of Zone 2 was 21 W lower and 9 bpm higher than the fresh test said [@stevenson2022]. A rider holding fixed power drifts towards the threshold from below. A rider holding fixed heart rate sees their power sink. One caution: the riders had fasted overnight and drank only water, and the authors expect the shift to be smaller in someone who has eaten and is taking in carbohydrate. There was also no control trial in which the riders simply sat for two hours between tests, although it is hard to see why sitting would lower a threshold by 10%. {grade B}

## How it works

The reviews of this field are candid that nobody can yet fully explain why two hours of steady riding lowers the power you can sustain [@jones2024; @joneskirby2025]. There are several candidate mechanisms, each with some evidence, and none of them yet explains why one rider loses 1% and another 30%. What follows is the current best account, with the gaps marked.

Start with what critical power is. Below it, oxygen uptake rises when you start riding and then settles. Above it, oxygen uptake keeps climbing until it reaches VO₂max and you have to stop. Critical power is the highest power at which uptake can still settle. It follows that anything which raises the oxygen cost of each watt, or lowers the amount of oxygen the body can deliver, will lower critical power, without anything having gone wrong in the usual sense of the word.

Both happen during a long ride.

Take the cost first. In the two-hour rides from Jones's lab, the power was held constant and oxygen uptake was not. In one study the same power went from 64% to 68% of VO₂peak over the two hours [@clark2019a]; in another, from 65% to 72% [@clark2019b]. The rider's computer shows the same number throughout while the body works progressively harder to produce it.

Part of this is fuel. As a ride goes on and carbohydrate stores run down, the muscles burn proportionally more fat. In the second of those studies, carbohydrate use fell from 1.77 to 1.17 grams a minute while fat use rose from 0.68 to 1.08 [@clark2019b]. Fat is the larger store by far, but it needs more oxygen than carbohydrate does to produce the same amount of ATP [@joneskirby2025]. Jones and his colleague Brett Kirby estimate that the change in fuel accounts for roughly 30% of the rise in oxygen uptake over two hours. So fuel is part of the explanation and the smaller part.

A second contributor is which muscle fibres are doing the work. Chapter 1 introduced type I fibres, the slow, efficient ones. They are recruited first and do most of the work at moderate power. As those fibres tire or run low on glycogen, the nervous system has to call on fibres further up the recruitment order to keep the power constant. Those type II fibres can produce the force, but they use more ATP and more oxygen to do it [@joneskirby2025]. The power on the screen is unchanged while a more expensive set of fibres is producing a growing share of it. The reviews favour this as the explanation for much of the rest of the rising oxygen cost. It is well supported as a general principle of how muscle is recruited. How much of the fall in critical power it accounts for in any particular rider has not been measured.

Delivery is the other side. Over a long ride you lose fluid as sweat, which lowers blood volume. Your core temperature rises, and blood is diverted to the skin to shed heat, where it competes with the working muscles. The heart fills a little less with each beat and beats faster to compensate. This is cardiovascular drift, the slow rise in heart rate at constant power that everyone who rides with a heart rate monitor has seen. In the Auckland study heart rate rose 8.2% over two hours at constant power [@stevenson2022]. If the drift goes far enough, the maximum amount of oxygen the heart can deliver falls, and VO₂max, the thresholds and critical power can fall with it [@joneskirby2025].

Heat makes all of this worse, and it is the mechanism I have felt most directly.

> **From the saddle.** My power dropped massively during the heatwave of 2026, when the training room was 37 degrees.

Riding indoors in the heat is taken up properly in a later chapter. The point here is that part of what feels like fading legs is the circulation.

Then there is glycogen. The oldest explanation for fading late in a ride is that the muscles run out of stored carbohydrate, and nobody doubts that glycogen matters in long exercise. What Jones's lab found is that it doesn't neatly explain the fall in critical power. They took muscle biopsies from 14 men before and after two hours of riding. Glycogen fell by about 65% and critical power by about 11%, but the riders who lost the most glycogen were not the riders who lost the most critical power: the correlation between the two was 0.19, which is close to none [@clark2019b].

That result is often repeated as "it's not about glycogen", which goes further than the data. The authors themselves point out that a biopsy samples a tiny piece of one muscle, and that a real relationship could be hidden by that alone. A study of 14 people is also too small to detect a relationship of moderate size reliably, a problem the Reading the research box in this chapter returns to. The fair summary is that in these data, glycogen loss did not predict who faded most. The same study found that the rise in oxygen cost did not predict it either. The two most obvious explanations both fail to account for the differences between riders, and the question is open. {grade C}

Carbohydrate during the ride is a clearer story. When the riders in the earlier study repeated the two-hour ride drinking 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour, their critical power afterwards was 254 W, against 236 W on a placebo and 260 W fresh [@clark2019a]. Carbohydrate removed most of the fall. It did so without sparing muscle glycogen, which dropped by almost the same amount in both trials. Blood glucose and the rate of carbohydrate burning late in the ride were higher with the drink, and the authors' reading is that a steady supply of glucose from the blood kept the working fibres going. Fuelling gets its own section later in this chapter, with a larger and more recent trial.

Put together, the picture is of a rider whose watts become more expensive as the hours pass, whose oxygen supply slowly tightens, and whose fuel is shifting towards the kind that costs more oxygen to burn. Each change is modest. They compound, because critical power sits where supply and cost meet.

This also suggests why training might improve durability, and here the chapter moves from evidence to inference. If the trouble starts when type I fibres tire and more costly fibres take over, then anything that lets type I fibres carry the load for longer should delay it: more capillaries around them, more mitochondria inside them, a greater capacity to burn fat. Those are among the adaptations to endurance training described in Chapter 2. It is a plausible chain of reasoning, and it is the reasoning behind the long easy ride. Whether training actually changes durability, and which training, is a question for trials, and the rest of this chapter looks at how few there are.

### Durability in the wild

It would be satisfying to say that van der Poel or Pogačar has exceptional durability, and plenty of commentary does. Here is what can be known from outside.

The results give distances, times and gaps. For the Luxembourg stage that means 177 km, a lead of 3:30 with 45 km left and 1:04 at the line [@skodatour2026]. They don't give power. A rider's speed on a long climb can be turned into an estimate of power, but the estimate rests on a guess at his weight, the wind and how long he sat in someone's slipstream.

The published science has the opposite limitation. The largest study of professional power files covers 112 riders and reports them as two anonymous groups [@mateomarch2022]. It can tell you what WorldTour riders as a class do after 45 kJ/kg of work, and nothing about any one of them.

That is why this chapter makes no claim about van der Poel's physiology or Pogačar's. Their rides are here because they are what durability looks like from the roadside. Everything the chapter concludes comes from riders who were measured.

## What the professionals show

Professional cyclists are the best-studied group in this field, for a practical reason: they race with power meters, their teams keep every file, and a season of racing contains maximal efforts made at every stage of fatigue.

The study with the most riders comes from a Spanish group led by Manuel Mateo-March, using data collected between 2013 and 2021 from 112 male professionals: 66 from the WorldTour, the sport's top division, and 46 from ProTeams, the division below [@mateomarch2022]. For each rider they found the best power ever recorded for durations from 10 seconds to two hours. Then they found the best power for the same durations among efforts made after 15, 25, 35 and 45 kJ/kg of work that day.

Power fell at every duration as the work mounted up. After 15 kJ/kg the losses were between 1.6% and 3.0%, depending on the duration. After 45 kJ/kg they were between 6.0% and 9.7% [@mateomarch2022].

The comparison between divisions is what made the study widely cited. Fresh, the WorldTour riders and the ProTeam riders were hard to tell apart: their best power did not differ consistently. After 45 kJ/kg the WorldTour riders had lost 4.7% to 8.8% and the ProTeam riders 7.6% to 11.6%, with the gap showing mainly in efforts of five minutes and longer [@mateomarch2022]. On these figures, what separates the top division from the second is less what riders can do in the first hour than what they can still do in the last.

[FIGURE: best power by duration, fresh and after 45 kJ/kg, WorldTour against ProTeam, redrawn from Mateo-March 2022. Waits on the full text.]

Other groups have found the same pattern lower down the ladder. Among 53 climbers studied by Gabriele Gallo and colleagues, the fade after 20 to 50 kJ/kg was the only measure that separated the best professionals from the best under-23 riders, although not every comparison in that study reached statistical significance [@gallo2022]. During the 2020 Vuelta a España, eight WorldTour and seven ProTeam riders were compared week by week. Fresh, the WorldTour riders were better only over 30 minutes. After 35 kJ/kg they were better over 30 seconds, one minute, five minutes, 20 minutes and 30 minutes [@muriel2022]. That study is as close as the research comes to my fourth day in the Pyrenees, and with 15 riders it is a small one. {grade B}

Before taking these numbers home, it helps to see how far away they are. In a lab study of trained amateurs described later in this chapter, three hours at 169 W came to 24 kJ/kg [@norte2026]. The professionals' 45 kJ/kg is nearly double that. It describes the closing stages of a long professional race, a state few amateurs ever ride themselves into.

The method has a weakness, and it is worth understanding because power-file studies are becoming common. The "fatigued" figures are the best efforts a rider happened to make late in a ride. Nobody asked him to make them. A rider produces a maximal five-minute effort after 45 kJ/kg only if the race puts him in a position where it is worth doing, which means he is in the front group on the last climb. A domestique who has finished his work for the day rides in at whatever pace gets him to the bus. Better riders are more often in the finale, so they have more chances to record a high number late on. Some of the gap between divisions may be opportunity. This is my own reading of the method, and I have not seen the size of the effect estimated.

A second complication is that kilojoules are a blunt measure of how tired someone is. Two thousand kilojoules ridden steadily in the bunch and 2,000 that include repeated attacks are different days. A group led by Peter Leo counted only the work done above critical power and found that in under-23 riders, power for efforts of a minute or longer fell significantly after 5 kJ/kg of it, while professionals held on until 7.5 kJ/kg [@leo2024]. A 2026 systematic review of 21 studies reached the same conclusion more generally: hard work does more damage per kilojoule than easy work, with losses of 10% to 20% reported after as little as 2.5 to 15 kJ/kg of high-intensity riding [@sanchezjimenez2026]. For anyone comparing their own files, the lesson is to compare like with like. Power after three steady hours and power after a three-hour chain gang are answers to different questions.

Semi-professionals look somewhat different again. A Dutch-led group tested 16 of them three times across a season, fresh and after about 38 kJ/kg. One-minute power fell when they were tired. Ten-minute power did not [@voet2024]. That sits awkwardly beside the professional data, where the differences showed mainly in longer efforts, and I don't have an explanation for it beyond the obvious ones: a different level of rider, a lab protocol in place of race files, and 16 people.

Whether durability can be read off an ordinary lab test is disputed. In the amateur riders tested in Jones's lab, the fall in critical power was unrelated to VO₂max or to critical power when fresh [@joneskirby2025]. In 10 young professionals studied by James Spragg and colleagues, the change in critical power after a set of hard efforts correlated very strongly with VO₂max, threshold power and efficiency, with correlations between 0.74 and 0.89 [@spragg2023a]. Ten riders is a very small group for claims of that kind, and the Reading the research box below explains why those numbers deserve less trust than they seem to. For now the fair statement is that nobody knows whether durability is a separate quality or mostly a reflection of being fit.

## What trains it

This is the part of the chapter with the weakest evidence, and the people who know the field best say so. Jones and Kirby's 2025 review, the most thorough attempt to answer the question, describes the data as "scant" and states that no long-term study has yet set out to find the training that best improves durability [@joneskirby2025]. What exists is one 10-week training trial in beginners, one study of strength training in trained cyclists, and a set of comparisons between groups: trained against untrained, faster against slower, professionals against under-23s.

Take the comparisons first. Trained runners drift less than active adults who don't run over an hour at the same relative intensity: in one study of 20 people the relative intensity rose by 2.6% in the trained group and 8.3% in the others [@unhjem2024]. Faster runners lose less running economy over 90 minutes than slower ones, 2.4% against 4.5% in a study of 44 men [@zanini2024]. The same study, as Jones and Kirby describe it, found that among runners of similar ability, those who regularly ran for longer than 90 minutes held their economy better than those who didn't [@joneskirby2025]. Professionals fade less than under-23s. Each comparison points the same way, and none can separate cause from effect. People who are naturally durable may find long sessions easier and so do more of them.

Spragg's group came closer by following 30 under-23 professionals through a season. Riders who spent more time below the first threshold improved their two-minute power when fatigued, and riders whose training became more polarised improved their fatigued 12-minute power [@spragg2023b]. The correlations were around 0.4. A correlation of that size is loose, and it is still observation: nobody was assigned to ride more easy hours.

The one trial that assigned people to easy or hard riding comes from Finland. Pekka Matomäki and colleagues took 35 sedentary or recreationally active adults and gave them 10 weeks of either low-intensity riding, averaging 6.8 hours a week, or high-intensity intervals, averaging 1.6 hours a week [@matomaki2023]. Before and after, each did a three-hour ride at an easy fixed power while the researchers tracked how much heart rate, energy cost and other measures drifted, and when the drift began.

Durability improved in both groups, and by about the same amount. In the low-intensity group the average drift went from 7.7% to 6.3% and its onset moved from 106 to 131 minutes, changes that fell just short of statistical significance. In the interval group the drift went from 8.8% to 5.4% and the onset from 108 to 137 minutes, both significant [@matomaki2023]. There was no significant difference between the groups.

Read with care, that trial says three things. Training of either kind makes beginners more durable. Four times the hours of easy riding bought no more durability than the intervals did. And the changes were small. It can't tell you what happens in a rider with five years of training behind them, or over a year instead of 10 weeks, and the reviews' main suggestion is precisely that durability comes from volume accumulated over years [@joneskirby2025]. A 10-week trial cannot test that idea. {grade B} for the claim that training improves durability in untrained people.

The study in trained cyclists was of strength training. Bent Rønnestad's group in Norway had 11 well-trained cyclists add heavy leg strength training twice a week for 12 weeks while nine carried on with endurance training alone [@ronnestad2011]. Afterwards each rode for just over three hours at an easy pace and then did a five-minute all-out effort. The strength group's power in that effort rose from 371 W to 400 W. The control group's didn't change. It is one study of 20 riders, but it is an intervention in trained cyclists, measured late in a long ride, and the thing that worked was heavy weights. Strength training is covered in a later chapter. It belongs here as a reminder that the long ride is not the only candidate.

That leaves the long easy ride. The case for it is the mechanism described earlier, the cross-sectional comparisons, and Spragg's season of data. Against it stands nothing. No trial has shown that long rides fail to improve durability. There is simply no trial in trained riders that has tested them. I think the long ride is a reasonable bet. I would not tell you it has been shown to work. {grade C}

Fuelling is a different matter, because there the trials exist.

> **From the saddle.** One thing I try to do is out-fuel the riders I'm riding with. I can be a bit of a greedy pig, but that often helps keep me at the front of the group and gives me a strong finish in sportives.

The study from Jones's lab described earlier found that 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour removed most of the fall in critical power over two hours [@clark2019a]. A larger and more recent trial tested whether more is better.

### One study, up close: carbohydrate dose and critical power

In 2026 a group at Liverpool John Moores University recruited 16 trained cyclists and triathletes, 15 men and one woman, with an average age of 35 and an average VO₂max of 51.8 mL/kg/min. All trained more than five hours a week [@norte2026]. They are a fair match for the readers of this book.

Each rider's critical power was measured fresh with a three-minute all-out test. Then, on three separate days at least six days apart, each rode for three hours at 95% of the power at their gas exchange threshold, which is the top of Zone 2 by another name. It averaged 169 W [@norte2026]. Straight afterwards they did the three-minute test again. On one day they drank only water, on another a drink supplying 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour, and on the third 120 grams an hour, as a mix of maltodextrin and fructose. The order was randomised and balanced, so that any effect of doing the rides first or last was spread evenly across the three conditions. This is a crossover design: every rider is compared with themselves, which removes the differences between people that swamp small studies.

The riders were well fed going in. For 24 hours before each ride they ate a supplied diet containing 8 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight, and they had a set breakfast [@norte2026]. Nobody started short of glycogen.

Fresh, critical power averaged 277 W. After three hours on water it was 236 W, a fall of about 15%. With 60 grams an hour it was 257 W, a fall of about 7%. With 120 grams an hour it was 266 W, a fall of about 4%. Each step was statistically significant. W′, the reserve above critical power, dropped by a similar amount whatever the riders drank [@norte2026].

![How much critical power 16 trained riders had lost after three hours at 169 W, by what they drank. Drawn from the results of Norte and colleagues [@norte2026].](figures/carb-dose.svg)

Three things in the details matter. The first step did most of the work: going from nothing to 60 grams an hour saved 20 W, and doubling it saved 9 W more. Stomach complaints were mild at every dose, though fullness and cramping scored higher at 120 grams than on water. And during the three-hour rides themselves, oxygen cost and efficiency did not differ between the drinks, so the carbohydrate was not working by making each watt cheaper. The riders on the highest dose could simply get closer to their VO₂max in the final test [@norte2026].

What the study can't tell us is just as specific. The paper describes no attempt to disguise which drink was which, so the riders presumably knew when they were on water, and a three-minute all-out test depends on how hard someone is willing to go. Fifteen of the 16 were men. Carbohydrate was supplied by a supplement company, although the funding came from a public research foundation. Critical power was estimated from a single short test, and the authors themselves think the method may have understated the fall. And it measured one all-out effort after one ride. So it shows carbohydrate protecting late-ride power on the day, and leaves open whether fuelling well in training makes you more durable in the long run. {grade B}

### Reading the research: correlation and small samples

Two studies in this chapter reported correlations, and they show two different ways a correlation can mislead.

Spragg's season-long study found that riders who spent more time below the first threshold improved their fatigued two-minute power, with a correlation of 0.43 [@spragg2023b]. Squaring a correlation gives the share of the variation it accounts for. Here that is 18%, which leaves 82% to everything else. Then there is the direction. Easy riding might build durability. Or riders who are already durable, or who are simply healthy and uninjured that year, might be the ones able to pile up easy hours. Or a third thing, such as a coach who prescribes both more volume and better fuelling, might drive both. An observational study can't choose between these, however carefully it is done. Only assigning riders to different training can.

Spragg's lab study found correlations as high as 0.89 between riders' lab measures and the change in their critical power [@spragg2023a]. That looks close to proof. It came from 10 riders. With a sample that small, the true value could plausibly lie anywhere from about 0.6 to 0.97, by the standard calculation of a 95% confidence interval. For the weakest of the reported correlations, 0.74, the plausible range runs from about 0.2 to 0.93. The same arithmetic applies in the other direction to the glycogen result earlier in the chapter. A correlation of 0.19 from 14 riders is compatible with a true value anywhere between about −0.4 and 0.65, which covers everything from a moderate negative relationship to a strong positive one [@clark2019b]. With 10 or 14 people, chance alone can produce a large correlation or hide a real one.

When a paper reports a correlation, look for the number of people before the size of r. Below about 30, treat a striking correlation as a reason to run a bigger study. Chapter 3 goes through the reasoning in full.

## What to do with it

Start by finding out how much you fade, because the range between riders is so wide that the average tells you little about yourself.

The simplest test needs a power meter and two rides. On the first, after a warm-up, ride as hard as you can for a fixed time: Spragg's group used two, five and 12 minutes [@spragg2023b], and five or 12 suits most amateurs. A week or so later, do the same effort at the end of a ride of two and a half to three hours at your usual endurance pace, eating and drinking as you normally would. The percentage you lose is a rough measure of your durability. Keep the route, the fuelling and the temperature as similar as you can, and expect the result to move by a few per cent from one attempt to the next for no reason at all.

You can also watch drift without a hard effort. On a long ride at steady power, compare your average heart rate in the last half hour with the half hour after you warmed up. In the Finnish trial, untrained people drifted by 5% to 9% over three hours at an easy pace [@matomaki2023]; in the Auckland study, trained riders who had not eaten drifted 8.2% in two [@stevenson2022]. Heat and dehydration push the number up on their own, so compare cool day with cool day.

For Zone 2 training, the Auckland result has a direct consequence. If you ride at the very top of your zone by power, the top comes down to meet you, and after two hours you may be above it. If you hold a heart rate ceiling set from a fresh test, your power will sink further than it needs to, because the heart rate at the threshold rises as you tire [@stevenson2022]. Neither method is right late in a long ride. My own rule of thumb is to set power 10 to 15 W under the top of the zone for rides over two hours, and to eat.

Eat on anything longer than about 90 minutes, which is roughly where critical power began to slide in Jones's lab [@clark2019a]. Sixty grams of carbohydrate an hour gets you most of the protection in the trials above. More helps a little further if your stomach accepts it, and that is worth practising in training before trying it in an event [@norte2026].

Then there is the long ride itself. Keep doing it. The mechanism is plausible, the comparisons of durable and less durable athletes point to accumulated volume, and nothing else on offer has better evidence apart from fuelling and, on one study, the weights room. But hold the belief at its proper strength. If the next few years bring a trial showing that an hour of intervals builds as much durability in trained riders as it did in the Finnish beginners, that should change your mind, and it will change this chapter.
