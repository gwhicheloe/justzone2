# Why elite athletes ride easy (Chapter 8): brief

Status: **written with the first draft on 11 October 2026, at George's request ("can you write the next chapter"). The argument has not been separately approved.**
Target: 6,000 words. Part II, *The easy part*. First draft: about 5,200 words. Nothing was padded; the question of how many hard sessions belongs to Chapter 13 and durability to Chapter 9.

## The question

Why do the best endurance athletes do most of their training easy, and does their example transfer to a rider on six to ten hours a week?

## The argument (to approve)

1. **The chapter's position, stated on its first page:** in the diaries of eleven Olympic and world champion skiers, hard training was a fixed dose, about 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold in two or three sessions, in every phase of the year, while the easy hours rose and fell between 15 and 10 a week (Tønnessen 2014, read in full). The same figure comes out of the 17-year record of one skier (Solli 2017). So the 80/20 ratio is a result of how much elite athletes train, and a rider with seven hours who copies the ratio copies the wrong thing. **This is my inference from two sets of diaries, and the draft says so.**
2. What the records agree on is "mostly easy" (91 to 95% of time in skiers and rowers). What they do not agree on is how the rest is split: most are pyramidal, and "polarised" describes a minority (Stöggl & Sperlich 2015).
3. Road cycling is the untidy case: 77 / 15 / 8 in professionals in season, 94 / 4 / 2 in a pursuit squad, a pyramid in 28 World Tour riders, and only 17 to 19% of time below the first threshold in one group of under-23 professionals. Counting method alone moves the answer a long way (Sanders 2017).
4. **The champions' easy training was almost all below Zone 2:** 86% of endurance time in zone 1 of the Norwegian scale against 5% in its Zone 2, which is this book's Zone 2. World-class runners do the same. The one cyclist with a public account, Pogačar, puts his long rides in Zone 2 at 320 to 340 W (added at George's request; news reports of a podcast). **The book's rule, stated the same way here and in Chapter 7: an easy ride should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions. On six to ten hours that is Zone 2; on 15 or more it is lower.** Grade C; my proposal, to confirm.
5. The pattern was found by coaches and athletes over decades, not by experiment: Lydiard, the Norwegian rowers of 1970 to 2001, the Dutch speed skaters, one skier's career. All observational, all confounded, and a medal table holds only the athletes the system suited (grade C).
6. Why it might work: a ceiling on hard training; easy hours as the only thing left to add; two signalling routes (a suggestion in reviews, untested). The claim that the middle should be avoided is the weakest part of the polarised idea: the Norwegian runners' model is built on it.
7. The trials: early small ones favoured polarised training (Esteve-Lanao 2007, Neal 2013, Stöggl & Sperlich 2014); pooled analyses went from a moderate effect in three trials (2019) to none in 17 (2024) and none in individual data from 13 (2025). In cyclists, 41 studies show no difference (Cove 2025). Grade B that the split of the hard work makes no measurable difference over 4 to 16 weeks; nothing is known beyond that.
8. For six to ten hours: fix the hard sessions first, keep the rest easy enough to repeat the week, ignore the ratio, and count a ride between the thresholds as a hard session (grade C).
9. The ending: elite practice supports a large volume of mostly easy training, and very little of it in Zone 2.

## Weak points

- **The position rests on diaries from 11 skiers and one more skier.** That hard time was steady is a fact of those records. That it marks a ceiling, and that the same dose suits a cyclist or an amateur, is not shown by anything.
- **"About 1.3 hours" is not interval time.** In the single skier's record it includes the recoveries between efforts, and in the champions' diaries the athletes assigned the minutes themselves. The draft says the first of these. The comparison with an amateur's two interval sessions is loose.
- **Only one of the main sources is about cyclists in any depth**, and the cycling records are the least consistent. Skiers and runners carry the argument.
- **Abstract only:** Seiler 2010, Guellich 2009 and 2010, Fiskerstrand 2004, Orie 2014, every cycling description except Leo 2020, and the trials of Esteve-Lanao, Neal, Ingham, Muñoz. **Rosenblat 2025 and Cove 2025, the two most important pooled analyses, are abstract only.**
- Four distributions (Lucía's professionals, the pursuit squad, Zapico's under-23s, the speed skaters) come from a review's table, not the papers.
- The two 2022 papers with opposite titles have not been read. The draft quotes only their titles.
- Seiler's 350 and 750 hours a year is second-hand.
- The explanation for why cycling is untidy (races and groups set the pace) is mine.
- The reverse-causation reading of the junior cyclists is mine.
- "A track event that lasts about four minutes" has no source in the list.
- The history of running training is from one review.
- **The rule for how hard an easy ride should be is reasoning.** No trial compares the top of the easy range with the bottom, and the skiers' and runners' practice is the main evidence against it. My explanation of why they differ (hours, sessions, fuel) is untested.
- **Pogačar's figures are news reports of a podcast, read through a summarising tool.** I have not heard the audio. One report (not read, behind a login) appears to give the terrain the other way round. What his team means by Zone 2 is not stated.
- Living people named: Seiler, Stöggl, Sperlich, Esteve-Lanao and Bakken, each as the author of a paper, with nothing said beyond what the papers say; and Tadej Pogačar, at George's request (11 October), for what he said about his own training in an interview. Nothing is claimed about his physiology. The podcast's host is named in the reference list and not in the text. The skier in Solli 2017 and the runners in Casado 2023 are identifiable from the description.
