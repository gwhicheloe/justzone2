# Chapter 8: Why elite athletes ride easy

Eleven Norwegian cross-country skiers and biathletes, each the winner of at least one individual Olympic or world championship gold medal, kept a diary of every training session in the year that led to the best result of their careers. Researchers at the Norwegian Olympic Federation later added the diaries up. The athletes had trained for 770 hours each on average, in 470 sessions, which is about 15 hours a week across the whole year. Of the time they spent on endurance training, 91% was below the first lactate threshold [@tonnessen2014].

Records like these are where the advice to train mostly easy comes from, and they are usually read as a recipe: four easy sessions to one hard one, or 80/20. I think that reading takes the wrong number from the diaries. In every phase of the year the same athletes logged about 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold, spread over two or three sessions, and that figure hardly moved while their easy training rose to 15 hours a week in summer and fell to 10 in the racing season [@tonnessen2014]. The hard training looks like a fixed dose, of a size within an amateur's reach. The easy training is whatever a full-time athlete has the hours and the recovery to add on top. If that is right, the ratio is a consequence of how much the champions train, and a rider with seven hours a week who copies it has copied the wrong thing.

That is an inference from diaries, and the evidence carries it only part of the way.

## What the records show

Stephen Seiler, whose 2006 study of junior skiers opened Chapter 5, summarised the descriptive studies in 2010. Athletes of national and international standard who trained 10 to 13 times a week converged, he wrote, on about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% built around hard work such as intervals. They appeared to "self-organize" towards a high volume of training with hard sessions applied carefully [@seiler2010]. Chapter 5 explained that the 80 is a count of sessions. Counted in minutes the same training comes out nearer 90 or 95% easy, because most of a hard session is warm-up, recovery and cool-down {shadow @sylta2014}. The eleven champions show both figures at once: 77% of their sessions were easy, and 91% of their time [@tonnessen2014].

From here on a distribution is written as three percentages of training time: below the first threshold, between the thresholds, and above the second. The champions' was 91 / 3 / 5.

Other sports show the same first number. Thirty-six German junior rowers of national and international standard rowed at 95 / 2 / 3 over a 37-week season [@guellich2009]. The most successful female cross-country skier in the history of the sport, a Norwegian with six Olympic gold medals, trained at 92 / 3 / 5 across her five best years [@solli2017]. World-class distance runners cover at least 80% of their distance at low intensity all year round {shadow @haugen2022}.

Where the records disagree is in how the remainder is split. A distribution with more time above the second threshold than between the thresholds is called polarised, and one with more between them is called pyramidal. When Thomas Stöggl and Billy Sperlich reviewed the published records in 2015 they found that most described a pyramid. Polarised distributions turned up in three studies of cross-country skiers and one of marathon runners [@stoggl2015]. The name that became attached to elite training describes a minority of the elite training on record.

Road cycling is the untidy case. Thirteen professional cyclists followed through a season rode at 88 / 11 / 2 in the rest period, 78 / 17 / 5 before the racing began and 77 / 15 / 8 during it [@lucia2000jjp; @stoggl2015]. Eight professionals who wore heart rate monitors through 22 stages of a Tour de France spent the race at 70 / 23 / 7 [@lucia1999]. A study of 28 riders from the sport's top division, published in 2025, found a pyramid in every phase of the season {shadow @mateomarch2025}. At the other extreme is the German squad that broke the world record for the 4,000-metre team pursuit in 2000, a track event that lasts about four minutes. Its riders covered 29,000 to 35,000 km a year, with road stage races as the heaviest blocks, at 94 / 4 / 2 [@schumacher2002; @stoggl2015].

The records of 30 under-23 professionals do not fit the pattern at all. Through a whole season their heart rates were below the first ventilatory threshold for 17 to 19% of their riding time, and between the thresholds for 51 to 63% [@leo2020]. The authors offered three explanations: that the coaches wanted it so, that the riders rode their easy sessions too hard, or that the zones had been set against the wrong heart rates. I can't choose between them from the paper.

Part of the untidiness comes from the counting. Fifteen well-trained road cyclists had the same ten weeks of training sorted three ways. By heart rate it was 87 / 9 / 4. By power it was 80 / 9 / 12. By the riders' own rating of each session it was 45 / 30 / 25 {shadow @sanders2017}. The rest, I suspect, comes from the sport. A skier or runner training alone chooses the pace, and a cyclist in a road race or a group has it chosen for them.

The diaries also record how easy the easy training was. The Norwegian federation's scale has five zones, and Chapter 5 placed its zones 1 and 2 both below the first threshold. In the champions' version zone 1 ran up to 73% of maximum heart rate, with blood lactate under 1.2 mmol/L, and Zone 2 from 74 to 83%, with lactate of 1.3 to 2.0 mmol/L. That Zone 2 is the band this book calls Zone 2, the upper part of easy. The champions spent 86.0% of their endurance training time in zone 1 and 5.3% in Zone 2. In the summer preparation phase that was 14.3 hours a week against 0.9 [@tonnessen2014].

Runners at the top do the same. Most of their low-intensity running is in the lowest zone of the scale used to describe it, and a typical easy run is 3 to 5 km/h slower than the athlete's marathon pace {shadow @haugen2022}.

For professional cyclists I have found no record that divides easy riding into its lower and upper parts. The nearest thing is Tadej Pogačar's account of himself. In September 2024, as the reigning Tour de France champion, he gave a long podcast interview about his training [@attia2024]. As it was reported, he put his Zone 2 at 320 to 340 W, said that on flat roads he will ride in it for five hours, and added that by the end of a ride like that the power has slipped to about 300 W [@hansen2024; @idlprocycling2025]. He weighed about 65 kg at that year's Tour [@attia2024], which puts his Zone 2 at roughly 5 W/kg. The 14 trained cyclists and triathletes of Chapter 9, who rode about nine hours a week, reached their first threshold at 217 W {shadow @stevenson2022}. He judges the zone, he said, from heart rate, power, speed and how he feels [@attia2024].

That is an interview and not a training record, and his Zone 2 is whatever his team's testing says it is. Taken at face value, it has the Tour de France champion doing his long rides in Zone 2, where the skiers do theirs below it.

Chapter 7 set this book's rule for an easy ride: it should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions, which for a rider on six to ten hours a week means Zone 2. I think the skiers and the cyclist are both following it. In summer the skiers fitted 15 easy hours a week around their hard sessions, in ten sessions, working their arms as well as their legs [@tonnessen2014], and an hour near the top of the easy range burns carbohydrate far faster than an hour lower down {shadow @gollnick1974}. For them the intensity that leaves the hard sessions untouched is a low one. Pogačar's account suggests that on a bike a professional can make five hours of Zone 2 a regular part of his week. A rider with seven hours, and a day or more between hard sessions, has more room than either and gives up work by riding lower. Nothing in these records tests that reading, and the skiers' figures come from diaries in which the athlete assigns each part of a session to a zone. {grade C}

## How the pattern came about

Nobody designed it in a laboratory. A 2023 review of a century of distance-running training concluded that most of what runners do was arrived at by trial and error, and little of it by experiment [@casado2023]. From the 1930s to the 1950s the leading runners trained on intervals: Emil Zátopek ran sessions of 40 or 60 repetitions of 400 metres. In the 1960s the New Zealand coach Arthur Lydiard argued against the hard interval programmes and built his runners' preparation on 10 to 12 weeks of mostly easy continuous running, aiming at 100 miles a week. His athletes Peter Snell and Murray Halberg won four Olympic gold medals between them from 1960 to 1964. By the 1970s and 1980s many international runners had combined Lydiard's volume with interval sessions, on a pattern of hard days and easy days usually credited to the Oregon coaches Bill Bowerman and Bill Dellinger [@casado2023].

Rowing left numbers. Twenty-eight Norwegian rowers won international medals between 1970 and 2001, and 21 of them answered a detailed questionnaire about how they had trained. Between the 1970s and the 1990s the time the medallists spent training at low blood lactate went from 30 hours a month to 50. Their training at race pace and above went from 23 hours a month to about 7. Total volume rose by 20%, from 924 to 1,128 hours a year. Over the same period the medallists' VO₂max rose by 12%, from 5.8 to 6.5 litres a minute, and their six-minute ergometer performance by almost 10% [@fiskerstrand2004].

Dutch speed skaters made the same move without the extra hours. Across ten Olympic cycles from 1972 to 2010 the programmes of the medal winners went from 40 / 40 / 20 to about 80 / 12 / 8, with no trend in the total hours trained [@stoggl2015]{shadow @orie2014}.

The skier with six gold medals went through it in a single career. Between the ages of 20 and 35 her training rose from 522 hours a year to 940. Early in her senior career she trained with long blocks of hard intervals, at about 88 / 2 / 10, improved quickly, and after a few years stopped improving. Her next improvement came with less hard training and much more easy training, at about 92 / 3 / 5, and it led straight into her five best seasons [@solli2017].

A squad of 51 German junior track cyclists gives a comparison between riders. Over 15 weeks of basic preparation, 17 of them raised their power at a fixed blood lactate by 11% and 17 lost 7%. The two groups had covered the same total distance. The improvers had ridden more of it at low intensity, and the decliners more of it around the threshold [@guellich2010].

All of this is observation, and it points one way: athletes and squads did better as their training became bigger and easier. It cannot show that the training was the cause. Over the same decades altitude camps were added to the rowers' winters [@fiskerstrand2004], and equipment, coaching and the number of people competing for a seat will have changed as well. A medal table also holds only the athletes a training system suited. The rower who broke down on 1,100 hours a year is not among the 28. And in the cycling squad the arrow could run either way: a junior who was losing fitness that winter for some other reason would have found the same speed harder, and more of his riding would have registered as threshold work. {grade C}

## Why it might work

The first explanation is that hard training has a ceiling, and the champions' diaries show where it was for them. Their time above the first threshold came to 1.3 hours a week in the summer preparation phase, 1.3 in the autumn and 1.4 in the racing season. It was done in two sessions a week, rising to three before a championship, and over the year it made about 100 sessions and 63 hours. Their easy training in the same three phases was 15.2, 14.4 and 9.8 hours a week [@tonnessen2014].

![Hours are the means of eleven athletes, from the training diaries analysed by Tønnessen and colleagues [@tonnessen2014]. Easy is zones 1 and 2 of the Norwegian five-zone scale and hard is zones 3 to 5. The percentages and the bottom row are my arithmetic.](figures/champions-week.svg)

The single skier's records have the same shape. Her time between the thresholds and above the second, added together, came to 5.6, 5.4 and 5.7 hours a month in the three phases of her year, which is again about 1.3 hours a week, while her easy training went from 76 hours a month in summer to 55 in the racing season [@solli2017]. Her figures count the recoveries between intervals as hard time, so the efforts themselves were shorter than that.

Chapter 6 gave one reason a ceiling should exist. In Seiler's highly trained runners, heart rate variability was back to normal 5 to 10 minutes after an easy session and about 30 minutes after a harder one, and in less trained people the harder sessions took far longer to recover from {shadow @seiler2007}. Seiler's 2010 review found no convincing evidence that giving already well-trained athletes more hard training produced lasting gains [@seiler2010]. One group in the trial described later in this chapter did 27 interval sessions in nine weeks, gained less than a group that did 14 alongside far more easy training, and lost 3.7% of its body weight on the way [@stoggl2014].

Once the hard training is at its ceiling, the only training left to add is easy, which was Chapter 7's argument. What the added hours do is less certain. They raise the total training load, which was the best predictor of mitochondrial content in the largest analysis of the question {shadow @molmen2025}. They may build the things that change over years and that short studies miss, the capillaries and the heart of Chapter 2 and the durability of Chapter 9. Reviews of elite training have also suggested that long easy sessions and short hard ones reach the muscle by different routes, the calcium signal of many thousands of contractions and the energy signal of a few very hard minutes, so that a programme with both gets more than either gives alone [@casado2023]{shadow @laursen2010}. Nobody has shown that they add up in the way the suggestion needs. {grade C}

The polarised idea makes a second claim, which is that training between the thresholds should be kept small because it costs as much to recover from as harder training and achieves less. Seiler's runners support the first half: a session between the thresholds took the same 30 minutes to recover from as intervals above the second {shadow @seiler2007}. The records give the second half little support. Most elite distributions are pyramids [@stoggl2015]. Eight world-class Norwegian sprint skiers did more low-intensity and more moderate-intensity training than eight of national class {shadow @sandbakk2011}. And the model followed by the Norwegian runners who have lately won Olympic and world titles is built on the middle. It has three or four interval sessions a week run at a blood lactate of 2 to 4.5 mmol/L, checked with a meter during the session, and one session near VO₂max, inside 150 to 180 km of running. Marius Bakken, the 5,000-metre runner who worked the method out on himself in the late 1990s, is one of the authors of the paper that describes it. His reason was that holding lactate down let him do much more fast running in a week than harder intervals had allowed [@casado2023].

In 2022 the journal *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* printed two papers back to back. One, with Seiler among its authors, was titled "Polarized Training Is Optimal for Endurance Athletes" [@foster2022]. The other was titled "Polarized Training Is Not Optimal for Endurance Athletes" [@burnley2022].

The pyramid, the polarised week and the Norwegian threshold model share a very large volume of easy training and a hard part controlled closely enough to be repeated every week for years. Where exactly the hard part sits looks to me like the smaller choice. {grade C}

Selection is the remaining explanation. These are full-time athletes who train eight to ten times a week [@tonnessen2014], the men among the champions had a VO₂max of 85 mL/kg/min [@tonnessen2014], and the skier did 18 to 25% of her training at altitude camps [@solli2017]. She also took 12 years to build from 522 hours to 940, and the authors of her case study think the long build-up is what let her absorb the load [@solli2017]. What suits a person selected for the work and prepared for it over a decade is weak evidence for what suits anyone else.

## What the trials found

No trial has assigned Olympic champions to a training programme. Stöggl and Sperlich wrote that neither elite athletes nor their coaches like to have training altered for an experiment [@stoggl2014], and the trials that exist used club and national-level athletes for periods of six weeks to five months.

An early one was published in 2007. Jonathan Esteve-Lanao and colleagues randomised 12 sub-elite runners to five months of training that differed only below the second threshold. Both groups did about 8% of their training time above it. One group did about 80 / 12 / 8 and the other 67 / 25 / 8. Both ran a 10.4 km cross-country time trial faster afterwards, the easier group by 157 seconds and the other by 122, a difference that reached statistical significance {shadow @estevelanao2007}.

A trial in cyclists followed in 2013. Twelve trained men did two six-week blocks, one polarised at 80 / 0 / 20 on 6.4 hours a week and one threshold-heavy at 57 / 43 / 0 on 7.5 hours a week, in random order and with four weeks of detraining between. Their 40 km time trial improved after both blocks. Peak power rose by 8% after the polarised block against 3%, power at the lactate threshold by 9% against 2%, and time to exhaustion at a hard fixed power by 85% against 37% [@neal2013].

The paper's title says that polarised training has a greater impact on endurance than the three alternatives. The full text supports something narrower.

The distributions are counts of sessions. The polarised group is described as 68 / 6 / 26, which means that 14 of its 54 sessions were interval sessions. Each held 16 minutes of hard effort. By my arithmetic from the paper's table, that is under four hours of intervals in 104 hours of training, less than 4% of the time [@stoggl2014].

The groups did not train for the same number of hours, and the polarised group trained for 38 hours more than the interval group over the nine weeks [@stoggl2014]. Only the comparison with the high-volume group is free of that difference.

That comparison has a problem of its own. Before the trial every athlete had been training the high-volume way, with at most two threshold sessions a week, and none had been doing intervals regularly. The high-volume group therefore carried on as before, and the authors say that this may be why it did not improve. The polarised group kept its volume and was given something new. What the trial shows most securely is that adding two interval sessions a week to a high-volume programme raised VO₂peak in athletes who had not been doing intervals. It does not show that a ratio was responsible.

Nothing was raced, and the authors list the absence of a time trial as a limitation.

Four of the seven athletes who dropped out came from the threshold group, and among those who stayed in it the change in VO₂peak ran from a gain of 4% to a loss of 20% [@stoggl2014]. A fifth of a trained athlete's VO₂peak does not disappear in nine weeks of training three threshold sessions a week. Illness or a bad test is a likelier explanation, and in a group that had lost four of its members one such result moves the average a long way.

The headline number is also very large. The authors themselves wrote that an 11% rise in nine weeks in athletes of this standard "has to be put in perspective" [@stoggl2014], and Chapter 4 explained why the largest results from the smallest groups are the ones most likely to shrink when the experiment is repeated.

Later trials found less. Fourteen national-class rowers trained for 11 weeks at 94 / 1 / 6 or 93 / 3 / 2. Crews could not be split up, so the groups were not randomised. Power in a 2,000-metre ergometer test rose by 1.5% in both, which was not a significant change in either {shadow @treff2017}. Sixty well-trained runners spent 16 weeks on a pyramid, a polarised programme, or eight weeks of one followed by eight of the other, at matched training loads. Every group improved its 5 km time by between 0.6 and 1.7%. The group that went from pyramid to polarised did best by about half a percentage point, a margin the authors acknowledged was smaller than the test could reliably detect {shadow @filipas2022}. An older trial had found that over 12 weeks the hard work might not be needed at all: 18 experienced rowers were split between training entirely at or below the lactate threshold and doing 30% of their training hard, and the two groups improved their 2,000-metre time and their VO₂peak by the same amount {shadow @ingham2008}.

### Reading the research: an effect that shrank

The first pooled analysis of polarised training was published in 2019. Its authors found four randomised trials comparing polarised with threshold-heavy training in trained athletes, three of which measured a time trial and could be combined. Pooled, the polarised groups improved by 0.66 of a standard deviation more than the others, with a confidence interval from 0.15 to 1.17 [@rosenblat2019]. The trials it drew on had 112 athletes between them [@silvaoliveira2024].

Five years later a second group pooled 17 studies and 437 athletes. On time trials the difference between polarised training and everything else was 0.01 of a standard deviation, with an interval from −0.28 to 0.25, which is no difference. VO₂peak still favoured polarised training, by 0.24, with an interval that only just excluded zero. That advantage appeared in programmes shorter than 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes and nowhere else, and the authors pointed out that the highly trained athletes were in the short studies, so the two could not be told apart [@silvaoliveira2024].

In 2025 the author of the 2019 analysis returned to the question with the people who had run many of the trials, Seiler among them. They pooled the individual results of 348 athletes from 13 studies. Between polarised and pyramidal training they found no difference in VO₂max or in time trial performance, and no significant difference between polarised training and any other distribution [@rosenblat2025].

This is Chapter 4's simulation happening in real data. The first trials of a new idea are small, the small trials that get noticed are the ones that found something, and a first meta-analysis can only pool what exists. Nobody need have done anything wrong. When a training claim rests on a pooled result from three trials, the sensible reading is that the true effect lies at the low end of the interval or below it.

The same caution applies to what the later analyses found in subgroups. The 2025 paper reports that competitive athletes gained more VO₂max from polarised training and recreational athletes more from pyramidal [@rosenblat2025]. Chapter 4 listed the subgroup among the signs that a finding may not last, and this one has not yet been tested by a trial designed to look for it.

Cyclists have a pooled analysis of their own. Published in 2025, it took in 41 studies of trained cyclists, with 797 riders in 81 training groups. Training improved VO₂max and time trial performance. Programmes classed as polarised did no better than those that were not. Longer programmes did better than shorter ones, and the volume of training, by the week or in total, showed no relationship with the size of the gain [@cove2025].

Among programmes that are mostly easy and contain some hard work, no way of dividing the hard work has been shown to make athletes faster than another over 4 to 16 weeks [@silvaoliveira2024; @rosenblat2025; @cove2025]. {grade B} Whether one does better over years is unknown, because nobody has run the trial. {grade C}

## Six to ten hours a week

The athletes in the records train 15 to 20 hours a week [@tonnessen2014; @solli2017; @fiskerstrand2004]. Most readers of this book train less than half of that.

The trials closest to that reader are of recreational runners. Thirty of them trained for ten weeks at about 77 / 3 / 20 or 46 / 35 / 19. The first group improved its 10 km time by 5.0% and the second by 3.6%, a difference that was not statistically significant {shadow @munoz2014}. Thirty-eight more trained for eight weeks at 77 / 3 / 20 or 40 / 50 / 10, with the two programmes matched for training load. Both groups improved by similar amounts, and the group that did more of its running between the thresholds got there in 24.8 hours of training against 29.9 {shadow @festa2019}. The cyclists in the 2013 crossover were on six to eight hours a week and did better on the polarised block over six weeks [@neal2013]. The pooled analysis of cyclists found no difference [@cove2025]. Nothing here says the mix matters much at these volumes, and one trial says the threshold-heavy mix saves time.

The diaries suggest a different way to use the elite example, which is to copy the amount of hard training and ignore the ratio. The champions did about 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold, in two or three sessions [@tonnessen2014]. In a seven-hour week that much hard training is 19% of the time. For the champions in summer it was 8%, and a seven-hour rider who trained to their percentage would do about half an hour of hard riding a week, which is far less than the champions did themselves.

Seiler is reported to have seen the same thing across athletes. According to a 2025 review, his view is that athletes training about 350 hours a year, which is a little under seven hours a week, tend to show threshold-heavy distributions, and that polarised and pyramidal patterns appear above about 750 hours [@sun2025]. It is a description of what people do, reported at second hand, and no evidence about what works best.

The professional peloton offers a small natural comparison. Over four years, 10 professional women trained fewer hours than 20 professional men, spent less of their time in the low zones, and arrived at a similar total training load. The authors' reading was that the women made up for lower volume with higher intensity {shadow @vanerp2020}.

Volume still counts for those who are adapted to it. When 18 male professionals were confined to indoor trainers for seven weeks in 2020, their training fell by a third, to about 12 hours a week with 2 of those hours at high intensity. Most readers of this book would call that a training camp. Every one of them lost power over 5 and 20 minutes, by between 1% and 19% {shadow @muriel2021}. Those riders had also lost racing, the road and their routines, so the lost hours cannot be isolated. It is still a warning against reading this chapter as an argument that hours do not matter.

For a rider on these hours, the elite example says to decide the hard sessions first and let the easy riding, and the percentages, follow. Chapter 17 builds the week that way.

The best evidence that easy training matters comes from athletes who do 15 hours of it a week, and in the one set of diaries that divides it up, 86% of their training time was below Zone 2 and 5% was in it [@tonnessen2014]. Elite practice is evidence for a large volume of training, most of it easy, and beyond one rider's interview it lends little to Zone 2 in particular. The case for Zone 2 is the narrower one from Chapter 7: a rider with few hours gets the most from the easy ones by riding near the top of the range.
