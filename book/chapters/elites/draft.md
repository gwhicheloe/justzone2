# Chapter 8: Why elite athletes ride easy

Eleven Norwegian cross-country skiers and biathletes, each the winner of at least one individual Olympic or world championship gold medal, kept a diary of every training session in the year that led to the best result of their careers. Researchers at the Norwegian Olympic Federation later added the diaries up. The athletes had trained for 770 hours each on average, in 470 sessions, which is about 15 hours a week across the whole year. Of the time they spent on endurance training, 91% was below the first lactate threshold [@tonnessen2014].

These records are usually read as a recipe: four easy sessions to one hard one, or 80/20. I think that takes the wrong number from the diaries. The same athletes logged about 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold in every phase of the year, spread over two or three sessions. Their easy training rose to 15 hours a week in summer and fell to 10 in the racing season [@tonnessen2014]. The hard training looks like a fixed dose within an amateur's reach, with easy hours added around it. If that reading is right, a seven-hour rider should copy the hard dose rather than the ratio. The diaries alone cannot establish it.

## What the records show

Across sports, elite athletes do about four easy sessions for every hard one, and counted by time the easy share is higher still. Stephen Seiler, whose 2006 study of junior skiers opened Chapter 5, summarised the descriptive studies in 2010. Athletes of national and international standard who trained 10 to 13 times a week converged, he wrote, on about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% built around hard work such as intervals. They appeared to "self-organize" towards a high volume of training with hard sessions applied carefully [@seiler2010]. As Chapter 5 explained, that 80% is a share of sessions, not of time. Counted in minutes the same training comes out nearer 90 or 95% easy, because most of a hard session is warm-up, recovery and cool-down {shadow @sylta2014}. The eleven champions show both figures at once: 77% of their sessions were easy, and 91% of their time [@tonnessen2014].

From here on a distribution is written as three percentages of training time: below the first threshold, between the thresholds, and above the second. The champions' was 91 / 3 / 5.

Other sports show the same first number. Thirty-six German junior rowers of national and international standard rowed at 95 / 2 / 3 over a 37-week season [@guellich2009]. World-class distance runners cover at least 80% of their distance at low intensity all year round {shadow @haugen2022}.

Where the records disagree is in how the remainder is split. A distribution with more time above the second threshold than between the thresholds is called polarised, and one with more between them is called pyramidal. When Thomas Stöggl and Billy Sperlich reviewed the published records in 2015 they found that most described a pyramid. Polarised distributions turned up in three studies of cross-country skiers and one of marathon runners [@stoggl2015]. The name that became attached to elite training describes a minority of the elite training on record.

Road cycling doesn't fit so neatly: professional cyclists spend far more of their time between the thresholds than the 2 to 3% of the rowers and the champions in the figure. Thirteen professionals followed through a season spent 11 to 17% of their riding there [@lucia2000jjp; @stoggl2015], and eight who wore heart rate monitors through a Tour de France spent almost a quarter of the race there [@lucia1999].

The pattern holds in newer records too. A study of 28 riders from the sport's top division, published in 2025, found a pyramid in every phase of the season {shadow @mateomarch2025}. At the other extreme sits the German team-pursuit squad in the figure, at 94 / 4 / 2 for a track event that lasts about four minutes [@schumacher2002; @stoggl2015].

The 30 under-23 professionals in the figure's bottom row do not fit the pattern at all, with 17 to 19% of their riding time below the first ventilatory threshold and 51 to 63% between the thresholds [@leo2020].

Part of the difference comes from the counting. Fifteen well-trained road cyclists had the same ten weeks of training sorted three ways. By heart rate it was 87 / 9 / 4. By power it was 80 / 9 / 12. By the riders' own rating of each session it was 45 / 30 / 25 {shadow @sanders2017}. The rest, I suspect, comes from the sport. A skier or runner training alone chooses the pace, and a cyclist in a road race or a group has it chosen for them.

The diaries also show how easy the easy training was: most of it was below Zone 2. The Norwegian federation's scale has five zones, and Chapter 5 placed its zones 1 and 2 both below the first threshold. In the champions' version zone 1 ran up to 73% of maximum heart rate, with blood lactate under 1.2 mmol/L, and Zone 2 from 74 to 83%, with lactate of 1.3 to 2.0 mmol/L. That Zone 2 is the band this book calls Zone 2, the upper part of easy. The champions spent 86.0% of their endurance training time in zone 1 and 5.3% in Zone 2. In the summer preparation phase that was 14.3 hours a week against 0.9 [@tonnessen2014].

For professional cyclists I found no record separating the easy hours this way. Tadej Pogačar gave his own account in a September 2024 podcast interview, as the reigning Tour de France champion [@attia2024]. As it was reported, he put his Zone 2 at 320 to 340 W, said that on flat roads he will ride in it for five hours, and added that by the end the power has slipped to about 300 W [@hansen2024; @idlprocycling2025]. He weighed about 65 kg at that year's Tour [@attia2024], which puts his Zone 2 at roughly 5 W/kg. The 14 trained cyclists and triathletes of Chapter 9, who rode about nine hours a week, reached their first threshold at 217 W {shadow @stevenson2022}. He judges the zone, he said, from heart rate, power, speed and how he feels [@attia2024].

An interview is not a training record, and his Zone 2 is whatever his team's testing says it is. Taken at face value, it has the Tour de France champion doing his long rides in Zone 2, where the skiers do theirs below it.

Alistair Brownlee, the only triathlete to have won two individual Olympic titles, gave a similar account in September 2026, and his easy riding was much slower. As the episode's notes give it, he trained about 35 hours a week at his peak and up to 40, with 15 to 18 hours or more on the bike, of which only one or two were hard. His easy rides averaged about 200 W, from a rider with an FTP of over 400 W [@eriksson2026]. Those rides were at half his FTP or less, below the 56% of FTP at which Chapter 5 put the start of Coggan's level 2 {shadow @allencoggan2010}. His easy runs were at 5 minutes a kilometre, and his best 10 km works out at 2 minutes 51 a kilometre. Everyone who came to train with him, he said, was surprised by how slowly he went most of the time. He gave his reason too: to do "the maximum volume possible and protect the really hard sessions, the vast majority of that volume had to be as easy as possible" [@eriksson2026]. His test of an easy session was that he came back from it feeling better than when he set out.

I think the skiers, the triathlete and the cyclist are all following the same rule with different numbers. Chapter 7's rule for an easy ride is that it should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions, which for a rider on six to ten hours a week means Zone 2. In summer the skiers fitted 15 easy hours a week around their hard sessions, in ten sessions, working their arms as well as their legs [@tonnessen2014], and an hour near the top of the easy range burns carbohydrate far faster than an hour lower down {shadow @gollnick1974}. For them the intensity that leaves the hard sessions untouched is a low one, and Brownlee, on 35 hours, said as much. Pogačar's account suggests that on a bike a professional can make five hours of Zone 2 a regular part of his week. A rider with seven hours, and a day or more between hard sessions, has more room than either and gives up work by riding lower. Nothing in these records tests that reading, and the skiers' figures come from diaries in which the athlete assigns each part of a session to a zone. {grade C}

## How the pattern came about

A 2023 review of a century of distance-running training traced most practices to trial and error [@casado2023]. From the 1930s to the 1950s the leading runners trained on intervals: Emil Zátopek, the Czech Olympic champion, ran sessions of 40 or 60 repetitions of 400 metres. In the 1960s the New Zealand coach Arthur Lydiard argued against the hard interval programmes and built his runners' preparation on 10 to 12 weeks of mostly easy continuous running, aiming at 100 miles a week. His athletes Peter Snell and Murray Halberg won four Olympic gold medals between them from 1960 to 1964. By the 1970s and 1980s many international runners had combined Lydiard's volume with interval sessions, on a pattern of hard days and easy days usually credited to the Oregon coaches Bill Bowerman and Bill Dellinger [@casado2023].

Rowing left numbers. Twenty-eight Norwegian rowers won international medals between 1970 and 2001, and 21 of them answered a detailed questionnaire about how they had trained. Between the 1970s and the 1990s the medallists' easy training went from 30 hours a month to 50, and their training at race pace and above fell from 23 hours a month to about 7. Their total volume rose by a fifth. Over the same period their VO₂max rose by 12% and their six-minute ergometer performance by almost 10% [@fiskerstrand2004].

Dutch speed skaters made the same move without the extra hours. Across ten Olympic cycles from 1972 to 2010 the programmes of the medal winners went from 40 / 40 / 20 to about 80 / 12 / 8, with no trend in the total hours trained [@stoggl2015]{shadow @orie2014}.

All of this is observation, and it points one way: athletes and squads did better as their training became bigger and easier. It cannot show that the training was the cause. Over the same decades altitude camps were added to the rowers' winters [@fiskerstrand2004], and equipment, coaching and the number of people competing for a seat will have changed as well. A medal table also holds only the athletes a training system suited. The rower who broke down on 1,100 hours a year is not among the 28. {grade C}

## Why it might work

The champions' steady hard dose suggests a limit to how much hard training they could usefully do. Their two weekly hard sessions rose to three before a championship, about 100 sessions a year [@tonnessen2014].

![Hours are the means of eleven athletes, from the training diaries analysed by Tønnessen and colleagues [@tonnessen2014]. Easy is zones 1 and 2 of the Norwegian five-zone scale and hard is zones 3 to 5. The percentages and the bottom row are my arithmetic.](figures/champions-week.svg)

Recovery is the likely reason for the ceiling. In Seiler's highly trained runners of Chapter 6, heart rate variability was back to normal 5 to 10 minutes after an easy session and about 30 minutes after a harder one, and in less trained people the harder sessions took far longer to recover from {shadow @seiler2007}. Seiler's 2010 review found no convincing evidence that giving already well-trained athletes more hard training produced lasting gains [@seiler2010]. One group in the trial described later in this chapter did 27 interval sessions in nine weeks, gained less than a group that did 14 alongside far more easy training, and lost 3.7% of its body weight on the way [@stoggl2014].

Once the hard training is at its ceiling, the only training left to add is easy, which was Chapter 7's argument. What the added hours do is less certain, and the candidates are all long-term. They raise the total training load, which was the best predictor of mitochondrial content in the largest analysis of the question {shadow @molmen2025}. They may build the things that change over years and that short studies miss, the capillaries and the heart of Chapter 2 and the durability of Chapter 9. Reviews of elite training have also suggested that long easy sessions and short hard ones reach the muscle by different routes, the calcium signal of many thousands of contractions and the energy signal of a few very hard minutes, so that a programme with both gets more than either gives alone [@casado2023]{shadow @laursen2010}. Nobody has shown that they add up in the way the suggestion needs. {grade C}

The polarised idea makes a second claim, that training between the thresholds should be kept small because it costs as much to recover from as harder training and achieves less, and this one has less support. Seiler's runners support the first half: a session between the thresholds took the same 30 minutes to recover from as intervals above the second {shadow @seiler2007}. The records give the second half little support. Most elite distributions are pyramids [@stoggl2015]. And the model followed by the Norwegian runners who have lately won Olympic and world titles is built on the middle. It has three or four interval sessions a week run at a blood lactate of 2 to 4.5 mmol/L, checked with a meter during the session, and one session near VO₂max, inside 150 to 180 km of running. Marius Bakken, the 5,000-metre runner who worked the method out on himself in the late 1990s, is one of the authors of the paper that describes it. His reason was that holding lactate down let him do much more fast running in a week than harder intervals had allowed [@casado2023].

In 2022 the journal *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* printed two papers back to back. One, with Seiler among its authors, was titled "Polarized Training Is Optimal for Endurance Athletes" [@foster2022]. The other was titled "Polarized Training Is Not Optimal for Endurance Athletes" [@burnley2022].

The pyramid, the polarised week and the Norwegian threshold model share a very large volume of easy training and a hard part controlled closely enough to be repeated every week for years. Where exactly the hard part sits looks to me like the smaller choice. {grade C}

Selection is the remaining explanation. These are full-time athletes who train eight to ten times a week, and the men among the champions had a VO₂max of 85 mL/kg/min [@tonnessen2014]. What suits a person selected for the work is weak evidence for what suits anyone else.

## What the trials found

No trial has assigned Olympic champions to a training programme. Stöggl and Sperlich wrote that neither elite athletes nor their coaches like to have training altered for an experiment [@stoggl2014], and the trials that exist used club and national-level athletes for periods of six weeks to five months.

The one crossover trial in cyclists favoured polarised training. In 2013, twelve trained men did two six-week blocks, one polarised at 80 / 0 / 20 on 6.4 hours a week and one threshold-heavy at 57 / 43 / 0 on 7.5 hours a week, in random order and with four weeks of detraining between. Their 40 km time trial improved after both blocks. Peak power rose by 8% after the polarised block against 3%, power at the lactate threshold by 9% against 2%, and time to exhaustion at a hard fixed power by 85% against 37% [@neal2013].

### One study, up close: four ways to train for nine weeks

The largest of the early trials was run by Stöggl and Sperlich in Salzburg and published in 2014 [@stoggl2014].

The distributions are counts of sessions. The polarised group is described as 68 / 6 / 26, which means that 14 of its 54 sessions were interval sessions. Each held 16 minutes of hard effort. By my arithmetic from the paper's table, that is under four hours of intervals in 104 hours of training, less than 4% of the time [@stoggl2014].

The groups did not train for the same number of hours, and the polarised group trained for 38 hours more than the interval group over the nine weeks [@stoggl2014]. Only the comparison with the high-volume group is free of that difference.

That comparison has a problem of its own. Before the trial every athlete had been training the high-volume way, with at most two threshold sessions a week, and none had been doing intervals regularly. The high-volume group therefore carried on as before, and the authors say that this may be why it did not improve. The polarised group kept its volume and was given something new. What the trial shows most securely is that adding two interval sessions a week to a high-volume programme raised VO₂peak in athletes who had not been doing intervals. It does not show that a ratio was responsible.

Nothing was raced, and the authors list the absence of a time trial as a limitation.

Four of the seven athletes who dropped out came from the threshold group, and among those who stayed in it the change in VO₂peak ran from a gain of 4% to a loss of 20% [@stoggl2014]. A fifth of a trained athlete's VO₂peak does not disappear in nine weeks of training three threshold sessions a week. Illness or a bad test is a likelier explanation, and in a group that had lost four of its members one such result moves the average a long way.

The headline number is also very large. The authors themselves wrote that an 11% rise in nine weeks in athletes of this standard "has to be put in perspective" [@stoggl2014], and Chapter 4 explained why the largest results from the smallest groups are the ones most likely to shrink when the experiment is repeated.

### Reading the research: an effect that shrank

Three pooled analyses of polarised training have been published, each larger than the last.

::: trial
Question: Across the trials, does polarised training improve time-trial performance more than other ways of dividing the same training?
| | Studies pooled | Athletes | Time trials, polarised against the rest
| 2019 [@rosenblat2019] | Four randomised trials against threshold-heavy training, three of which measured a time trial | 112 [@silvaoliveira2024] | Better by 0.66 of a standard deviation, interval 0.15 to 1.17
| 2024 [@silvaoliveira2024] | 17 | 437 | 0.01 of a standard deviation, interval −0.28 to 0.25: no difference
| 2025, individual results [@rosenblat2025] | 13 | 348 | No difference from pyramidal training, and no significant difference from any other distribution
:::

The 2025 analysis was by the author of the 2019 one, working with the people who had run many of the trials, Seiler among them, and it found no difference in VO₂max either [@rosenblat2025]. The 2024 analysis still found VO₂peak favouring polarised training, by 0.24 of a standard deviation, with an interval that only just excluded zero. That advantage appeared in programmes shorter than 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes and nowhere else, and the authors pointed out that the highly trained athletes were in the short studies, so the two could not be told apart [@silvaoliveira2024].

Pooling three small trials doesn't remove the selection problem in Chapter 4. The trials most likely to be published are the ones with large effects, so an early meta-analysis can inherit their exaggeration. As more trials accumulated here, the time-trial advantage disappeared.

The same caution applies to what the later analyses found in subgroups. The 2025 paper reports that competitive athletes gained more VO₂max from polarised training and recreational athletes more from pyramidal [@rosenblat2025]. Chapter 4 listed the subgroup among the signs that a finding may not last, and this one has not yet been tested by a trial designed to look for it.

Cyclists have a pooled analysis of their own. Published in 2025, it took in 41 studies of trained cyclists, with 797 riders in 81 training groups. Training improved VO₂max and time trial performance. Programmes classed as polarised did no better than those that were not. Longer programmes did better than shorter ones, and the volume of training, by the week or in total, showed no relationship with the size of the gain [@cove2025].

Among programmes that are mostly easy and contain some hard work, no way of dividing the hard work has been shown to make athletes faster than another over 4 to 16 weeks [@silvaoliveira2024; @rosenblat2025; @cove2025]. {grade B} Whether one does better over years is unknown, because nobody has run the trial. {grade C}

The programmes shared a large amount of easy training with some hard work. The evidence gives little reason to choose between polarised and pyramidal versions of that mix.

## Six to ten hours a week

The athletes in the records train 15 to 20 hours a week [@tonnessen2014; @fiskerstrand2004]. Most readers of this book train less than half of that.

At an amateur's hours, the mix made little difference in the two trials that tested it, both in recreational runners. Thirty of them trained for ten weeks at about 77 / 3 / 20 or 46 / 35 / 19. The first group improved its 10 km time by 5.0% and the second by 3.6%, a difference that was not statistically significant {shadow @munoz2014}. Thirty-eight more trained for eight weeks at 77 / 3 / 20 or 40 / 50 / 10, with the two programmes matched for training load. Both groups improved by similar amounts, and the group that did more of its running between the thresholds got there in 24.8 hours of training against 29.9 {shadow @festa2019}. Nothing here says the mix matters much at these volumes, and one trial says the threshold-heavy mix saves time.

The better way to use the elite example is to copy the amount of hard training and ignore the ratio. The champions did about 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold, in two or three sessions [@tonnessen2014]. In a seven-hour week that much hard training is 19% of the time. For the champions in summer it was 8%, and a seven-hour rider who trained to their percentage would do about half an hour of hard riding a week, which is far less than the champions did themselves.

Volume still counts for those who are adapted to it. When 18 male professionals were confined to indoor trainers for seven weeks in 2020, their training fell by a third, to about 12 hours a week with 2 of those hours at high intensity. Most readers of this book would call that a training camp. Every one of them lost power over 5 and 20 minutes, by between 1% and 19% {shadow @muriel2021}. Those riders had also lost racing, the road and their routines, so the lost hours cannot be isolated. It is still a warning against reading this chapter as an argument that hours do not matter.

For a rider on these hours, the elite example says to decide the hard sessions first and let the easy riding, and the percentages, follow. Chapter 17 builds the week that way.

Elite practice lends little support to Zone 2 in particular: most of the champions' easy hours were below it [@tonnessen2014]. The case for riding near the top of the range remains Chapter 7's practical judgement for riders with fewer hours.
