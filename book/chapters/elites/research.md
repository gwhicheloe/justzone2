## 1. The descriptive record

### Tønnessen et al. 2014, *The road to gold* [FT]
- 11 Norwegian cross-country skiers and biathletes (4 men, 7 women), each with at least one individual Olympic or World Championship senior gold; the men had 41 golds between them and the women 25, 1985 to 2011. VO₂max 85 ± 5 (men), 73 ± 3 (women) mL/kg/min [Abs]. Authors at the Norwegian Olympic Federation, Oslo; Seiler is last author.
- The year leading to each athlete's most successful competition, from their own diaries (paper until about 2005, then digital), transferred session by session by the researchers. **Self-recorded.**
- 770 ± 99 h (622 to 942) in 470 ± 68 sessions (375 to 585). [Own] 770 / 52 = 14.8 h a week.
- Endurance 94% of training time, strength 5%, sprint 1%.
- By time in zone: 91 ± 1% of endurance time below the first lactate threshold (zones 1 and 2 of five), 9 ± 1% above it (zones 3 to 5).
- Five zones: 86.0 ± 3.4 / 5.3 ± 3.0 / 3.3 ± 0.9 / 3.3 ± 1 / 2.1 ± 1.0%. [Own] In the three-zone form: 91.3 below the first threshold, 3.3 between (zone 3), 5.4 above the second (zones 4 and 5).
- By session goal: 77 ± 2% easy sessions, 23 ± 2% hard.
- The scale (Table 2; "average self-reported zone cut-offs of 29 elite XC skiers"): zone 1 under 1.2 mmol/L, 54 to 73% of maximum heart rate; zone 2 1.3 to 2.0, 74 to 83%; zone 3 2.1 to 3.6, 84 to 88%; zone 4 3.7 to 5.7, 89 to 93%; zone 5 over 5.8, over 94%. Zones 1 and 2 are below LT1, zone 3 between LT1 and LT2, zones 4 and 5 above LT2. (The Intensity chapter gives the federation's Zone 2 as 72 to 82%, from Seiler 2010; the two versions differ by a point or two.)
- Hard training: 63 ± 14 h a year (46 to 85) in 106 ± 20 sessions; "HIT duration did not change (1.3 h.week−1) during any of the phases"; frequency 2 a week in general preparation, 3 in the peaking phase.
- Table 4, hours a week by phase (transition / general preparation Jun–Oct / specific preparation Nov–Dec / competition overall Jan–Mar / pre-peaking / peaking / regeneration):

### Solli, Tønnessen & Sandbakk 2017 [FT]
- "The most successful female cross-country skier ever": six Olympic golds, 18 World Championship golds, 110 World Cup wins [Abs]. **The paper does not name her in the text; the acknowledgements thank Marit Bjørgen by name. The draft does not name her (question for George).**
- 17 years, 2000 to 2017: 8,105 sessions (7,642 workouts, 463 competitions).
- Annual volume rose 80%, from 522 h at age 20 to 940 h at age 35, about 10 to 18 h a week.
- Best five years (May 2010 to April 2015): 937 ± 25 h in 543 ± 9 sessions; 849 h of endurance.
- Endurance time (modified session-goal method: **interval time runs from the start of the first interval to the end of the last, recoveries included**): 92.3 ± 0.3 / 2.9 ± 0.5 / 4.8 ± 0.5. By session: 76.1 / 7.3 / 16.6.
- Low intensity defined as under 2 mmol/L, 60 to 82% of maximum heart rate.
- Easy time: 21% warm-up, 14% sessions under 90 min, 65% sessions over 90 min [Abs].
- Per month by phase (general preparation / specific preparation / competition) [Abs]: easy 76 / 68 / 55 h; between the thresholds 2.8 / 2.2 / 1.0 h; above the second 2.8 / 3.2 / 4.7 h. [Own] Above the first threshold in total: 5.6 / 5.4 / 5.7 h a month, about 1.3 h a week (÷ 4.33). Matches Tønnessen's figure.
- Career: about 88 / 2 / 10 at ages 20 to 27, about 92 / 3 / 5 at 28 to 35. Two changes of approach: early, "extensive blocks of HIT", "rapid performance improvements that stagnated after a few years"; then "a reduction in the amount of HIT, and the implementation of relatively large amounts of LIT", directly before the five best years. First international gold at 23 on "slightly in excess of 700 h".
- Altitude camps: 18 to 25% of annual volume.
- Authors' hypothesis: the 12-year build-up let her tolerate the later load.

### Seiler 2010 [Abs]
Athletes training 10 to 13 times a week "converge on" about 80% of sessions at low intensity (about 2 mmol/L) and about 20% "dominated by periods of high-intensity work"; athletes "appear to self-organize toward a high-volume training approach"; intensification studies in already well-trained athletes "do not provide any convincing evidence" of long-term gains.

### Other sports
- Guellich, Seiler & Emrich 2009 [Abs]: 36 German junior rowers (31 international, 5 national junior finalists), 10.9 sessions a week, 37 weeks, diary with heart rate: 95 / 2 / 3 of rowing (under 2, 2 to 4, over 4 mmol/L).
- Fiskerstrand & Seiler 2004 [Abs]: 28 Norwegian rowers who won international medals 1970 to 2001; 21 of the 27 living answered a questionnaire. 1970s to 1990s: VO₂max 5.8 to 6.5 L/min (+12%); six-minute ergometer performance "almost 10%" better; training under 2 mmol/L from 30 to 50 h a month; race-pace and supra-maximal training (about 8 to 14 mmol/L) from 23 to about 7 h a month; volume +20%, 924 to 1,128 h a year; altitude camps moved into winter preparation.
- Orie et al. 2014 [Abs]: Olympic medal-winning Dutch speed skaters' programmes over 10 Olympiads (38 years), from interviews with coaches and skaters: zone 1 share rose (r = 0.96), zones 2 and 3 fell; no trend in total hours. **1972: 40 / 40 / 20; 2010: about 80 / 12 / 8** [Rep, Stöggl & Sperlich 2015, read].
- Haugen et al. 2022 [FT, parts]: world-class distance runners: 11 to 14 sessions a week; marathoners 160 to 220 km a week, track runners 130 to 190; at least 80% of distance at low intensity all year (zones 1 and 2 of the review's seven); "Most of this training is in turn executed in zone 1"; easy runs "typically 3–5 km h−1 slower than marathon pace".
- Sandbakk et al. 2011 [Abs]: 8 world-class against 8 national-class Norwegian sprint skiers: the world-class did more low- **and moderate-intensity** training.
- Esteve-Lanao 2005 [Abs]: 8 sub-elite runners, 6 months; time in zone 1 against race time r = −0.79 (p = 0.06) and −0.97 (p = 0.008). Eight people. Not used.
- Casado 2021 [Abs]: 85 elite male runners; total distance r ≥ 0.75 with performance, easy runs r ≥ 0.68. Not used.
- Stöggl & Sperlich 2015, review [FT, parts]: "Most retrospective studies report a pyramidal TID"; polarised distributions in three studies of cross-country skiers and one of marathon runners. Also: athletes prefer low intensity when volume is high, while "the majority of coaches favored higher intensity", in rowing, "mainly for theoretical reasons" [Rep]. Not used.

## 2. History (all from Casado, Foster, Bakken & Tjelta 2023 [FT, section 2], a review)
- Interval training named in the 1930s by Woldemar Gerschler and Herbert Reindel; Emil Zátopek ran "60 × 400 m or 40 × 400 m". Casado also calls him a "Czech runner" and "multiple-time Olympic champion in events from 5000 m to marathon from 1948 to 1952" (used from 17 October to say who he was).
- 1960s: Arthur Lydiard (New Zealand) "criticized the hard interval training regimes"; 10 to 12 weeks of mostly easy continuous running "targeted at reaching 100 miles (160 km) per week", then hills, then track intervals. Coached Peter Snell (three Olympic golds, 800 and 1,500 m, 1960 to 1964) and Murray Halberg (5,000 m gold, 1960). Ernst van Aaken in Germany proposed much the same.
- 1970s and 1980s: Lydiard's volume plus interval sessions in the preparation period; "hard day–easy day" "usually attributed to" Bill Bowerman and Bill Dellinger at Oregon.
- Since then "two to five weekly sessions of interval training and/or longer tempo runs combined with a relatively high volume of easy" running; "Most of these training characteristics have been determined through a 'trial and error' approach rather than by the outcomes of intervention studies."
- The Norwegian model: three to four lactate-guided threshold interval sessions a week (2 to 4.5 mmol/L, measured every one to three repetitions) and one session at VO₂max intensity, within 150 to 180 km a week [Abs]. Marius Bakken, a co-author, developed it on himself from the late 1990s; 5,000 m in 13:06.39; "he could perform a much higher training volume compared with that when he carried out interval training in z4". The paper names Jakob Ingebrigtsen and his brothers as users. **The draft names Bakken (an author describing his own training) and not the Ingebrigtsens (question for George).**

## 3. Mechanism
- Recovery: Seiler 2007 (in Finding your zones and What easy riding does): 5 to 10 min after easy sessions, about 30 min after a session between the thresholds or intervals.
- Signalling: Laursen 2010 [Abs]: adaptations to high-volume and high-intensity training "show considerable overlap", but "the molecular events that signal for these adaptations may be different"; about 75% low, 10 to 15% very high "has been suggested as an optimal" distribution. Casado 2023 [Abs]: the Norwegian model "may increase the mitochondrial proliferation through the optimization of both calcium and … AMPK signaling pathways". **Both are suggestions in reviews. No study in this chapter tests them.** The calcium and AMPK routes themselves are in How bodies adapt.
- Stöggl & Sperlich 2014 discussion [FT]: threshold work "might generate unwarranted sympathetic stress". Speculation; not used.

## 4. The trials
- Esteve-Lanao et al. 2007 [Abs]: 12 sub-elite runners, randomised, 5 months, heart-rate controlled: 80.5 / 11.8 / 8.3 against 66.8 / 24.7 / 8.5. Simulated 10.4 km cross-country race: −157 ± 13 s against −121.5 ± 7.1 s (p = 0.03). [Own] 35.5 s apart. Group sizes not in the abstract.
- Neal et al. 2013 [Abs]: 12 male cyclists, randomised crossover, two 6-week periods separated by 4 weeks of detraining: polarised 6.4 ± 1.4 h a week at 80 / 0 / 20, threshold 7.5 ± 2.0 h at 57 / 43 / 0. 40 km time trial, peak power, lactate threshold and high-intensity capacity rose in both. Greater after polarised: peak power 8 ± 2% against 3 ± 1%; lactate threshold 9 ± 3% against 2 ± 4%; time to exhaustion at 95% of maximal work rate 85 ± 14% against 37 ± 14%. No difference in the time trial is claimed. No change in mitochondrial enzymes.
- **Stöggl & Sperlich 2014 [FT], the close-up.** 48 "healthy competitive endurance athletes" (15 cyclists, 21 runners, 4 triathletes, 8 from the Austrian cross-country ski team by team membership), 31 ± 6 years, VO₂peak 62.6 ± 7.1 (52 to 75), used to 10 to 20 h a week. Before the study: "none of the participants had regularly executed HIIT. All had followed a HVT training protocol with a maximum of two THR training sessions per week." Randomised by VO₂peak and mode into four groups, nine weeks, heart-rate controlled; tested on a bike or treadmill.
  - High volume: six sessions a week in loading weeks, three 90-min easy, two 150 to 240 min easy, one 60-min threshold interval session.
  - Threshold: two 60-min threshold interval sessions, one 90-min threshold session, one fartlek, two 90-min easy.
  - Intervals only ("HIIT"): two blocks of 12 sessions in 15 days of 4 × 4 min at 90 to 95% of peak heart rate.
  - Polarised: two 60-min interval sessions (4 × 4 min), two 150 to 240 min easy sessions with six to eight 5-second sprints, two 90-min easy.
  - 41 finished. Seven lost: 2 from intervals-only, 1 from high volume, 4 from threshold (illness 2, competition schedule 3, not following the programme 2). [Own] If the groups began at 12 each, the threshold group ended with 8; the paper does not give group sizes.
  - Table 1, the nine weeks: hours 104 ± 20 (polarised), 66 ± 1 (intervals), 84 ± 7 (threshold), 102 ± 11 (high volume). Sessions 54, 47, 49, 58. **The distribution is a count of sessions:** polarised 37 easy, 3 threshold, 14 hard = 68 / 6 / 26%; intervals 20 / 0 / 27 = 43 / 0 / 57; threshold 23 / 26 / 0 = 46 / 54 / 0; high volume 49 / 9 / 1 = 83 / 16 / 1. (The 2015 review lists this study's method as "session goal approach".)
  - [Own] Polarised group: 14 interval sessions × 16 min of intervals = 224 min = 3.7 h of 104 h = 3.6% of training time.
  - VO₂peak: polarised +11.7 ± 8.4% (60.6 to 67.4), intervals +4.8 ± 5.6%, no significant change in the other two; in the threshold group individual changes ranged from −20% to +4%.
  - Time to exhaustion in the ramp test: +17.4, +8.8, +6.2, +8.0% (polarised, intervals, threshold, high volume); "no statistical differences were found between the four training concepts" (F = 2.0, NS).
  - Speed or power at 4 mmol/L: +8.1, +5.6, +1.4, +1.2%. Peak speed or power: +5.1, +4.4, +1.8, −1.5%.
  - Body mass −3.7% in the intervals-only group (73.5 to 70.7 kg).
  - Authors' limitations: no time trial ("a direct transfer to specific competition situation … need to be established"); the 11% gain "is large for well-trained endurance and has to be put in perspective"; "due to that the participants of this study mainly used HVT prior to this experiment, the HVT model might not have provided an adequate stimulus".
  - Authors on per-session yield: 27 interval sessions gave +4.8%, 14 gave +11.7%.
  - Stated aim and hypothesis (Introduction, read 14 Oct 2026 for the trial table): "The aim of this study was to compare the effects of four training concepts"; "We hypothesized that the POL and HIIT group would lead to superior improvements compared with HVT and THR." Abstract conclusion: "POL resulted in the greatest improvements in most key variables of endurance performance in well-trained endurance athletes. THR or HVT did not lead to further improvements in performance related variables." [FT]
  - [Own] 104 − 66 = 38 hours more training in the polarised group than the interval group over nine weeks (used in the draft).
- Ingham et al. 2008 [Abs]: 18 experienced rowers, 12 weeks, all training at or below the lactate threshold against 70% at or below and 30% hard: 2,000 m ergometer performance and VO₂peak improved equally (p = 0.8 and 0.42); power at the lactate threshold improved more in the all-easy group (23.5 against 5.1 W).
- Treff et al. 2017 [FT, parts]: 14 national elite rowers (20 years, VO₂max 66), 11 weeks, **not randomised** (crews could not be split): 94 / 1 / 6 against 93 / 3 / 2. 2,000 m ergometer power +1.5 ± 1.7% and +1.5 ± 2.6%, neither significant, no difference between groups. Diaries, with coaches free to adapt the plan.
- Muñoz et al. 2014 [Abs]: 30 recreational runners, 10 weeks: about 77 / 3 / 20 against 46 / 35 / 19. 10 km: 39:18 to 37:19 (5.0%) against 39:24 to 38:00 (3.6%); about 41 s apart; "This difference was not significant." A subset analysis of the most compliant favoured polarised; the abstract's group numbers for it do not add up (12 runners; n = 6 and n = 16). Not used.
- Festa et al. 2019 [Abs; dropout paragraph FT]: 38 recreational runners, 8 weeks, matched for training impulse: 77 / 3 / 20 against 40 / 50 / 10. Both improved (2 km speed +3.5% and +3.0%); no differences; the threshold-heavy group trained 24.8 h against 29.9 h ("saving 17%"). Dropouts 1 of 19 against 4 of 19.
- Filipas et al. 2022 [FT, parts]: 60 well-trained male runners analysed (VO₂peak 68; four dropouts, one per group), 16 weeks, matched load, about 300 min a week easy (77 to 80% of time), so about 6.5 h a week [Own]. Pyramidal, polarised, and each order of eight weeks of one then the other. All improved; 5 km gains 0.6 to 1.7%; pyramidal-then-polarised "about 0.5% higher than in the other groups", "at least a 5-s further improvement"; the paper gives the smallest detectable change in a 5 km time trial as 3.2% and says the result is below it.
- **Meta-analyses**
  - Rosenblat, Perrotta & Vicenzino 2019 [Abs]: search to November 2016; four randomised trials of polarised against threshold in trained athletes, three pooled for time trial: ES −0.66 (95% CI −1.17 to −0.15) favouring polarised, "a moderate effect". Trial quality 4 or 5 out of 10. 112 participants [Rep, Silva Oliveira 2024].
  - Silva Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024 [FT]: 17 studies, 437 people, median 10 weeks (4 to 16). VO₂peak favours polarised, SMD 0.24 (0.01 to 0.48), p = 0.040, 11 studies. Time trial −0.01 (−0.28 to 0.25), p = 0.92, nine studies. Time to exhaustion 0.30 (−0.20 to 0.79). Speed or power at the second threshold 0.04 (−0.21 to 0.29). No significant difference against any single alternative (threshold, pyramidal, intervals, control). Under 12 weeks: VO₂peak 0.40 (0.08 to 0.71); 12 weeks or more: 0.04 (−0.32 to 0.40). Highly trained: 0.46 (0.10 to 0.82); trained: 0.08 (−0.23 to 0.39). **Authors:** "studies including highly trained athletes were also those that had a shorter duration … it is not possible to disentangle". Every randomised trial rated "some concerns" for bias; the authors nonetheless rate certainty "high" for every outcome. Count of single studies on time trial: eight no difference, one favouring polarised. The included list has both Stöggl & Sperlich 2014 and a 2017 paper on the same athletes; whether both are in the VO₂peak pool I could not tell from the text.
  - Rosenblat et al. 2025 [Abs; author list from Crossref]: individual participant data, 13 studies, 348 athletes (296 men, 52 women; 150 recreational, 198 competitive), VO₂max 46.6 to 68.3. Polarised against pyramidal: VO₂max SMD −0.06 (p = 0.68), time trial −0.05 (p = 0.34). "No statistically significant differences between POL and any of the other TID interventions." Subgroup: the VO₂max response differed between recreational and competitive athletes (SMD −0.63, p < 0.05): "Competitive athletes may have greater improvements to VO2max with POL, while recreational athletes may improve more with a PYR TID." **Seventeen authors, among them the authors of many of the pooled trials; Seiler is last.** No confidence intervals in the abstract.
  - Cove et al. 2025 [Abs]: cyclists only, VO₂max at least 59, interventions over four weeks: 41 studies, 81 training groups, 797 participants. Training improved VO₂max (g = 0.42) and time trial (g = 0.39), "with no significant differences between training modalities"; longer programmes did better; "No associations were found between weekly or total training volume and changes in VO2max or time-trial performance."
- The exchange in *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2022;54(6): Foster, Casado, Esteve-Lanao, Haugen & Seiler, "Polarized Training Is Optimal for Endurance Athletes", pp. 1028–1031; Burnley, Bearden & Jones, "Polarized Training Is Not Optimal for Endurance Athletes", pp. 1032–1034 [bibliographic, Crossref]. **Neither read.** A third-party digest (Sportsmith, by Gareth Sandford) reports Burnley and colleagues as arguing that by time most "polarised" training is pyramidal and that work between the thresholds is undervalued [Rep, not used in the draft].
- Sun et al. 2025 [FT, one paragraph]: "Seiler posits that athletes who engage in approximately 350 h every year frequently display a TID pattern akin to the lactate threshold model", moving to polarised or pyramidal above 750 h. **A second-hand attribution with no data; the website's evidence review presents it as an observation by Sun. Used in the draft as Seiler's suggestion, reported by a review.**

## 5. Where the evidence runs out
- No trial of intensity distribution in athletes of the standard described in section 1.
- No trial longer than five months; the median is ten weeks.
- No trial that adds easy hours to trained cyclists at a fixed amount of hard work (the gap noted in What easy riding does).
- No record that splits professional cyclists' easy riding into lower and upper parts.
- The chapter's position (the hard work is a fixed dose, the ratio is an outcome) is **my inference** from two sets of diaries. The steady 1.3 h a week is a fact of those diaries; that it is a ceiling, and that it applies to cyclists or amateurs, is not shown.

## The book's rule for how hard an easy ride should be (11 October 2026)

George, on the conflict between What easy riding does (top of Zone 2 beats the bottom) and this chapter (champions train below Zone 2): "it's important the book is consistent- maybe we take an opinion here and stick to it". **The rule proposed and written into both chapters: an easy ride should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions. On six to ten hours a week that is Zone 2, a few beats under the first threshold; on 15 hours or more it is lower.** Durability's rule of thumb (10 to 15 W under the top of the zone on rides over two hours) already fits.
- For: at equal hours the top of the range is more work and a larger signal (What easy riding does); training load predicts mitochondrial gain (Mølmen 2025); recovery is flat below the threshold in Seiler 2007.
- Against: no trial compares positions within the easy range; Olympic champion skiers and world-class runners do nearly all their easy training in the lowest zone.
- The explanation for the skiers (15 easy hours in ten sessions; carbohydrate use 2.7 times as fast at 64% as at 31% of VO₂max, Gollnick 1974) is [Own]. So is the reading of Pogačar's account. Grade C throughout.
