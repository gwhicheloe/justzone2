## The question

Where is the top of your Zone 2, how is it measured, and how good are the shortcuts?

## The argument (to approve)

1. The top of Zone 2 is the first lactate threshold: the power and heart rate at which lactate production first outruns clearance.
2. The boundary matters because of what it costs to cross it. Below it effort settles, lasts for hours (Black 2017) and leaves little to recover from: nine trained runners recovered in 5 to 10 minutes after one or two hours below it, and in 30 minutes after anything above it (Seiler 2007, grade B). Whether just-below builds more fitness than well-below is left to Part II.
3. **The book's definition:** the last intensity before blood lactate rises 0.5 mmol/L above the rider's own baseline, in a test with stages of at least four minutes. Three reasons: lactate is what the Zone 2 argument was built on (San Millán & Brooks 2018); a rider can measure it; and a rise above one's own baseline is better than a fixed 2 mmol/L.
4. The choice is practical, not proven (grade C). Breathing has as good a claim, was slightly more repeatable in one study, and differs from lactate by 0 to 24 W on average. No trial compares the two as training anchors. The rule only works with stages of several minutes: in a fast ramp the same 0.5 rise marks the second threshold.
5. Measuring it: the laboratory test, the home meter (typical error 0.1 to 0.5 mmol/L, so confirm a rise at the next stage), technique, and repeatability (SD of 19 W between two tests).
6. Breathing and the talk test: the closest proxy and the best-supported free one (grade B), but checked against breathing, not lactate. Only one study reports lactate at the talk-test stages.
7. Heart rate variability: on average near the lactate threshold, up to 30 beats out for one rider (close-up, grade B).
8. Percentages of maximum heart rate, the drift test, nose breathing and feel: weak or untested (grade C). Maximum heart rate is now one paragraph.
9. A step test on a smart trainer that takes lactate and the talk test together. **Untested as a package.**
10. Second threshold: performance tests; the Conconi test did not survive.
11. The heart rate at the lactate threshold held steady through a professional season while power rose (Lucía 2000), so power needs rechecking more than heart rate.

## Where I disagree with George's starting point, for him to weigh

- "Zone 2 can only really be determined by lactate testing" is true once the book defines it by lactate. The evidence does not show lactate to be a better boundary than breathing; the draft says so plainly and grades the choice C.
- Choosing lactate makes the talk test, the method most readers will use, a proxy for a proxy. The draft says that too.
- I think the choice is still the right one for this book, for the three reasons in point 3. If George would rather the book stayed with the panel's "lactate or ventilatory", the *Why lactate* section and one paragraph in the Intensity chapter are what would change.

## Weak points

- **The exact rule (0.5 mmol/L above baseline; four-minute stages) is my proposal.** It follows Meixner 2025 and Olieslagers 2026 and is supported by Jamnick 2018, but no source names it the best definition.
- **What the baseline is, is also my choice** (added 11 October after George asked why lactate dipped in the sketch). The papers differ: Pallarés 2016 averaged the early stages, Meixner 2025 labels its baseline a minimum, Olieslagers 2026 and Jamnick 2018 do not say. The draft takes the average and says why. The first version of the draft wrongly gave the average as the rule of the Würzburg and Leuven studies. A real early fall in lactate is often described but I have no source for it, so the draft puts early wobble down to the meter's error only.
- **Home lactate testing rests on thin evidence:** two meter studies read as abstracts; nothing on self-testing by riders; sampling technique is standard practice with no source. I have taken "home lactate tests" to mean finger-prick meters.
- **Every talk-test study is abstract only.** Persinger 2004 and Foster 2008 are on the papers-wanted list.
- **The step protocol is an untested synthesis, and the author will not be testing its lactate half:** George said on 11 October that he is too squeamish for a home lactate test (now the chapter's *From the saddle* passage). He could try the talk-test half. The lactate steps are therefore recommended from the literature alone.
- Seiler 2007 is abstract only, nine runners, one recovery marker, and its boundary was set by breathing.
- San Millán & Brooks 2018 is abstract only. "The case made on podcasts descends from work such as…" is my characterisation.
- Folk rules with no source: Zone 2 at 60–70% of maximum, the drift test's 5%, nose breathing.
- The Grayson story is second-hand and unsourced; the draft says "is credited".
- Yeh 1983's "16%": the abstract does not give the units.
- First-person sentences about what the author could or could not find rest on Europe PMC searches made on 11 October.
- First names, nationalities and institutions not given in the papers are from memory.
- **Libel rule (Drugs chapter brief):** Conconi is named only as the first author of the 1982 paper. Keep it that way.
