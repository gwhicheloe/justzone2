# Chapter 6: Finding your zones

Subtract your age from 220. For half a century that sum has stood in for maximum heart rate on gym machines, in textbooks and in the set-up screens of heart-rate monitors, and most heart-rate zones are percentages of its answer. In 2000 two exercise physiologists at the University of New Mexico, Robert Robergs and Roberto Landwehr, went looking for the study that had produced it [@robergs2002].

They began with textbooks. Of the 18 they checked, 13 printed the formula with no source, and one of the 13 was Robergs's own. The other five cited five different papers. Many books set the formula beside the heart rate reserve method of the Finnish physiologist Martti Karvonen, so they contacted him. Karvonen said he had never published research on it and suggested they try Per-Olof Åstrand. A month later Åstrand was in Albuquerque to collect a lifetime achievement award. He had not derived it either, though he had said in lectures that a formula of that kind looked close to the data and would be convenient to use [@robergs2002].

The trail ended at a 1971 review of exercise and heart disease by Samuel Fox, John Naughton and William Haskell [@fox1971]. In its section on intensity there is a figure of about 35 points, each one the maximum heart rate reported for a group of people of a given age, gathered from about ten earlier sources. Nobody fitted a line to them. The caption said that no single line would represent the data adequately, and that 220 minus age "defines a line not far from many of the data points". Robergs and Landwehr read the points off the figure and fitted the line themselves. It came out at 215 minus 0.91 times age, and the scatter around it had a standard error of 21 beats per minute (bpm) [@robergs2002].

The formula was a remark in a caption, about a line judged by eye through other people's averages.

Better equations have been fitted since. In 2001 Hirofumi Tanaka and colleagues pooled 351 studies covering 18,712 people and arrived at 208 minus 0.7 times age, then confirmed it in 514 people tested in their own laboratory [@tanaka2001]. A Norwegian study that took 3,320 healthy adults to a verified maximum got 211 minus 0.64 times age [@nes2013]. Both show that the old formula reads low for older riders. For a 60-year-old it gives 160 bpm, and the newer equations give 166 and 173.

![Maximum heart rate predicted from age by the equation from 3,320 healthy Norwegian adults, with bands of one and two standard errors (10.8 bpm) either side of it. The shares of people inside each band assume the errors are normally distributed, which the paper's abstract does not state [@nes2013].](figures/hrmax-age.svg)

The newer equations correct the average and leave the scatter where it was. The Norwegian paper reports a standard error of 10.8 bpm. If the errors are spread in the usual bell shape, about one adult in three has a true maximum more than 11 beats from the predicted one, and one in twenty is more than 21 beats away. Sex, fitness, activity and body mass made almost no difference to the prediction, so a rider has nothing to add to the equation that would narrow it [@nes2013]. Robergs and Landwehr collected 43 published equations and reported prediction errors above 10 bpm in most of those based on age alone. Their first conclusion was that there is at present no acceptable method of estimating maximum heart rate [@robergs2002]. {grade A}

A rider who sets zones from age then stacks a second estimate on the first, which is where the first threshold lies as a percentage of that maximum. Chapter 5 fixed Zone 2 as the top of the easy range, immediately below the first threshold. Finding your own Zone 2 means finding that threshold in your own body, and the methods on offer run from a formula to a laboratory. Each can be judged the way Chapter 5 judged FTP: by what it was checked against, and by how far out it can be for one person.

## Percentages of a maximum

Suppose you know your true maximum. In the 100 adults of Chapter 5 the first threshold lay anywhere from 60% to 90% of it [@iannetta2020]. In 50 trained cyclists it averaged 81%, and about a third of them were more than four percentage points away from that [@meixner2025]. The rule printed on gym machines and built into many apps puts Zone 2 at 60–70% of maximum. For the average rider in the cycling study, the top of that band is about 20 beats below the threshold. A rider following the rule is riding easy, which does no harm, and is a long way under the intensity the 2025 panel meant by Zone 2.

![Two rules for Zone 2 and four measurements of the first threshold, each as a percentage of the person's own maximum or peak heart rate. The 60–70% rule is in wide use and I have no source for its origin. The Norwegian band and the 50 cyclists are from Meixner and colleagues [@meixner2025], the 16 volunteers from Persinger and colleagues [@persinger2004], and the 100 adults from Iannetta and colleagues [@iannetta2020].](figures/threshold-percent.svg)

Percentages of VO₂max do no better. A German group had 21 fit men ride for an hour at 60% of their VO₂max, adjusting the power to hold oxygen uptake steady. Their blood lactate averaged 2.1 mmol/L. Individually it ran from 0.7, which is a resting level, to 5.6, which most definitions would place above the second threshold. Sorting the men into three groups by fitness did not shrink the spread [@scharhag2010]. {grade B}

Karvonen's heart rate reserve method takes a percentage of the gap between resting and maximum heart rate, which brings in one number that is the rider's own. In the cycling tests of one talk-test study the ventilatory threshold came at 74% of the reserve on average [@persinger2004]. The method is still a fixed fraction of a maximum that is usually guessed.

Phil Maffetone's 180 formula leaves the maximum out. It is 180 minus age, less 5 or 10 beats for illness, injury or inconsistent training, plus 5 for two years of steady progress [@maffetone180]. Every adjustment but one pushes towards caution. Its author's page describes it as distilled from his own assessments of athletes and cites no study that compares it with a measured threshold, and I could not find one. It starts from age, and nothing in the research above suggests that age predicts the threshold any better than it predicts the maximum.

## How sharp the laboratory is

A laboratory test is the best starting point for anyone who can get one, and it is less exact than its price suggests. Chapter 5 gave the reasons on the lactate side: 25 ways of reading one curve [@faude2009], and answers that shift with the length of the stages [@jamnick2018].

The breathing side has its own. The first ventilatory threshold is a bend in a noisy line, and somebody has to decide where the bend is. In 1983 a group of researchers gave the breathing data from eight people's ramp tests to four exercise physiologists, working independently, and asked each to mark the threshold. For a given subject their answers spanned 16% on average, a range the authors compared to the whole reported difference between untrained and trained people [@yeh1983]. Laboratories now reduce this with averaging and with two or three readers who must agree, which is an acknowledgement that readers differ.

## Reading the threshold from heartbeats

The newest home method needs only a chest strap. A heart at rest does not beat like a metronome. The gaps between beats vary, and the variation has structure: long and short gaps follow one another in patterns that repeat across time scales. As exercise gets harder the structure dissolves and the variation becomes random. An index called DFA alpha 1, from a technique named detrended fluctuation analysis, puts a number on this. It sits around 1.0 in very light exercise and falls towards 0.5, the value for pure noise, at high intensity [@rogers2021].

In 2021 a group led by Bruce Rogers proposed that the midpoint, 0.75, marks the first threshold. The value was chosen on theory, as halfway between order and noise. They tested it in 15 men running on a treadmill and it held: the first ventilatory threshold (VT1) came at 152 bpm on average and the heartbeat threshold at 154, with a correlation of 0.97 [@rogers2021]. Free phone apps now calculate the index live from a chest strap.

That correlation deserves the suspicion Chapter 5 recommended. The 15 men were aged from 19 to 52, and their heart rate at the threshold ran from 108 to 183 bpm. Across a spread that wide, almost any two measures that rise with effort will correlate. The limits of agreement are the better guide, and they ran from 12 beats one way to 8 the other [@rogers2021]. Twenty beats from end to end is about the width of a heart-rate zone.

### One study, up close: a chest strap against the laboratory

In 2026 a group at KU Leuven in Belgium tested the method on a bike, with a protocol of the kind a coach would use [@olieslagers2026]. They recruited 24 active men and analysed 21, whose average age was 23, who did about 10 hours of sport a week, and whose VO₂peak averaged 56 mL/kg/min. Each rode one test to exhaustion, starting at 40 W and rising by 30 W every four minutes. A mask measured breathing gases, a drop of blood was taken from the earlobe after each stage, and a chest strap recorded every heartbeat. The people who read the ventilatory thresholds worked blind to the other results and separately from those who read the heartbeat data.

The 0.75 threshold came at 161 bpm on average, and VT1 at 144. The heartbeat method put the top of the easy range 17 beats and 45 W too high. For individual riders the limits of agreement ran from 41 beats too high to 7 too low, and the correlation between the two measures was 0.36. A personalised cut-off, set from each rider's own starting value as Rogers's group had since proposed [@rogers2024], cut the average error to 9 beats and left the scatter as wide. The authors concluded that the method was not reliable enough for finding the first threshold in a test of this kind [@olieslagers2026].

The same tables show the laboratory's two measures of the first threshold disagreeing with each other. The first rise in lactate, defined as 0.5 mmol/L above baseline, came at 153 bpm and 180 W. VT1 came at 144 bpm and 156 W. That is 9 beats and 24 W apart on average, in the same riders during the same test. For individuals the limits of agreement ran from 30 beats one way to 11 the other, and the correlation between the two reference measures was 0.51 [@olieslagers2026].

Set against the lactate threshold instead of the ventilatory one, the heartbeat method was 8 beats out on average, a difference that was not statistically significant. The personalised version matched the lactate threshold exactly on average, with limits of about 30 beats either side. A pooled analysis of 22 earlier studies had found the same lean, with the heartbeat threshold agreeing better with lactate than with breathing [@tanner2024].

The laboratory's two measures do not always sit this far apart. In the 50 cyclists of Chapter 5, tested with three-minute stages, they agreed on average to within a watt [@meixner2025]. How well they agree seems to depend on the protocol as well.

The authors list the study's limits [@olieslagers2026]. All 21 riders were young men. Each did one test, so nobody knows whether a rider's heartbeat threshold would come out the same the following week. Four-minute stages suit lactate sampling and may blur the ventilatory threshold, which is easier to see in a smooth ramp. The work was funded by the university and the authors declared no conflicts of interest. For a rider it settles one point: a live DFA alpha 1 reading on a phone should not set the top of Zone 2 unchecked. {grade B}

The 2024 pooled analysis looks more encouraging in its summary. Across 22 studies the average difference between the heartbeat threshold and the laboratory one was trivial, and the pooled correlation was 0.85. The body of the paper reports that about 90% of the variation between those studies reflected real differences in what they found, and that a new study could expect a correlation anywhere from 0.06 to 0.99 [@tanner2024]. The trivial average comes from studies that erred in both directions.

### Reading the research: validated against what?

A validation study compares a cheap method with a reference and reports the gap. The gap belongs to both of them. If the reference is itself uncertain, part of the error charged to the cheap method was never its fault, and no cheap method can be shown to be more accurate than the reference is.

The Leuven study lets a reader see this because it measured two references. Against breathing, the heartbeat threshold was 17 beats out and the difference was statistically significant. Against lactate it was 8 beats out and the difference was not. Had the group collected only lactate, they could fairly have reported that the method agreed with the laboratory on average. Studies in this field differ in which reference they use, and the pooled analysis found that the choice changes the result [@tanner2024].

So when a paper says a home method agrees with the laboratory, find out which laboratory measure was the reference: breathing or lactate, and by which of Chapter 5's definitions. Find out how repeatable that reference is. If the same rider were tested again, or the same test read by another physiologist, the answer would move by some amount, and the 1983 study of four physiologists is one of the few to have measured it. And check whether the people who read the reference were kept blind to the home method's result, as they were in Leuven. A threshold found by eye is easy to nudge towards a number the reader already knows.

For the first threshold, the two best references were about 10 beats apart on average in one careful study, and up to 30 apart in individual riders [@olieslagers2026]. A home method that lands within 10 beats of either has done about as well as the laboratory does against itself.

## The talk test

The oldest method needs no equipment. It is credited to a Professor Grayson, who in 1939 is said to have advised British mountaineers to climb no faster than they could speak, though the paper that repeats the story gives no source for it [@woltmann2015]. The modern version comes from Carl Foster's group at the University of Wisconsin–La Crosse. During a test of rising intensity, at the end of each stage, the person recites a set passage aloud and is asked whether they can speak comfortably. "Yes" makes it a positive stage, anything uncertain makes it the equivocal stage, and "no" makes it negative.

The reason it works lies in what speech does to breathing. To talk, you have to let air out slowly and under control, and you can't do that while breathing hard. In 1981 two British physiologists measured the cost. Six people ran at five steady speeds, and in the fifth minute at each speed they read a 100-word passage aloud, which took about half a minute. At every speed their ventilation while reading fell to about 55% of what it had been. At the fastest speed that was a cut of 38 litres a minute from 78. About a quarter of the air they did move at that speed went out in silent breaths and not as speech. When they stopped reading, ventilation overshot by 14% and was back to normal within 30 seconds. The authors concluded that the demands of speech take precedence over the demands of gas exchange [@doust1981].

A talking rider is therefore breathing about half as much as the effort calls for and making it up between sentences. Below the first threshold there is room for that. Above it, as Chapter 5 described, buffering lactate releases extra carbon dioxide that has to be breathed out, the drive to breathe rises out of proportion to the work, and half a minute at half ventilation becomes uncomfortable. That is the likely reason comfortable speech ends near the first ventilatory threshold. It is an inference from the two sets of findings, and no study I have read shows the steps in order.

Talking has a cost as well. When 14 people ran at 65%, 75% and 85% of VO₂max with and without talking, talking lowered their ventilation and oxygen uptake and raised their blood lactate and blood pressure, while heart rate did not change [@meckel2002]. The summary of the paper gives no sizes for these effects. They suggest that a rider who chats through a long ride is under a little more strain than the heart-rate monitor shows.

Foster's group checked the talk test against the ventilatory threshold in 2004. Sixteen healthy volunteers did tests of rising intensity on a treadmill and on a cycle ergometer, once with their breathing gases measured and once with the talk test. The authors concluded that the equivocal stage was "almost exactly equivalent" to the threshold on both machines, and that negative stages were consistently above it [@persinger2004]. The figures in the paper's summary are less tidy. On the bike the threshold came at 82% of peak heart rate and the equivocal stage at 87%. On the treadmill the figures were 89% and 86%. Neither difference was statistically significant. With 16 people, a gap of five percentage points, about 9 beats for someone with a maximum of 180, can fail to reach significance and still be real, as Chapter 4 explained. On a bike, in that study, the uncertain stage sat somewhat above the threshold.

A second study tested whether the two move together. The researchers lowered people's thresholds by having them give blood and raised them with training, and both times the last positive stage of the talk test shifted to match [@foster2008]. This is stronger evidence than a correlation, because the researchers moved one thing and watched the other follow. The summary gives no numbers for the size of the shifts. It does report that when the intensity was a little above the threshold, speech took more than two minutes on average to become uncomfortable. It also reports that the errors leaned one way: people tended to pass the test when they were in fact above the threshold. {grade B}

Trained cyclists have been tested twice, and the two papers recommend different stages. In 18 highly trained cyclists the equivocal stage matched the first ventilatory threshold, at 148 bpm against 150 and 3.6 W/kg against 3.7, and the negative stage matched the second, at 176 bpm against 177 [@rodriguezmarroyo2013]. In 12 highly trained cyclists in another laboratory the last positive stage came at 129 bpm, far below the threshold, the equivocal stage at 147 and the negative stage at 156, and the authors concluded that the negative stage was the one that estimated the threshold [@gillespie2015]. The equivocal stages of the two studies agree almost to the beat. The likeliest reason for the different advice is the question asked. The first study called a stage negative when the rider "could definitely not talk". The second called it negative when the rider answered "no" to whether he could speak comfortably, which happens much sooner. The talk test's answer depends on the wording of its question, and a rider using it should choose the words once and keep them.

The length of the stages matters as much. Earlier talk-test studies had used stages of two minutes, and speech takes longer than that to become uncomfortable just above the threshold [@foster2008]. A short stage can end before the rider has found out. When 16 active people ran for 30 minutes at the pace of each stage found in a test of rising speed, all of them completed the half hour at the last positive stage, three could not complete it at the equivocal stage, and only two completed it at the negative stage [@woltmann2015]. An intensity just under the first threshold can be held for hours. For some of those runners the uncertain stage had evidently been well above it.

Taken across these studies, the equivocal stage is the best single estimate of the first threshold. It tends to run a few beats high, more so with short stages, and the last clearly comfortable stage lies on the safe side of it. Every one of these studies took the ventilatory threshold as its reference, which is the fair comparison for a test of breathing. None reports limits of agreement in its summary, so how far out the test can be for one rider is not known. A 2022 review, with Foster among its authors, judged the talk test a valid marker of both thresholds [@bok2022]. Most of this evidence comes from one research group.

## Counting, nose breathing and pleasure

The counting talk test turns speech into a number. At rest, the person counts aloud at a steady pace as far as one breath will take them. During exercise they do it again, and the count is expressed as a percentage of the resting count. In 40 young adults on a treadmill that percentage tracked heart rate reserve with a correlation of −0.93, and counting to between a third and a half of the resting figure corresponded to the range the American College of Sports Medicine calls moderate to vigorous [@norman2008]. Nobody has compared it with a measured threshold, so it can't yet say where Zone 2 ends.

Breathing through the nose is a cue cyclists often pass on: if you can keep your mouth shut, you are in Zone 2. The switch from nose to mouth has been measured. In 30 adults exercising at rising intensity with a mask over the nose, 20 changed from breathing through the nose alone to nose and mouth together at an average of 105 W, when they were moving about 35 litres of air a minute. Four breathed through the mouth from the start and five never opened it. Each person's switch point was consistent, the points varied widely between people, and what related to them most closely was how hard breathing felt and how much work the nose demanded [@niinimaa1980]. I found no study that compares the switch with the first threshold. For nine of those 30 people it could not have marked anything.

In 2008 a group of exercise psychologists led by Panteleimon Ekkekakis had 30 young adults do 15-minute treadmill sessions below, at and above their own ventilatory threshold, and rate repeatedly how good or bad they felt. Above the threshold their pleasure fell, and it fell in much the same way across the group [@ekkekakis2008]. The 2022 review found that ratings between "fairly good" and "good" on a rating scale called the Feeling Scale go with the first threshold. It also found that people asked to hold a given feeling settle at heart rates too varied for the scale to mark the boundary [@bok2022]. Zone 2 is, roughly, the hardest riding that still feels good, and the scale is too loose to find its top edge. {grade C}

Perceived exertion is in the same position. The review puts the first threshold at 10 to 11 on Borg's 6-to-20 scale, and the 12 trained cyclists above rated their uncertain stage at 13.6 [@bok2022; @gillespie2015].

## Drift, and the lactate meter

Coaches often recommend a drift test. Ride for an hour at a steady power and compare your average heart rate in the second half with the first. A rise of less than 5% is taken to mean the power was at or below the first threshold. The physiology under it is real. Over a long steady effort heart rate creeps upward while the volume of blood pumped with each beat falls [@coyle2001drift], and Chapter 5 described the creeping oxygen cost that begins above the first threshold. I could find no study that tests the 5% rule against a measured threshold. Heat, dehydration and the length of the ride all raise drift for reasons unconnected to the threshold, as Chapter 9 describes, so a failed test may only show that the room was warm. It is a reasonable check on a number found another way, done in a cool room with a fan. {grade C}

A portable lactate meter brings one of the laboratory's instruments home. The meters have an error of 0.2 to 0.4 mmol/L [@jones2019], and the first threshold is usually defined as a rise of 0.5. The rider also still has to choose among the 25 definitions [@faude2009]. In Leuven, with trained staff and laboratory equipment, the lactate and breathing thresholds came out 9 beats apart. A home lactate test done well gives one of two reference answers that can differ by that much.

## A step test for the turbo trainer

A smart trainer makes the talk test easy to run properly. In ERG mode the trainer holds a set power whatever the cadence, so each stage is exactly the power it is meant to be. The protocol below is assembled from the studies above. Nobody has tested it as a package, and each choice is given with the finding it rests on.

1. Pick a day when you are rested, and ride in a cool room with a fan. Heat and fatigue both raise heart rate at a given power.
2. Warm up for 10 minutes at a very easy power.
3. Start well below where you expect Zone 2 to be and ride four-minute stages, adding 10 to 15 W each time. Four minutes, because speech takes more than two to become uncomfortable just above the threshold [@foster2008]. Small steps, so that the last comfortable stage and the first uncertain one are close together.
4. In the last 40 seconds of each stage, note your heart rate, then say the same passage aloud: about 100 words that you know by heart, the length used in the 1981 breathing study [@doust1981].
5. Ask yourself the same question every time: could I speak comfortably? Answer yes, not sure, or no. Settle the wording before you start [@gillespie2015].
6. Stop at the first "no". The test goes nowhere near your maximum.
7. The "not sure" stage is your estimate of the first threshold. The heart rate and power of the last clear "yes" are the top of your Zone 2. Taking the stage below allows for the test's lean towards passing people who are slightly over [@foster2008; @persinger2004].
8. Repeat it on another day, because heart rate at a fixed effort varies by several beats from one day to the next [@lamberts2004]. If the two results are within about five beats, use the lower. If they are not, do a third.

![A sketch of the step test. The powers are not data: the heights only show that each step is a little harder than the last.](figures/talk-test-steps.svg)

Then confirm the result on an ordinary ride. Spend an hour just under that heart rate in the same cool room. You should be able to talk in full sentences at the end, and your heart rate at a steady power should not climb much. If it climbs steadily, lower the ceiling by a few beats.

No study gives limits of agreement for a talk test run this way, so its error for one rider is unknown. The laboratory's two measures were about 10 beats apart in Leuven, and the talk test can't be shown to do better than that. Anyone with a heart condition, or on medication that alters heart rate such as beta blockers, should ask a doctor before testing or training by heart rate. The talk test asks for no maximal effort, and it has been used with cardiac patients [@reedpipe2014].

## The second threshold, and the maximum

Zone 2 needs only the first threshold. The hard sessions of Part III need the second, and a rider who wants percentages as a cross-check needs a true maximum.

Chapter 5 covered the 20-minute test: FTP matched the laboratory steady state on average in one study, overshot it in another, and can be 9% out for one rider [@borszcz2019; @inglis2020]. Critical power can also be found without a laboratory. In one validation study, all-out efforts of 12, 7 and 3 minutes in a single session, with 30 minutes between them, gave a critical power with a standard error of 4.5% against the laboratory figure and limits of agreement of −26 W to +29 W. The best 3-, 7- and 12-minute powers picked out of the riders' ordinary training and racing files did nearly as well, at 5.2% and −34 W to +44 W [@karsten2015]. The talk test reaches the second threshold too. In the 18 trained cyclists, the stage at which they could definitely not talk matched the second ventilatory threshold [@rodriguezmarroyo2013]. And the heartbeat index that failed at the first threshold did better at the second: in Leuven its 0.5 value came within 1 beat and 2 W of the second lactate threshold on average, with limits of 17 beats and 54 W either side [@olieslagers2026].

A once-popular home test for the second threshold has not lasted. In 1982 Francesco Conconi and colleagues had 210 runners circle a track at steadily rising speed while their heart rate was recorded. Heart rate climbed in a straight line with speed and then, near the top, bent away from the line. The paper reported the bend in every runner, and in the ten whose blood lactate was also measured it coincided with the lactate threshold [@conconi1982]. A threshold test that needed only a heart-rate monitor and a track became popular. Fifteen years later Andrew Jones and Jonathan Doust repeated it with 14 trained runners on a treadmill. The bend appeared in nine of them. Where it appeared it came at about 95% of maximum heart rate, 14 beats above the lactate turnpoint. Seven of the runners then ran just below each marker. All seven completed half an hour below the lactate turnpoint, and below the heart-rate bend they lasted 16 minutes on average. The authors' explanation was that the bend is heart rate beginning to level off at its maximum [@jonesdoust1997]. The original link to lactate had rested on ten runners out of 210.

A maximum heart rate should be measured. Robergs and Landwehr put the error of a properly measured maximum at about two beats, against ten or more for any formula [@robergs2002]. The highest reading from a chest strap at the end of a long climb ridden flat out, or at a race finish, is likely to be nearer the truth than any equation. It should be a cycling figure. In one set of 193 people tested both ways, the equation fitted to their cycling tests gave a maximum 13 beats lower at age 40 than the equation fitted to their treadmill tests [@robergs2002]. Those figures come from a conference summary quoted in Robergs and Landwehr's table, so they are second-hand. The maximum also moves a little with fitness. A review found that endurance training lowers it and a lay-off raises it, by 3% to 7% [@zavorsky2000]. Older riders, untrained riders and anyone with a heart condition should not go looking for a maximum without medical advice.

## Keeping the numbers right

How often to retest depends on which number. In 2000 Alejandro Lucía and colleagues tested 13 professional road cyclists three times in a season: in November during their break, in January, and in May when they were racing. The power at their thresholds rose as they got fitter. The heart rates hardly moved. At the first ventilatory threshold they averaged 155, 156 and 159 bpm across the three tests, and at the second 178, 173 and 176 [@lucia2000]. The authors concluded that one test at the start of the season is enough to set a professional's heart-rate targets for the year.

Those were professionals with many years of training behind them, and nobody has shown the same for a rider in a first season, whose heart may be changing faster. For an established rider the finding suggests that a heart-rate ceiling, once found, should last, and that the power that goes with it is what needs rechecking. Every couple of months, and after illness or a long break, is a reasonable interval, though no study sets one.

Within that, heart rate wobbles from day to day. Forty-four people who did the same submaximal running test at the same time on five consecutive days showed a spread of about 5 bpm at the stage where they were most consistent, near 90% of maximum, and more at the easier stages [@lamberts2004]. It also moves within a ride. In the Auckland study that Chapter 9 describes, two hours of easy riding raised the heart rate at the first threshold by 9 bpm and lowered the power by 21 W [@stevenson2022]. A ceiling written as a single number of beats claims more precision than any of these measurements has.

The lower edge of Zone 2 needs no such care. The 2025 panel expected much the same adaptations a little above and a little below it [@sitko2025], and Part II examines whether that is right. The top edge separates two domains, and it is the one worth a test.

The talk test is also the one method that goes out on the road with you. If you are riding at your Zone 2 heart rate and can't finish a sentence comfortably, the number is wrong for that day.
