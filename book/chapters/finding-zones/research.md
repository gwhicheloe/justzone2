# Finding your zones: research notes

Written 11 October 2026. Tags: [FT] full text; [Abs] abstract; [Rep] reported by a source I read; [GK] general knowledge, unchecked; [Own] my arithmetic or inference.

**How far each source was read.** Full text: Robergs & Landwehr 2002 (all ten pages, from the journal's own PDF, saved in `papers/`), Olieslagers 2026 (all), Rogers 2021 (methods, results, limitations, conflict statement), Tanner 2024 (results and conclusion of the two first-threshold meta-analyses, limitations, conflict statement). Meixner 2025, Jones 2019 and Stevenson 2022 were read for earlier chapters. **Everything else is abstract only**, including every talk-test study. **Not read at all:** Fox, Naughton & Haskell 1971 (what it contains is from Robergs & Landwehr); Karvonen 1957 (no abstract exists); Maffetone's page was read through a summarising tool.

The website post *How Do You Actually Find Your Zone 2?* covers the same ground. Its numbers were re-checked against abstracts or full texts for this chapter, and **two of them do not hold up** (see "Corrections to the website post" at the end).

## Second pass, 11 October: lactate first

**George's comment on the first draft:** too much on maximum heart rate and the bad ways of finding it. His understanding is that Zone 2 is about the lactate threshold, which can only really be determined by lactate testing (laboratory or a home meter). The book should be more opinionated: decide the best definition of the zones and say why. The chapter should lead with the lactate thresholds, what they are, the biology, and why zones matter; everything else is a proxy.

**What the rewrite does.** Leads with the first lactate threshold and why it matters; states the book's definition and argues for it; covers how to measure it in a laboratory and at home; then treats breathing, the talk test, heart rate variability, percentages, drift and feel as proxies, each judged against lactate where the evidence allows. The maximum heart rate material is cut to one paragraph and the `hrmax-age` figure is dropped. The Intensity chapter's definition passage and its list of terms were changed to match.

**The definition the draft adopts (my proposal, for George to confirm):** the top of Zone 2 is the last stage before blood lactate rises 0.5 mmol/L above the rider's own baseline, in a step test with stages of at least four minutes.

**Where the evidence does and does not back George's view. He should know this before the book commits:**
- Nothing I have read shows the lactate threshold to be a *better* boundary than the first ventilatory threshold. The 2025 panel named both. Jamnick 2020 finds "some evidence" for both as the lower boundary.
- In the one study that retested both, the breathing threshold was slightly *more* repeatable than the lactate threshold (Pallarés 2016: SD of test–retest differences 13 W against 19 W).
- The two differ: lactate was 0 W, 14 W and 24 W above breathing in the three cycling studies that measured both.
- No trial has compared training anchored to one against training anchored to the other. The choice is by mechanism, lineage and practicality: grade C.
- "Only determinable by lactate testing" is true once Zone 2 is defined by lactate. It also means the talk-test evidence, which was all collected against breathing, is one step removed.
- The lactate definition is protocol-bound: see Pallarés below.

### New sources for this pass

- Seiler, Haugen & Kuffel 2007 [Abs]: nine highly trained male runners (VO₂max 72; 14 h/week) and eight trained (VO₂max 60; 7 h/week). Highly trained did four sessions: 60 min and 120 min below VT1; 60 min including 30 min between VT1 and VT2 (lactate 2.7 ± 0.4 mmol/L); 60 min above VT2 (6 × 3 min at 96% VO₂max; 7.1 mmol/L). Supine heart rate variability followed for 4 h afterwards. Below VT1, 60 or 120 min: back to pre-training values within 5 to 10 min. Threshold and intervals: a "significant, but essentially identical" delay, back by about 30 min. Trained group after the intervals: 90 min or more. "The first ventilatory threshold may demarcate a 'binary' threshold for ANS/HRV recovery in highly trained athletes." **The boundary in this study was set by breathing, not lactate.** One marker of recovery (HRV), not performance.
- San-Millán & Brooks 2018 [Abs]: professional endurance athletes, moderately active people and patients with metabolic syndrome; indirect calorimetry and blood lactate during graded exercise. Fat oxidation higher and lactate lower in the professionals. Fat oxidation and lactate inversely correlated within each group (r = −0.97, −0.98, −0.92) and −0.76 across all points. They propose lactate and fat oxidation as an indirect measure of "metabolic flexibility and oxidative capacity". Group sizes are not in the abstract. Cross-sectional. That this paper is the source of the lactate-based Zone 2 of the podcasts: [GK], consistent with Storoschuk 2025.
- Pallarés 2016 [FT, now read: methods, results, both tables]: 14 trained male cyclists (VO₂max 62.1), two identical ramp tests (25 W per minute, finger blood every 2 minutes, i.e. every 50 W; Lactate Pro meter) and several 30-minute rides to find MLSS.
  - LT defined as "the highest workload not associated with a rise in lactate concentration above baseline"; other thresholds as fixed rises above baseline.
  - VT1 200 ± 36 W (lactate 1.1 mmol/L there); LT 214 ± 33 W (1.6 mmol/L); difference 14 W, not significant (p = 0.147), r = 0.72, SD of differences 34 W.
  - **Baseline + 0.5 mmol/L fell at 260 ± 36 W, the same as MLSS (255 ± 32 W; p = 0.449).** In a fast ramp the 0.5 rise marks the second threshold, not the first.
  - Test–retest: VT1 CV 3.5%, mean difference −2.9 W, SD of differences 13.3 W. LT CV 3.7%, mean difference −2.1 W, SD 18.7 W.
  - Two observers read every threshold, with a third if they disagreed.
- Jamnick 2018 [FT, from the Intensity notes]: 17 trained cyclists, MLSS 264 W. Baseline + 0.5 mmol/L from tests with 3-, 4-, 7- and 10-minute stages: 235, 229, 228, 225 W. Fixed 4.0 mmol/L: 306, 291, 281, 279 W. [Own] The first-rise rule moved 10 W across stage lengths; the fixed rule 27 W.
- Meixner 2025 [FT, Intensity notes]: baseline + 0.5 at 199 W, VT1 at 200 W, fixed 2.0 mmol/L at 211 W, 2.5 at 224 W; nine of 50 never below 1.5 mmol/L. Three-minute stages of 25 W.
- [Own] Lactate threshold minus VT1 in cyclists: 0 W (Meixner, −1), 14 W (Pallarés), 24 W and 9 bpm (Olieslagers).
- Tanner RK 2010 [Abs] (not the 2024 meta-analysis author): three portable analysers against a laboratory analyser. Typical error below 4 mmol/L: 0.1 mmol/L (Lactate Pro), 0.4 (Lactate Plus), 0.5 (Lactate Scout). "All portable analysers tended to under-read" the laboratory analyser. Limits of agreement up to 2.1 mmol/L across the whole range (1 to 18).
- Bonaventura 2015 [Abs and key points]: five portable analysers and one point-of-care device against a laboratory criterion, 22 blood samples from five people. Reliability "generally <0.5 mM" for 1 to 10 mmol/L. All tended to under-read; "the practical application of these negative biases would overestimate the ability of the athlete and prescribe a training intensity that would be too high". Analytical error within a brand "much smaller than the biological variation".
  - [Own] A constant under-reading cancels out of a rule based on a rise above the same meter's baseline, and does not cancel out of a fixed 2.0 mmol/L rule. That is an argument for the baseline rule; nobody has tested it.
- Rodríguez-Marroyo 2013 [Abs]: blood lactate was 1.3 ± 0.5 mmol/L at the talk test's equivocal stage and 1.6 ± 0.3 at VT; 4.2 at the negative stage and 4.0 at the respiratory compensation threshold. The only talk-test study here whose abstract reports lactate.
- Lucía 2000 [Abs]: heart rate at the *lactate* threshold 154, 152, 154 bpm across the season.
- **Unsourced in this pass [GK]:** how to take a clean sample (wash and dry the skin, wipe away sweat, discard the first drop, same site each time); that sweat contains lactate; what a meter and strips cost; that the original Zone 2 advice was framed around lactate below about 2 mmol/L.
- **"Home lactate tests":** I have taken George to mean handheld finger-prick meters. If he means something newer (a continuous sensor), I have not looked for evidence on those.

## The age formula (opening)

- Robergs & Landwehr 2002 [FT], a commentary in the *Journal of Exercise Physiology online*, University of New Mexico.
  - "There is no published record of research for this equation."
  - Their Table 2 lists where 5 research papers and 18 textbooks say the formula comes from. Of the 18 textbooks, 13 give no citation (one of the 13 is Robergs & Roberts 2000, his own); the other five cite five different sources (ACSM 2000, Londeree 1982, Miller 1993, Hagberg 1985, Asmussen 1959) [Own count from the table].
  - Textbooks tie the formula to Karvonen because it appears beside his heart rate reserve. Karvonen "was contacted in August of 2000" and said he never published research on the formula; he pointed them to Åstrand. Åstrand, in Albuquerque in September 2000 to receive a lifetime achievement award, said he had published no data deriving it, but had said in presentations that such a formula "appears close to research findings" and would be convenient.
  - "The correct citation for the origin" is Fox, Naughton & Haskell 1971, "a large review of research pertaining to physical activity and heart disease". A figure with "approximately 35 data points"; the abstract says the data came from "approximately 11 references consisting of published research or unpublished scientific compilations" (the legend of their redrawn figure lists ten named sources and "Unknown"). No regression was done. Fox's legend, as quoted: "no single line will adequately represent the data on the apparent decline of maximal heart rate with age. The formula maximum heart rate=220–age in years defines a line not far from many of the data points".
  - Robergs & Landwehr read the points off the figure and fitted a line: HRmax = 215.4 − 0.9147 × age, r = 0.51, standard error 21 beats/min.
  - First known prediction equation: Robinson 1938, 212 − 0.77 × age.
  - They collated 43 formulae. Standard errors of the single-variable equations in healthy samples in their Table 3 are mostly about 10 beats/min (Bruce 10, Froelicher 10, Fernhall 9.9, Jones 10.3, Whaley 10.5 and 10.7; Inbar 6.4 is the lowest).
  - A measured maximum is "likely to be accurate to within ±2 b/min, if the subject truly attains maximal exertion".
  - For setting training ranges, they judge errors of 8 beats or less "likely to be acceptable".
  - Conclusion 1: "Currently, there is no acceptable method to estimate HRmax."
  - Exercise mode: Ricard 1990 (a conference abstract; 193 women and men), from Table 3 [Rep]: treadmill 209 − 0.587 × age, cycle ergometer 200 − 0.687 × age. At age 40 that is 185.5 against 172.5, a gap of 13 beats [Own]. One conference abstract, second-hand: weak.
  - Fox, Naughton and Haskell's first names (Samuel, John, William): [GK].
- Tanaka 2001 [Abs]: meta-analysis of 351 studies, 492 groups, 18,712 subjects: 208 − 0.7 × age (r = −0.90 between group means and age). Laboratory check in 514 healthy people: 209 − 0.7 × age. No difference by sex or habitual activity. 220 − age underestimates in older adults. No standard error for individuals in the abstract.
- Nes 2013 [Abs], the HUNT Fitness Study: 3,320 healthy men and women with a verified maximal effort; 211 − 0.64 × age; standard error of estimate 10.8 beats/min; no interaction with sex, activity, VO₂max or BMI. That HUNT is Norwegian: [GK].
- [Own] If errors are roughly normal with a standard deviation of 10.8, about two-thirds of people fall within 11 beats of the prediction and 95% within about 21. So one person in three is more than 11 beats out and one in twenty more than 21. For a 50-year-old: 220 − 50 = 170; 211 − 32 = 179; 95% range about 158 to 200.
- [Own] For a 60-year-old: 220 − 60 = 160; 208 − 42 = 166; 211 − 38.4 = 172.6, written as 173.
- Zavorsky 2000 [Abs], a review: maximum heart rate falls with endurance training and rises with detraining and tapering; "can be altered by 3 to 7%"; effect sizes −0.48 and +0.54; the author asks for caution because research is thin.
- "Many heart-rate monitors and apps use 220 − age by default": [GK]. I have not checked any manufacturer's documentation.

## Percentages of a maximum

- Iannetta 2020 and Meixner 2025: in the Intensity notes. Used here by reference only (60 to 90% of HRmax; 81 ± 3.9%). The figure now uses Meixner's lactate row, 81.2 ± 3.0% (see "The percent-of-maximum figure, redrawn").
- [Own] Meixner's cyclists: VT1 at 81.4% of a mean HRmax of 179 is 146 bpm; 70% is 125 bpm; the top of the 60–70% band is about 20 beats below the average threshold. The Norwegian Zone 2 (72–82%) is about 18 beats wide at that maximum, the basis for "twenty beats is about the width of a heart-rate zone".
- "Zone 2 = 60 to 70% of maximum heart rate" as the common rule: [GK]. It is the second band of the five equal bands (50–60, 60–70 …) printed on many gym machines and used by many apps. **No source.** The website post uses the same figure, also unsourced.
- Scharhag-Rosenberger 2010 [Abs]: 21 healthy men, VO₂max 59.9 ± 11.8; 60 minutes at 60% and at 75% of VO₂max, work rate adjusted to hold VO₂ constant. Blood lactate at 60%: 2.1 ± 1.1 mmol/L, range 0.7 to 5.6. At 75%: 4.6 ± 1.9, range 2.2 to 8.0. No significant difference between three subgroups of seven split by VO₂max.
- Persinger 2004 [Abs] gives the ventilatory threshold on the cycle ergometer at 82% of peak heart rate and 74% of heart rate reserve (16 healthy volunteers).
- Heart rate reserve as Karvonen's idea: Robergs & Landwehr [FT]. What Karvonen's 1957 study did (I recall six young men training on a treadmill, and a threshold of 60% of the reserve): **unverified**; a web search confirmed only the 60% figure, second-hand. Not used.
- Maffetone's 180 formula [Rep, read through a summarising tool on 11 October 2026]: 180 minus age; subtract 10 (major illness, medication), subtract 5 (injury, regression, frequent infections, inconsistent training), no change (training consistently up to two years), add 5 (more than two years without problems and improving). The page cites no study against lactate or ventilatory thresholds; it says the formula was developed from his own one-to-one evaluations.

## How sharp the laboratory is

- Yeh 1983 [Abs]: eight normal subjects, ramp of 20 W/min on a cycle ergometer. "Four independent exercise physiologists determined AT from the gas response data. The reviewer variability (avg range 16%) of AT for a given subject was representative of AT values reported for untrained and trained individuals (40–70% maximum O2 consumption)." Conclusion: reviewer variability too large for clinical use. **The abstract does not say 16% of what.** The draft reports it as "an average range of 16%" and repeats the authors' comparison.
- Olieslagers 2026 [FT], for the same 21 riders (details under "HRV"): VT1 at 144 ± 10 bpm and 156 ± 18 W; LT1 (baseline + 0.5 mmol/L) at 153 ± 14 bpm and 180 ± 37 W. Mean difference 9 bpm (limits of agreement −30 to +11 for VT1 minus LT1) and 24 W (−81 to +33); r = 0.51 for heart rate, 0.65 for power; ICC 0.53 and 0.40. Significant by ANOVA (p = 0.002, 0.008). Ventilatory thresholds were read by two examiners, twice each on separate days, with a third brought in when they differed by more than 100 mL/min and could not agree. The authors: "gold-standard methods themselves exhibit variability and have limitations, often also demonstrating mean biases and wide LOA when compared with one another."
- In the Intensity notes: Pallarés 2016 (gap about 14 W, r = 0.72), Meixner 2025 (200 against 199 W), Faude 2009 (25 concepts), Jamnick 2018 (stage length changes the answer).

## The talk test

- Origin. Woltmann 2015 [Rep, full text read through a summarising tool from a third-party copy]: "The concept of the TT has been around since 1939, when Professor Grayson in the United Kingdom advised mountaineers to 'climb no faster than you can speak.'" **No reference is given for this.** A coaching website says he was at Oxford. I could not find a primary source. The draft says "is credited".
- Doust & Patrick 1981 [Abs]: six subjects, steady treadmill exercise at five speeds; a printed 100-word passage read aloud in the fifth minute, taking about 30 s. "At each exercise level ventilation was reduced during speech to about 55% of the control value"; at the highest level by 38 L/min from 78 L/min. Breathing frequency fell, tidal volume did not change. 24% of ventilation during speech at the heaviest level was silent (non-phonated) expirations. Afterwards ventilation overshot by about 14% and returned to control within 30 s. Interpretation: the speech requirement for restricted expiratory airflow takes "precedence" over gas exchange.
- Meckel 2002 [Abs]: 14 subjects running at 65, 75 and 85% of VO₂max with and without talking. Talking: oxygen uptake, ventilation and breathing frequency down; blood lactate, blood pressure and end-tidal CO₂ up; heart rate and tidal volume unchanged. No sizes of effect in the abstract.
- **The link from these to the ventilatory threshold is the standard explanation, and my wording of it is inference [Own]:** below the first threshold there is spare ventilation to give up for half a minute; above it, the extra CO₂ from buffering (Intensity chapter) raises the demand and the shortfall is felt.
- Persinger 2004 [Abs]: 16 healthy volunteers, incremental tests on treadmill and cycle ergometer, with gas exchange and with the talk test. Ventilatory threshold on treadmill against cycle: 77 / 67% of VO₂peak, 89 / 82% of peak heart rate, 84 / 74% of heart rate reserve. Equivocal stage: 83 / 73% of VO₂peak, 86 / 87% of peak heart rate, 80 / 81% of heart rate reserve. "Not significantly different." Negative stage "consistently above" VT. Conclusion: "almost exactly equivalent".
  - [Own] On the bike the equivocal stage sat 5 percentage points of peak heart rate above the threshold (87 against 82), about 9 beats for a peak of 180. With 16 people, "not significantly different" is weak evidence of no difference (the Durability chapter's box on null results applies). No limits of agreement in the abstract.
- Foster 2008 [Abs]: healthy young adults, four series of experiments: blood donation (lowers VT), training (raises it), and intensities set above and below VT. Changes in intensity at VT and at the last positive stage "matched each other" after both. "Predictive errors biased toward passing the TT when exercise intensity was greater than VT." Time for the test to turn negative above VT "averaged more than the 2 minutes that has been used in previous studies". No numbers of subjects or sizes of change in the abstract.
- Rodríguez-Marroyo 2013 [Abs]: 18 highly trained cyclists; same protocol twice, once with gas exchange, once reading "a standard paragraph" at the end of each stage. Equivocal = first stage where they "could not talk comfortably"; negative = "could definitely not talk". VT against equivocal: 3.7 ± 0.4 against 3.6 ± 0.4 W/kg; 150 ± 10 against 148 ± 12 bpm; lactate 1.6 against 1.3 mmol/L. Respiratory compensation threshold against negative: 5.3 against 5.3 W/kg; 177 ± 7 against 176 ± 10 bpm; lactate 4.0 against 4.2. Correlations for VT/equivocal: 0.86 (workload), 0.79 (heart rate). Foster is a co-author.
- Gillespie 2015 [Abs]: 12 highly trained male cyclists, VO₂max 65.9, HRmax 187 ± 11. Participants answered "Yes", "I'm not sure" or "No" to whether they could speak comfortably. Last positive stage: 129 ± 19 bpm, 68.6 ± 7.9% HRmax, 49.9% of VO₂max, RPE 11.1, all significantly below VT. Equivocal: 147 ± 17 bpm, 78.5% HRmax, RPE 13.6 (heart rate and RPE not different from VT; VO₂, at 67.2% of max, was lower). Negative: 156 ± 14 bpm, 83.1% HRmax, 73.9% of VO₂max, RPE 14.8, not different from VT. Conclusion: the negative stage estimated VT. The values at VT itself are not in the abstract.
  - [Own] Rodríguez-Marroyo's equivocal stage and Gillespie's fall at nearly the same heart rate (148 and 147). The two papers differ in where the negative stage falls (176 against 156 bpm) and so in which stage they recommend. The likeliest reason is the wording: "definitely could not talk" against "No" to "can you speak comfortably?". The size of the steps in Gillespie's protocol is not in the abstract; the 18-beat gap between last positive and equivocal suggests they were large.
- Woltmann 2015 [Abs]: 16 physically active volunteers; talk-test stages found in an incremental run, then 30-minute runs with speed adjusted to hold each stage. All 16 completed the last positive stage; 13 of 16 the equivocal; 2 of 16 the negative. [Rep, via summarising tool, not in the abstract]: stages were 2 minutes with the test after 1.5 minutes; the passage was the Pledge of Allegiance; lactate stayed near resting at last positive, stabilised late at equivocal and rose throughout at negative. Only the abstract's numbers are used in the draft.
  - [Own] If three in sixteen could not hold the equivocal intensity for half an hour, then with 2-minute stages it lay well above the first threshold for those runners. This fits Foster 2008's finding that short stages pass people who are above threshold.
- Reed & Pipe 2014 [Abs], a review: valid and reliable in athletes, healthy adults and cardiac patients; consistent across modes.
- Bok 2022 [Abs], a review of subjective methods, with Foster as an author: equivocal stage, RPE 10–11 and Feeling Scale +1 to +3 "are reflective of" VT; negative stage, RPE 13–15 and Feeling Scale around 0, of the respiratory compensation threshold. Responses at a fixed Feeling Scale rating "show extensive variability around ventilatory threshold", so it does not mark the boundary well. Note that Gillespie's cyclists gave RPE 13.6 at the equivocal stage, above Bok's 10–11.
- Counting talk test. Norman 2008 [Abs]: 40 young adults (22.5 years), treadmill; the count during exercise as a percentage of the resting count correlated −0.93 with % heart rate reserve and −0.92 with % VO₂ reserve; counting to "half to one third" of the resting value matched ACSM's moderate to vigorous range. Loose 2012 [Abs]: 36 people, four modes of exercise; resting count under 25 → 40–50% of it; 25 or more → 30–40%. **How the count is done (aloud, on one breath, "one-one-thousand…") is not in either abstract: [GK].** Neither study compared it with a measured threshold.

## Nose breathing and feel (side roads)

- Niinimaa 1980 [Abs]: 30 healthy adults (14 men, 16 women), graded submaximal exercise, nasal airflow measured with a nasal mask. 20 switched from nose to nose-and-mouth breathing at 105 ± 30 W, ventilation 35.3 ± 10.8 L/min; four breathed through the mouth habitually; five breathed through the nose throughout; one inconsistent. Switch point "quite consistent individually", varied a lot between people; most closely related to perceived exertion of breathing and nasal work of breathing.
- I searched Europe PMC for a comparison of the nose-to-mouth switch with a measured first threshold and found none. Absence from one search, not proof.
- Ekkekakis 2008 [Abs]: 30 young adults (14 women, 16 men); 15-minute treadmill sessions below, at and above their ventilatory threshold; pleasure rated repeatedly during exercise. Intensity did not change the improvement from before to after; during exercise, above the threshold there were "significant and relatively homogeneous decreases in pleasure".

## HRV (DFA alpha 1)

- Rogers 2021 [FT] (published online 2020/21, *Frontiers in Physiology*): 17 male volunteers aged 19 to 52, two excluded for atrial ectopic beats, 15 analysed. Treadmill, Bruce protocol (3-minute stages, speed and gradient both rising). Polar H7 strap. The 0.75 cut-off "was chosen based on this being the midpoint" between 1.0 (correlated, very light exercise) and 0.5 (uncorrelated, "white noise", high intensity). VT1 at 152 ± 21 bpm against 154 ± 20 at the HRV threshold; r = 0.97 for heart rate; mean difference −1.9 bpm, limits of agreement −12 to +8. Heart rate at VT1 ranged from 108 to 183 bpm across the 15. Limitations stated: no women; transfer to constant-load exercise untested; day-to-day repeatability untested. One author employed by a climbing-training company (Lattice Training); others declare none.
  - [Own] A range of 108 to 183 bpm at VT1 across 15 people guarantees a high correlation (the Intensity chapter's point).
- Rogers 2024 [Abs]: 14 participants, ramp test. Gas exchange threshold 131 ± 11 bpm; standard 0.75 threshold 146 ± 22 (p = 0.005); custom threshold (midway between the individual's early-ramp maximum and 0.5) 132 ± 17. Correlations 0.67 to 0.70. Opening sentence: the 0.75 threshold "has shown inconsistent agreement".
- Tanner 2024 [FT, parts]: 50 studies, 1,160 subjects. First threshold: 22 studies; standardised mean difference 0.08 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.19), prediction interval −0.43 to 0.59; pooled r = 0.84 in the text (0.85 in the abstract), prediction interval 0.06 to 0.99; 89% and 93% of variance from real differences between studies. "HRVT1 better agreed with LT1." Most subjects young healthy men; slight publication bias not ruled out. No conflicts; open-access fee paid by the University of Lausanne.
- Olieslagers 2026 [FT], KU Leuven: 24 physically active men recruited, 21 analysed (age 22.6 ± 3.4; 10.1 ± 4.5 h of sport a week; VO₂peak 55.8 ± 6.0). One visit: cycling step test, 4-minute stages, from 40 W, +30 W per stage, to exhaustion; gas exchange throughout; earlobe lactate after each stage; Polar H10 chest strap, signal quality checked beforehand; room about 17.5 °C. Thresholds read blind to the other measures; ventilatory thresholds by examiners independent of those reading the HRV thresholds.
  - Table 2 (mean ± SD), heart rate / power: VT1 144 ± 10 / 156 ± 18; LT1 153 ± 14 / 180 ± 37; HRVT1 (0.75) 161 ± 11 / 201 ± 34; personalised HRVT1 153 ± 11 / 177 ± 33; VT2 166 ± 9 / 226 ± 29; LT2 172 ± 11 / 234 ± 33; HRVT2 (0.5) 173 ± 10 / 236 ± 33.
  - Table 3. VT1 minus HRVT1: −17 bpm (limits −41 to +7), r = 0.36; −45 W (−117 to +27). LT1 minus HRVT1: −8 bpm (−36 to +21), r = 0.07, not significant (p = 0.150); −21 W (−100 to +58). Personalised: −9 bpm against VT1 (−33 to +15); 0 against LT1 (−29 to +30).
  - Table 4. LT2 against HRVT2: −1 bpm (−18 to +16), −2 W (−56 to +52). VT2 against HRVT2: −6 bpm (−24 to +11). VT2 against LT2: −6 bpm (−18 to +7), −8 W (−41 to +24).
  - **Direction:** the HRV threshold came out *higher* than VT1 (161 against 144 bpm), i.e. it would put the top of Zone 2 17 beats too high.
  - Limitations stated: men only; 4-minute stages may have compromised the ventilatory thresholds; no test–retest of the HRV thresholds. Funded by a KU Leuven grant; no conflicts.
  - [Own] 17 against 8: whether the method "fails" depends on the reference chosen.

## Drift

- Coyle & González-Alonso 2001 [Abs]: cardiovascular drift is a progressive fall in stroke volume after 10 to 20 minutes of exercise, which they attribute mainly to the rise in heart rate.
- The "5% rule" (a rise in heart rate of under 5% between halves of an hour at steady power means you were at or below the first threshold): [GK], from coaching sources. I searched Europe PMC for a study testing it against a measured threshold and found none. Absence from one search.
- Durability draft: untrained people drifted 5 to 9% over three hours (Matomäki 2023); heat and dehydration raise drift. Refer back; do not repeat.

## A home lactate meter

- Jones 2019 [FT, Intensity notes]: analyser error 0.2 to 0.4 mmol/L. Faude 2009: 25 concepts. Nothing new read for this chapter. Kept to a paragraph.

## The step test

- No study has tested this protocol as a whole. Each choice and its basis:
  - 4-minute stages: Foster 2008 (more than 2 minutes for speech to become uncomfortable above VT); Woltmann 2015 (2-minute stages put the equivocal stage too high for some). Olieslagers used 4-minute stages for lactate.
  - Steps of 10 to 15 W: so that the last comfortable step and the uncertain one are close together [Own reasoning, from Gillespie's 18-beat gap].
  - A passage of about 100 words taking about 30 seconds: Doust & Patrick 1981.
  - Taking the last clear "yes" as the ceiling: Foster 2008 (errors biased towards passing); Persinger 2004 (equivocal stage 5 points above VT on the bike).
  - Repeating on another day: Lamberts 2004 (day-to-day variation).
  - Starting "around half of FTP": from the website post, no source. [GK]

## Second threshold and maximum

- Karsten 2015 [Abs]: critical power from field tests against a laboratory test. Maximal efforts of 12, 7 and 3 minutes in one session with 30-minute recoveries: standard error 4.5%, limits of agreement −26 to +29 W. Best 3-, 7- and 12-minute powers taken from training and racing files: standard error 5.2%, limits −34 to +44 W. Number of cyclists not in the abstract. Beedie is a co-author (the placebo study in How to read a training study).
- Borszcz 2019, Inglis 2020: Intensity notes.
- Conconi 1982 [Abs]: 210 runners on a 400 m track, speed rising in steps, heart rate by ECG. "In all athletes examined" heart rate deflected from its straight line at high speed. The speed at the deflection matched the lactate "anaerobic threshold" in the 10 runners in whom lactate was measured.
- Jones & Doust 1997 [Abs]: 14 trained male distance runners, treadmill. Deflection seen in nine. It came at 186 ± 9 bpm against 172 ± 10 at the lactate turnpoint, "approximately 95% of maximum heart rate". Seven runners ran just below each marker: all completed 30 minutes below the lactate turnpoint (final lactate 2.4 mmol/L); below the deflection they lasted 15.9 ± 6.7 minutes (8.1 mmol/L). Conclusion: not valid; the deflection is the start of the plateau at maximum heart rate.
- [Own] 186 − 172 = 14 beats; 15.9 minutes is written as 16.
- Bodner & Rhodes 2000 [Abs], a review: validity "uncertain"; the degree of deflection depends heavily on protocol.

## Keeping the numbers right

- Lucía 2000 [Abs]: 13 professional road cyclists (VO₂max about 75), ramp tests in November, January and May. Power at the thresholds rose significantly; heart rates did not: at the lactate threshold 154, 152, 154 bpm; at VT1 155, 156, 159; at VT2 178, 173, 176. Conclusion: one test at the start of the season may be enough to set heart-rate targets.
  - [Own] Whether this holds for a novice gaining fitness fast is not shown.
- Lamberts 2004 [Abs]: 44 people, a submaximal running test at the same time of day on five consecutive days. At the stage with the least variation (about 90% of maximum) heart rate varied by 5 ± 2 beats/min across the days; standard error of measurement 1.1 to 1.4%. Lower stages varied more (no figures in the abstract).
- Stevenson 2022 [FT, Durability notes]: after two hours the heart rate at VT1 was 151 against 142 bpm fresh.

## What counts as the baseline (George's comment on the step-test figure, 11 October 2026)

George asked of the sketch: "how come lactate goes down from stage 1 to stage 2?" The sketch's values are made up (1.1, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.9, 2.8); the dip of 0.1 was put there to look like real readings. Checking the answer showed that the draft had claimed more than the papers say about the baseline.

- Pallarés 2016 [FT]: "Baseline lactate concentration was the average during the initial stages with values 0.5 mMol·L-1 above rest state." An average of the early stages. (The wording is the paper's; I read it as stages within 0.5 of the resting value.)
- Meixner 2025 [FT]: the method is written "Baseline blood lactate concentration + 0.5 mmol/L (BLamin+0.5)". The text gives no further definition. [Own] The subscript "min" points to a minimum, the lowest reading or the lactate at the minimum lactate equivalent; the paper does not say which. Blood was sampled before the test and in the last 15 s of every stage.
- Olieslagers 2026 [FT]: "LT1 was defined as the point at which blood lactate concentration increased by 0.5 mmol/L above baseline (Fleitas-Paniagua et al.)", computed with code from exphyslab.com on a third-degree polynomial fit. The baseline is not defined in the text.
- Jamnick 2018 [FT]: "The intensity at which blood lactate concentration increased 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 mmol.L-1 above baseline value(s)". Not defined further.
- **The earlier draft said the baseline "is the average of the flat stages … the rule used in the Würzburg and Leuven studies". That was wrong as a description of those two papers.** Corrected: the draft now says the papers differ, and that the book takes the average.
- [Own] The book's choice of the average: one stray low reading from a handheld meter (typical error 0.1 to 0.5 mmol/L, Tanner RK 2010 [Abs]) moves an average less than a minimum. Cost, stated in the draft: an average is never lower than the minimum, so it can place the threshold a stage later. No study I have read compares the two.
- [GK] Lactate is often said to fall over the first stage or two of a step test, because resting or pre-test lactate sits a little above the level reached in light exercise. **I have no source for this.** Two Europe PMC searches on 11 October found nothing usable. It is not in the draft, which attributes early wobble only to the meter's error. The automatic reply posted on George's comment gave this explanation, and a meter accuracy of "0.2 to 0.3 mmol/L" that is not from this chapter's sources (ours say typical error 0.1 to 0.5).
- Figure `lactate-steps.svg`: dip kept, and a line added under the plot saying the first four readings differ by a tenth or two and that their average, 1.1, is the baseline.
