# Chapter 10: Beyond performance

Between 1991 and 2014 the Cleveland Clinic put 122,007 patients through a treadmill test, and in 2018 its cardiologists checked how many of them had since died [@mandsager2018]. In the quarter of patients least fit for their age and sex, 23.7% had. Among the 3,570 whose fitness put them in the top 2.3%, the figure was 2.6%. After adjusting for age, smoking, diabetes, heart disease and the rest, the least fit carried five times the risk of death of the fittest. The authors wrote that being unfit was as dangerous as smoking or diabetes, or more so [@mandsager2018].

Figures like these are why Zone 2 is sold as medicine as well as training. Fitness predicts lifespan better than most things a doctor measures, steady riding builds fitness, and so an hour in Zone 2 is presented as an hour added to a life.

Fit and active people do live longer. How much of the difference comes from training, rather than the kind of person who becomes fit, is not known. The one long randomised trial found no significant reduction in deaths from adding supervised training to advice to follow activity guidelines. Trials do show that training lowers blood pressure and blood sugar. In the surveys, most of the lower risk arrives with the first few hours a week, well below a six-to-ten-hour rider's volume. The health evidence gives that rider no reason to favour Zone 2 over intervals.

## Fit people live longer

The Cleveland figures have limits that the headline leaves out. Fitness was not measured directly but estimated from the speed and slope of the treadmill when each patient stopped. And the patients were people sent for a test, mostly to look for heart disease or explain symptoms. Patients who had a drug-based stress test instead of exercising, 38,828 of them, were left out, as were those switched to one because they could not keep going [@mandsager2018].

The comparison with smoking also deserves a second look. The fivefold difference sets the least fit quarter against the top 2.3%, which is not the same kind of comparison as smokers against non-smokers. The smaller step, from just below average fitness to just above it, carried a hazard ratio of 1.41, exactly the figure for smoking [@mandsager2018]. A hazard ratio is how many times more often one group died at any point in the follow-up than the other, so 1.41 means 41% more. That smaller step is the one more readers could actually take, and it is still a large difference.

Surveys of activity tell the same story more modestly, because activity is a looser measure than fitness. In 2015 a group from the US National Cancer Institute pooled six cohorts in the United States and Europe: 661,137 adults, followed for a median of 14 years, who between them had 116,686 deaths [@arem2015]. They used the American guidelines as a yardstick: a minimum of 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week. People were grouped by how many multiples of that minimum they reported in their leisure time.

Even activity below the minimum went with 20% lower risk than none. At one to two times the minimum the figure was 31%, at two to three times 37%, and at three to five times 39%, after which the curve went flat: five to ten times the minimum also gave 39% [@arem2015]. People who reported ten times or more had 31% lower risk than the inactive, and the authors found no evidence of harm at that level. Their summary was that meeting the minimum gave nearly the maximum benefit [@arem2015].

Riding hours can be placed on that curve, roughly. A MET is the body's resting rate of energy use, about 3.5 millilitres of oxygen per kilogram per minute. Take a rider with a VO₂max of 55 mL/kg/min, close to the amateurs in the trials of Chapters 15 and 16. At 60% of it, which is near the first lactate rise in the two studies of Chapter 7's fat-max figure, that rider uses about 33 mL/kg/min, or 9.4 METs. An hour of that is 9.4 MET-hours against the surveys' weekly minimum of 7.5 [@arem2015], so six hours a week is about seven and a half times the minimum and ten hours about twelve and a half. A rider on six to ten hours is somewhere between the flat part of the curve and the far end of the data, where few of the people surveyed were. The surveys asked about walking, gardening, running and the like, and gave each activity a standard value, so this is a placing, not a calculation the study made.

The lowest intensities carry an association too. A 2019 analysis pooled eight cohorts in which 36,383 people wore movement sensors instead of describing their own activity. Over about six years, the most active quarter had 73% lower risk of death than the least active. Light activity on its own, the kind involved in slow walking and housework, followed the same pattern: 40%, 56% and 62% lower risk in the second, third and fourth quarters [@ekelund2019]. Time spent sitting ran the other way, with 2.63 times the risk in the most sedentary quarter.

Cycling has its own figures. In UK Biobank, 263,450 people who said how they travelled to work were followed for five years. Those who cycled had 41% lower risk of death from any cause than those who drove or took public transport, after adjustment for the usual factors. Those who walked had lower risk of heart disease but no significant difference in deaths overall {shadow @celismorales2017}.

That fit and active people live longer is one of the most consistent findings in epidemiology. That the training is the reason is a separate claim. {grade C}

### Reading the research: marker or cause

The question is as old as the field. In the 1950s Jeremy Morris and his colleagues in London found less coronary heart disease among the conductors of double-decker buses, who climbed the stairs all day, than among the drivers who sat, and less among postmen than among civil servants at desks {shadow @paffenbarger2001}. Morris knew the obvious objection, that heavier and less healthy men might choose the driver's seat, and his group went on to study the physique of busmen from the sizes of the uniforms they were issued {shadow @heady1956}.

The Cleveland authors could not tell whether high fitness picked out patients who were going to live longer anyway or made them live longer [@mandsager2018]. Illness, habits and inheritance could each contribute to the association.

The first is illness. People who are already becoming ill, before any diagnosis, walk less far on a treadmill and also die sooner. Leaving out the patients who could not exercise removes the most obvious cases and leaves the hidden ones in.

The second is habit. People who exercise also tend to smoke less, eat differently and go to the doctor, and adjustment catches only what was recorded.

The third is inheritance. Chapter 2 described how differently people respond to the same training, and some of the fitness a person has is fitness they were born with. If the same genes that make a person fit also protect them in other ways, fitness would predict a long life without training having anything to do with it.

Twins offer a test of the third. Finland has followed same-sex twins born before 1958 for decades, and asked about their exercise in 1975, 1981 and 1990. Across individuals, those who kept up vigorous exercise through all three surveys had 45% lower risk of death to 2013 than those who never did, and 27% lower after adjusting for education, smoking, alcohol, weight, work and health [@karvinen2015]. Then the researchers found the pairs in which one twin exercised throughout and the other never did. Among non-identical twins, who share about half their genes, the active twin had 42% lower risk. Among identical twins, who share all of them, there was no difference at all: a hazard ratio of 1.00 [@karvinen2015]. For the twin on the sofa, it is the best news in this chapter. An earlier analysis of the same cohort, over a shorter run of years, had found the same pattern {shadow @waller2010}.

The identical-twin result rests on 34 pairs, and its 95% confidence interval runs from about half the risk to about twice it [@karvinen2015]. Thirty-four pairs cannot show that exercise does nothing. They are, though, the nearest thing in the observational data to a controlled comparison, and the benefit that every other analysis shows does not appear in them. The authors suggest that some of the same genes influence how much people exercise, how fit they are and how long they live. {grade C}

A hazard ratio compares groups that differ in many ways; it does not directly measure what happens when someone changes their behaviour. Fitness reliably marks out people who live longer. The trials below can tell us more about what training itself changes.

## A trial of exercise and death

The only way to settle the question is a randomised trial, and death is rare enough that a trial needs thousands of people, years of follow-up or a population old enough to die in useful numbers. Researchers in Trondheim chose the third.

### One study, up close: Generation 100

In 2012 they invited every resident of the city aged 70 to 77, 6,966 people, to five years of training, and 1,567 agreed [@stensvold2020].

The confidence intervals show how little five years could settle. Against the control group, the interval group's best estimate was 37% fewer deaths, with an interval running from 67% fewer to 20% more. The moderate group's was 24% more, from 27% fewer to a little over twice as many [@stensvold2020].

The trial had the problem that every long exercise trial has: the control group would not sit still. At five years 69% of them still met the guidelines, about one in five had taken up interval training on their own, and only about half of each exercise group kept to its programme. The volunteers were also healthier than their neighbours. Deaths in the control group ran at less than half the 10% expected over five years among Norwegians of that age. The authors accept that a population this active may have had little room to gain [@stensvold2020]. A null result in people who were all exercising is not evidence that exercise does nothing.

Five years of supervised training added no measurable survival benefit for healthy, active 70-year-olds beyond following the guidelines. {grade B} The moderate sessions, at about 72% of peak heart rate and rated somewhat hard, were the nearest the trial came to steady Zone 2, although nobody measured whether they were below each person's first threshold. The interval group ended with higher peak oxygen uptake than both others and better scores for physical and mental quality of life [@stensvold2020]. There were no heart problems during supervised sessions. The three injuries were fractures from falls on slippery ground, two of them in the moderate group.

## What training does to the risk factors

Blood pressure falls with endurance training. A 2013 analysis of 93 randomised trials involving 5,223 healthy adults found an average fall of 3.5 mmHg in systolic and 2.5 mmHg in diastolic pressure [@cornelissen2013]. The size depended on where people started: 8.3 and 5.2 mmHg in people with high blood pressure, about 2 in those slightly above normal, and in people with normal pressure a fall of less than a millimetre in systolic pressure that could have been zero. {grade A}

Blood sugar falls too. In 47 trials of people with type 2 diabetes, structured exercise lowered HbA1c, a measure of average blood sugar over the previous two or three months, by 0.67 percentage points against controls [@umpierre2011]. Amount mattered: programmes of more than 150 minutes a week lowered it by 0.89 points and shorter ones by 0.36. Advice to exercise, without a programme, changed nothing unless it came with dietary advice. {grade A}

On intensity, the trials in patients lean the other way from the Zone 2 argument. In ten trials of 273 people with heart disease, high blood pressure, metabolic syndrome or obesity, interval training raised peak oxygen uptake by 3.03 mL/kg/min more than moderate continuous training, which the authors describe as almost double the gain {shadow @weston2014}. In 50 studies of interval training, insulin resistance fell more after intervals than after continuous training, by a small to moderate margin [@jelleyman2015]. Both sets of trials were small and short, and their authors call for longer ones. {grade B}

Lower blood pressure and blood sugar give a reason to expect fewer heart attacks and strokes. These trials did not count those events, so they cannot tell us how much the risk fell.

## Zone 2 as medicine, weighed

The case for Zone 2 as medicine starts from metabolic disease, in which the muscles burn fat poorly and let lactate build up. If Zone 2 is the intensity that best trains a muscle to burn fat and clear lactate, the argument goes, it must be the best exercise for metabolic health, and through that for a long life.

The observation about diseased muscle comes from the 2018 comparison in Chapters 6 and 7, which trained nobody {shadow @sanmillan2018}. The Canadian review concluded that Zone 2 had not been shown to be the best intensity for either mitochondria or fitness {shadow @storoschuk2025}. Nor do the trials above show it to be better: intervals did at least as well on fitness and insulin resistance, and the mortality trial's non-significant trend favoured intervals.

What the argument gets right is that steady exercise is effective medicine. It lowers blood pressure and blood sugar, it is safe for nearly anyone, and it can be done in amounts that hard training cannot, which matters because the diabetes trials found that amount counted. For a person starting from nothing, with a heart condition, or who simply will not do hard sessions, steady riding is a sound prescription. That is a long way from saying it is a special one. {grade C}

## Heart rhythm and arteries in lifelong athletes

Tour de France riders give the most extreme cycling data. Of the 786 French riders who started the race at least once between 1947 and 2012, 208 had died by 2012. Their death rate was 41% lower than that of Frenchmen of the same age, with fewer deaths from both cancer and heart disease [@marijon2013]. The authors could not separate the effect of the riding from the selection of unusually healthy young men, or weigh it against the effects of very heavy training and doping.

The costs show up in the heart's rhythm and its arteries. Among 52,755 people who completed the Vasaloppet, Sweden's 90 km cross-country ski race, between 1989 and 1998, those who had finished five or more times had 29% higher risk of atrial fibrillation than those who had finished once. Atrial fibrillation is an irregular rhythm of the heart's upper chambers. They also had about twice the risk of an abnormally slow heart rhythm [@andersen2013]. There was no link with the dangerous rhythms that cause cardiac arrest. The finding is stated for men.

In 284 men around 55 years old who played sport competitively or for recreation, CT scans found calcium in the coronary arteries of 68% of those with the most lifelong exercise, more than 2,000 MET-minutes a week, against 43% of those with under 1,000 [@aengevaeren2017]. But the most active men's plaques were more often fully calcified and less often of the mixed kind, which the authors describe as a more benign composition. At the 9.4 METs of the example rider above, 2,000 MET-minutes a week is about three and a half hours of riding, so every rider this book is written for is in that top group.

The most active endurance athletes, then, have more atrial fibrillation and more calcified plaque, and they still lived longer than the general population in the Tour data and longer than the inactive in the surveys. For a rider on six to ten hours the balance looks favourable. A rider who notices palpitations, or a heart rate that behaves strangely, should see a doctor, not a training book. {grade C}

## Colds

In the winter described in Chapter 7, when I rode more often and mostly easy, I fell ill less often with coughs and colds.

In 2011 David Nieman's group followed 1,002 adults through 12 weeks of autumn and winter. People who did aerobic exercise five or more days a week had 43% fewer days with cold symptoms than those who did it once a week or less. The third who rated themselves fittest had 46% fewer than the least fit third, after adjusting for seven other factors [@nieman2011]. The study was observational, and fitness was self-rated.

The same researcher's earlier work raised the opposite worry, that a marathon or very heavy training leaves the immune system open to infection {shadow @nieman1990}. That idea is now disputed. Those infections were self-reported. When a later study swabbed elite athletes who reported symptoms, only 11 of 37 episodes turned out to be infections; the rest may have been allergy, asthma or airways irritated by hours of heavy breathing [@campbell2018].

So regular exercise goes with fewer colds, and big events and very heavy training may go with more, for reasons that may have little to do with the exercise. No study I found compared easy riding with intervals for infections. My winter fits the first finding, and it also fits the fact that I was riding more often. {grade C}

## Weight

Chapter 7 dealt with the question of which fuel a ride burns and concluded that for weight it matters little. What matters is how much energy the riding uses. In the Midwest Exercise Trial, 141 overweight young adults exercised five days a week for ten months, burning either 400 or 600 kcal a session, without being asked to change their diet. Among those who completed nearly all their sessions, weight fell by 3.9 kg and 5.2 kg, while the control group gained 0.5 kg {shadow @donnelly2013}. Across 50 studies of interval training, weight fell by 1.3 kg against controls [@jelleyman2015]. Exercise alone takes off a few kilograms over months, in proportion to the work done, whatever the intensity. {grade B}

## What to do with it

The large surveys place a rider on six to ten hours well into the part of the curve where more activity buys no more lower risk, or beyond the end of the data. Whatever exercise does for lifespan, that rider has most of it already, and nothing in the evidence says that the split between Zone 2 and intervals changes it. If the one trial points anywhere, it points to the intervals.

For someone riding mainly for health, the first few hours of weekly activity carry most of the association with a longer life, and the risk factors improve with any intensity. {grade C} In Generation 100, about half of each exercise group was still doing its programme after five years [@stensvold2020]. The best exercise for health is the one a person will keep doing.
