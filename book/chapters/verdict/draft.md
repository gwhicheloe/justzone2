# Chapter 20: The verdict

No, it isn't just Zone 2.

For a rider on six to ten hours a week, the evidence in this book points to a week built around two hard sessions, well spaced and ridden properly, with the rest of the riding easy, most of it near the top of the easy range, and all of it done regularly, week after week. Zone 2 is the right intensity for most of those easy hours. Per hour, harder training does more. Zone 2 has no special power over fat or health, and elite athletes owe their engines to years of training, most of it easy and much of it below Zone 2. What Zone 2 does well is fill the hours that the hard sessions can't.

That is less exciting than the claim I started with, and more useful.

## What I believed, and what I found

The Introduction began with a winter of easy riding and a faster climb up Sa Calobra, and a sentence I had written for my website: that easy riding was the single most effective way to build mitochondria. The largest analysis of the question, 353 training studies, found that continuous training raised mitochondrial content by about 23% and interval training by 27%, and that per hour of training the harder kinds did far more [@molmen2025]. The sentence was wrong.

The winter still happened, and the climb was still faster. Chapter 7 offered the likeliest explanation. My hours that winter were about the same as before, three to five a week, but the riding became regular, four or five sessions every week where before it had been hard rides done inconsistently. Riding regularly is something the research supports [@molmen2025], and something easy riding makes easy to do. The intensity was probably the least important thing about it. Zone 2 had done something real for me. It just hadn't done it for the reason I had been told.

## The claims, graded

![The book's verdict on sixteen common claims about training, with the grade of the evidence behind each verdict. A means several randomised trials or a good meta-analysis; B, small, short or single-lab studies; C, observation, mechanism or opinion. The last column gives the chapter that weighs each claim.](figures/scorecard.svg)

The figure sets out the book's verdict on sixteen claims that a rider will meet, each with its grade.

**Zone 2 and mitochondria.** Not supported, at grade A. Per programme, steady riding builds about as much as harder riding in people who aren't yet trained. Per hour it builds less, and in people who are already well trained, more of it was not shown to add anything measurable [@molmen2025]. Easy riding builds plenty in a beginner. It is not the best builder of mitochondria for anyone.

**Fat burning.** Not supported, at grade B. The intensity of peak fat burning is real, and it varies from rider to rider and with what they have eaten. But the fat burned over a whole day didn't differ between days of easy and harder exercise {shadow @melanson2002}, and a pooled analysis of 31 studies found no difference in body fat between people assigned to intervals and people assigned to steady training [@keating2017]. Weight follows the work done, not the fuel mix.

**The elite pattern.** Supported, at grade A, as a description. The Olympic champions whose diaries Chapter 8 analysed spent 91% of their endurance training time below the first threshold, and about 1.3 hours a week above it [@tonnessen2014]. What is not supported is the step from their ratio to an amateur's week. Trials of different ways of dividing the hard work found no clear winner over 4 to 16 weeks [@silvaoliveira2024; @cove2025], and the elite example is better read as evidence for a large amount of training, most of it easy, around a modest and steady dose of hard work.

**Two hard sessions.** Supported, at grade B. Two a week, at least 48 hours apart, builds about as much as three over a few weeks, and spacing matters as much as the count [@stanley2013; @tonnessen2020]. One maintains for months. The hard dose that worked in the trials, about 40 minutes to an hour of hard work a week, is close to the champions' own, which suggests to me that recovery, not ambition, sets the ceiling. That last part is my inference, at grade C.

**Which sessions.** Supported, at grade B. In recreational cyclists on about six hours a week, 4 × 8 minutes doubled the gain from 4 × 4 [@seiler2013], and the sessions that let a rider pile up the most work close to the top of what they can sustain did best. Thirty-fifteens are a good second format. Sprints are for sprinting.

**Time near VO₂max.** Not shown, at grade C. It is the best yardstick for comparing sessions, and the study that linked it to gains turned out to be confounded by how much the riders trained.

**Long rides and durability.** Plausible, at grade C. Durability matters, it differs hugely between riders, and the comparisons point to accumulated volume. No trial in trained riders has tested whether long rides build it. Fuelling is the best-supported way to protect power late in a long ride.

**Health.** Zone 2 is not shown to be special, at grade C. Most of the lower risk of death in the large surveys comes with the first few hours of activity a week [@arem2015], and a rider on six to ten hours is well past it. Training lowers blood pressure and blood sugar whatever its intensity. For health, the best exercise is the one that gets done.

**Lactate clearance.** Not shown, at grade C. The claim that riding in Zone 2 is what trains the body to clear lactate comes from a comparison of professional athletes with less active people at one moment, which describes a very fit body without showing how it was built. The one training study I found that measured the lactate transporter directly found it fell after months of mostly easy training, in 20 skiers, and held steady in the group that trained near the threshold.

**Copying the ratio.** Not shown, at grade B. Trials that gave athletes polarised, pyramidal or threshold-heavy versions of mostly easy training found no consistent winner over a few months [@silvaoliveira2024]. A rider on seven hours who rides 80% easy has about an hour and a half of hard training, close to the champions' 1.3 hours, and that is a better reason for the ratio than the ratio itself.

**Years of easy volume.** Unknown, at grade C. This is the claim on which the case for a large volume of easy riding finally rests, and it may be true. The elite records are consistent with it. No trial has been long enough to test it.

**Strength and heat.** Heavy leg lifting probably makes trained cyclists a little faster in efforts from a few minutes to an hour, at grade B, from small studies in a few laboratories. Deliberate heat training is not shown to make an amateur faster in normal weather: the one trial in riders like the reader found them no faster than riders who trained normally, at grade C. Both have reasons beyond speed: lifting for the bones of a rider over 40, and a week or two of warm riding before a hot event.

**Building up.** The rule of adding no more than 10% a week is not supported, at grade B: in its one large trial, 20.8% of runners on a slower 13-week build-up were injured against 20.3% on a standard 8-week plan [@buist2008]. Avoid sudden jumps, and no particular rate is needed.

**Zones from formulas.** Not supported, at grade B. The first threshold falls anywhere from 60% to 90% of maximum heart rate in different people [@iannetta2020], so a zone set from a formula is a guess about where your threshold is. Chapter 6 gave better ways to find it.

## What Zone 2 is for

Zone 2 earns its place in a different way from the one the fashion claims. Chapter 7's rule is that an easy ride should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions. For a rider on these hours, that puts it near the top of the easy range, which is what Zone 2 now means. Riding there gives more work, more energy burned and more of the muscle involved than riding lower, at a cost that still lets the hard sessions be hard. That is a modest claim and I think a sound one, though no trial has compared the top of the easy range with the bottom.

Zone 2 is also the intensity that can be repeated. It doesn't hurt, it doesn't need recovery days of its own, and it can be done four or five times a week by someone with a job and a family. If the lesson of my own winter is regularity, Zone 2 is what made regularity possible. That is a real virtue, and it is a different one from the virtues it is usually credited with.

I built an app, JustZone2, for my own winter training. It holds a heart rate in a zone on a smart trainer, which makes the easy part of the week easier to do and does nothing for the hard part. None of the evidence in this book depends on it, and none of the advice needs it.

## What the research can't yet tell us

**Years, not weeks.** Almost every training trial in this book lasted between four and sixteen weeks. Elite athletes build their engines over a decade. Whether years of easy volume build something that a few months of intervals can't, a bigger and more compliant heart, more capillaries, more durability, is the most important open question for anyone who trains for a long time, and no trial has been long enough to answer it.

**Riders like the reader.** Most trials are of untrained people, students or well-trained athletes. Riders with jobs, on six to ten hours a week, in their forties and fifties, are rarely studied, possibly because they have no time to be. Women are studied much less than men. Older riders are studied less than either.

**The top of Zone 2 against the bottom.** The book's rule for easy riding rests on mechanism and reasoning. Nobody has assigned riders on the same hours to ride their easy hours high or low in the easy range and measured the result.

**Durability.** Nobody has tested whether long rides build it in trained riders, or what does.

**The ceiling on hard work.** The dose of hard training that works seems to be similar in amateurs and champions. Whether more would work for a rider who recovered well, over months rather than weeks, hasn't been tested.

Each of those is a trial someone could run. Until they are, the honest position on each is the grade beside it, and the useful habit is the one the book has tried to teach: ask who was studied, for how long, what was compared, and whether the claim is about a mechanism or a result.

## The week, once more

Two hard sessions, at least 48 hours apart: long intervals of four to eight minutes adding up to about half an hour of hard work, and 30/15s or a controlled threshold session. Easy riding for the rest, near the top of Zone 2, a few beats under the ceiling. A long ride if your events are long. Fuel on anything over 90 minutes. Lift through the winter if you can. Ride regularly. Measure the same thing every week and judge it every month. And when the next fashion arrives, which it will, ask what it was compared with. {grade B for the core; grade C for the rest}
