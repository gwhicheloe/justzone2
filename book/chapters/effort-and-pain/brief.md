## The question

Is it the muscle that stops you, or the brain? Can some people stand more pain than others, does training change that, and do professionals have more of it?

George's questions, in his words: "whether anything happens in the brain or nerves, the neuromuscular system, I'm not even sure what that is; can some people endure more pain than others; does training increase our threshold for pain; can pros handle more pain; is Pog in more pain than anyone else?"

## The argument (to approve)

1. In a maximal effort the muscle is held short of its limit by what the brain does with feedback from the legs. Block the feedback and riders start too fast and damage their muscles more (Amann 2009).
2. The neuromuscular system runs from motor cortex to motor unit and back. Fatigue is central as well as peripheral (Gandevia 2001).
3. Exhaustion in a hard ride is not muscle failure: riders who stopped at 242 W could at once produce 731 W (Marcora & Staiano 2010). Why they stop is disputed (Noakes's central governor; Marcora's perception of effort). The book does not pick a winner.
4. Effort and pain are different sensations and can be changed separately: mental fatigue raises effort (Marcora 2009); paracetamol lets riders ride harder at the same pain (Mauger 2010).
5. The pain signal is also a control signal for breathing and blood flow; without it riders last less long (Amann 2011).
6. Athletes tolerate more pain than non-athletes and feel it at about the same point (Tesarz 2012). Tolerance of pain during exercise, not laboratory pain, goes with time-trial performance, and adds a little beyond fitness (Astokorki & Mauger 2017).
7. Training raises tolerance, and in the one randomised comparison only hard intervals did; steady riding near the top of Zone 2 did not (O'Leary 2017). **This is the chapter's contribution to the book's question.**
8. Professionals: no pain data found. They resist mental fatigue better than recreational riders (Martin 2016), cause unknown. Whether any named rider suffers more is unanswerable.
9. Riders hold something back even flat out (Stone 2012).
