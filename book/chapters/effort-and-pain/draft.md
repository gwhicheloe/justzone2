# Chapter 3: Effort, pain and the brain

In 2009 Markus Amann and his colleagues at the University of Wisconsin gave eight cyclists an injection in the lower spine and then asked them to race a 5-kilometre time trial [@amann2009]. On one day the injection was fentanyl, a powerful opioid, placed where it would block the nerve signals travelling up from the legs to the brain without affecting the signals going down. The riders could still drive their muscles normally, and their leg strength was unchanged. What they had lost was most of the feedback from their legs, including the pain. On another day the injection was a placebo. Neither the riders nor the researchers running the test knew which was which.

With the feedback blocked, the riders started faster. Their power over the first half of the time trial was 6% higher than on the placebo day. Over the second half it was 11% lower. When the researchers stimulated the nerve to the thigh muscle afterwards, to measure how much force the muscle itself could still produce, it had lost 46% of its force, against 33% after the placebo ride [@amann2009].

The riders hadn't been freed to go faster. They had lost the signal that normally stops a rider going out too hard, and their muscles finished in a worse state than the riders would ever have chosen. The experiment shows that in a time trial ridden as hard as possible, the legs are held back from their limit by something the brain does with the information coming up from them.

## From brain to muscle and back

The neuromuscular system is the chain that turns an intention into a pedal stroke. It starts in the motor cortex, the strip of brain that issues commands for movement. The command travels down the spinal cord to motor neurons, nerve cells whose fibres run out to the muscle. Each motor neuron connects to a group of muscle fibres, anything from a few dozen to more than a thousand, and when it fires they all contract together. A motor neuron and its fibres are called a motor unit. To produce more force, the nervous system recruits more motor units and fires them faster.

Traffic also runs the other way. Muscles are full of nerve endings that report back: how stretched the muscle is, how much tension it is under and, through a set of thin fibres known as group III and IV afferents, how its chemistry is changing. Those thin fibres respond to the by-products of hard work, and their signals are what the brain experiences as the ache and burn of exertion. They were the fibres Amann's injection silenced.

The by-product usually blamed is lactic acid, and it is only one ingredient. In 2014 a group that included Amann injected the chemistry of hard work into a thumb muscle in ten volunteers [@pollak2014]. Acid, lactate or ATP on its own produced no sensation at all, even at the highest doses. Mixed at the levels found in a muscle during moderate exercise, they produced a feeling of fatigue, and at the levels of hard exercise, an ache. The burn is the nerves' reading of the muscle's chemistry as a whole.

Fatigue can arise anywhere along this chain, and physiologists divide it in two. *Peripheral fatigue* is in the muscle: the same nerve signal produces less force than it did. *Central fatigue* is upstream: the brain and spinal cord drive the muscle less hard than they could. The two can be told apart by stimulating the nerve directly, as Amann's group did, and seeing how the muscle responds when the brain is bypassed.

A review by the Australian neurophysiologist Simon Gandevia established how common central fatigue is {shadow @gandevia2001}. Even when people try as hard as they can, their nervous system usually does not activate the muscle fully, and during sustained efforts that shortfall grows. "Human muscle fatigue", the review ends, "does not simply reside in the muscle."

## How it works: what makes you stop

For most of the twentieth century the standard account of exhaustion was the one that came down from Archibald Hill, whose discovery of VO₂max opened Chapter 1. Muscles run short of oxygen, their chemistry is disturbed, and they fail. The brain had no part in it. The South African physiologist Tim Noakes has called this the "brainless model" [@noakes2012].

Noakes's alternative is that the brain regulates exercise in advance, to make sure it ends before anything is damaged. On this view the brain continuously sets how much muscle it is willing to recruit, using the feedback from the body, the distance still to go and its memory of previous efforts, and the feeling of fatigue is the means by which it enforces that decision. He describes fatigue as an emotion, generated by the brain, and largely independent of the actual state of the muscles at the moment it is felt. The idea is known as the central governor.

A second alternative comes from Samuele Marcora, an Italian physiologist who has spent much of his career in Britain. In 2010 he and Walter Staiano ran an experiment that is easy to describe [@marcorastaiano2010]. Ten fit men rode at a fixed 242 watts until they could no longer keep going, which took about ten and a half minutes. The instant they stopped, they were asked for one all-out maximal effort. They produced 731 watts.

That was well down on the 1,075 watts they could manage when fresh, so their muscles were certainly fatigued. But they had just declared themselves unable to continue at 242 watts, and their legs could still produce three times that. Whatever stopped them, it was not that their muscles could no longer do the work [@marcorastaiano2010]. Parents will have seen this before. A child too tired to walk another step can usually still run to an ice-cream van.

Marcora's explanation is that people stop when the effort required exceeds what they are willing to give, or when they come to believe the task is impossible. The quantity that matters in his account is the *perception of effort*, how hard the task feels, which rises steadily during a hard ride until it reaches the most a person will tolerate. Researchers measure it by asking. The usual tool is the scale devised by the Swedish psychologist Gunnar Borg, which runs from 6 to 20, and a rider's score on it is called their rating of perceived exertion {shadow @borg1982}.

One objection to the experiment is that a maximal effort lasting a few seconds draws on different energy stores from ten minutes at 242 watts, so the comparison flatters the muscle. That is my reservation, and it does not rescue the old model: a muscle that can still produce 731 watts has not failed.

The theories differ on what the brain is responding to, and the experiments in the rest of this chapter don't settle it. At the end of a maximal effort there is still capacity in the muscle, and what a rider can do depends partly on how the brain weighs the sensations coming from the body. {grade B that exhaustion in these tests is not muscle failure; grade C for any one theory of why}

## Effort and pain are different things

Riders use "pain" for everything unpleasant about a hard effort. Researchers separate two sensations, and the distinction changes how the evidence reads.

Effort is the sense of how hard you are driving yourself. It seems to come from the outgoing command, the brain's own record of how strongly it is signalling the muscles. Pain is incoming. It is carried by the thin afferent fibres from the muscle and reports that something there is wrong. In the ordinary course of a hard climb the two rise together, which is why they feel like one thing. Experiments can pull them apart.

A study from Marcora's group in 2009 changed effort without touching the muscle [@marcora2009]. Sixteen people did 90 minutes of a demanding computer task that required sustained concentration, and on another day watched documentaries for 90 minutes. After each they rode to exhaustion at 80% of their peak power. After the documentaries they lasted 754 seconds on average. After the mental task they lasted 640, about 15% less. Their physiological responses to the ride were largely the same on both days. What differed was their rating of effort, which was higher from the start after the mental task and so reached its maximum sooner.

A study by Alexis Mauger at the University of Exeter went at the pain side [@mauger2010]. Thirteen trained cyclists rode a 10-mile time trial twice, once after taking paracetamol and once after a placebo, in random order and with neither riders nor testers knowing which. On paracetamol they averaged 265 watts against 255, and finished in 26 minutes 15 seconds against 26 minutes 45.

The riders did not report less pain on paracetamol. Their pain ratings were the same in both rides, and so were their ratings of effort, while their heart rates and blood lactate were higher. They had ridden harder until it hurt exactly as much as before. That suggests riders pace a time trial to a level of pain they are prepared to accept, and that a drug which dulls the signal lets them reach that level at a higher power. {grade B}

Paracetamol is not a training aid, and that result is not advice. A painkiller taken to ride harder removes a warning the body has reasons for, and the stronger drugs that work the same way are covered in the chapter on drugs, which discusses tramadol.

### What the pain is for

Amann's group followed their time-trial study with one in which seven men rode at a fixed 318 watts for as long as they could, with and without the spinal block {shadow @amann2011}. If pain were purely a brake, removing it should have let them ride longer. They lasted 6.8 minutes with the block and 8.7 without it.

The reason is that the feedback from the legs does a second job. It tells the brainstem how hard the muscles are working, and the brainstem raises breathing and blood flow to match. With the signal blocked, the riders breathed too little for the work they were doing, their blood carried less oxygen, and their muscles fatigued about a third more by the end.

So the same nerve fibres that make hard riding hurt also make it possible. The burn of a hard effort is the conscious edge of a control system that keeps oxygen delivery matched to demand and keeps the rider from damaging muscle. A rider who could switch it off would not be faster.

> **From the saddle.** When I go into the red early in a ride, well above what I can sustain, it can be really hard to get back from there.

Amann's time-trial riders did the same thing with a drug's help. Riding 6% over their usual power in the first half cost them 11% in the second, and left their muscles more fatigued than a normally paced ride would have [@amann2009].

## Can some people stand more than others?

The *pain threshold* is the point at which a stimulus, such as pressure or cold, is first felt as painful. *Pain tolerance* is how long or how much of it a person will bear before asking for it to stop.

In 2012 a German group pooled 15 studies that compared athletes with normally active people on laboratory pain tests, 899 people in all [@tesarz2012]. Athletes had clearly higher pain tolerance. The pooled difference was 0.87 standard deviations, which is large: it puts the average athlete above roughly four in five of the comparison group. For pain threshold the studies disagreed, and once the weaker ones were set aside there was no significant difference.

Athletes, then, appear to feel pain at about the same point as everyone else and to put up with it for longer. {grade B}

The comparison has the weakness of every comparison between athletes and other people, the one met in Chapter 1 with the professional cyclists in Madrid. It cannot show whether training made the athletes tolerant or whether tolerant people are the ones who become athletes.

There is also the question of which pain. Mauger's group later tested 32 recreationally active people on two standard laboratory pain tests, a hand held in cold water and pressure on a muscle, and on a third they designed themselves: how much pain the person would tolerate during cycling when the effort was held at a fixed rating [@astokorki2017]. Then everyone rode a 10-mile time trial. Neither the cold-water test nor the pressure test predicted time-trial performance at all. Tolerance of pain during exercise predicted it strongly, with a correlation of −0.83.

That correlation needs care, because a fitter rider will score well on any cycling test. The researchers checked what pain tolerance added once ordinary fitness was accounted for. Peak power in a ramp test explained 74% of the differences in time-trial performance. Adding exercise-pain tolerance explained a further 7.5%. Fitness is most of it, and pain tolerance is a real but small part of what is left [@astokorki2017]. {grade C: one study, 32 people, and a correlation}

## Does training change it?

The first trial was Australian {shadow @jonesmd2014}. Twelve people cycled for 30 minutes three times a week for six weeks, and twelve carried on as normal. Before and after, everyone took a tourniquet test, in which a cuff cuts off blood flow to the arm and the person squeezes a handgrip repeatedly until they choose to stop. The training group lasted longer after six weeks. The control group did not change. Pain threshold, tested with pressure, did not change in either group, and during the tourniquet test the trained group rated the pain as highly as before. They were tolerating the same pain for longer.

The test was on the arm, and the training was on a bike. Nothing about the arm's muscle had been trained, so whatever changed was in the nervous system.

That study had a flaw its successors pointed out: the volunteers chose their own group, and the control group did no supervised exercise of any kind [@oleary2017]. The next trial fixed both.

### One study, up close: intervals, steady riding and a tourniquet

Thomas O'Leary and colleagues at Oxford Brookes University recruited 20 healthy adults, four of them women, none of whom did endurance training [@oleary2017]. They were randomly assigned to two groups of ten, balanced for fitness. Both groups trained on a cycle ergometer three times a week for six weeks.

One group did hard intervals: six efforts of 5 minutes, rising to eight after the third week, at a power halfway between the lactate threshold and VO₂max, with a minute's rest between. The other group rode continuously at 90% of the power at their lactate threshold, which is roughly the top of what this book calls Zone 2, for as long as it took to do the same total work. That came to between 60 and 80 minutes a session [@oleary2017].

Before and after, each person took the tourniquet test on an arm, without being able to see a clock. The researcher running the test did not know which group the person was in. Each person also rode to exhaustion at a fixed hard power.

Fitness improved by similar amounts in the two groups: the lactate threshold and VO₂max went up about as much with steady riding as with intervals. Pain tolerance did not. In the interval group it rose 41%. In the steady group it changed by −3%, which is no change. As in the Australian trial, the pain ratings during the test were the same as before training, so the interval group were lasting longer at the same pain [@oleary2017].

The rides to exhaustion split the same way. At the same power as before training, the interval group lasted 148% longer and the steady group 38% longer. And across all 20 people, those whose pain tolerance rose most tended to be those whose time to exhaustion rose most, with correlations of 0.44 and 0.51 for the two versions of the test [@oleary2017].

The authors' interpretation is that repeatedly riding at an intensity that hurts teaches the nervous system to tolerate it, that this is separate from the gain in aerobic fitness, and that it may be part of why interval training improves hard efforts more than steady training does.

Twenty people is a small trial, and they were untrained, so six weeks of anything was a large change for them. The pain was in an arm starved of blood, which is not the pain of a climb, and the authors say plainly that tolerance of one kind of pain may not carry over to another. The correlations are modest: at 0.44 to 0.51, changes in pain tolerance go with about a fifth to a quarter of the variation in the performance gain. And the study shows that pain tolerance and performance improved together, which is weaker than showing that one caused the other. {grade B that hard training raises tolerance of this kind of pain; grade C that this explains any of the performance gain}

An hour or more of steady riding just under the lactate threshold improved fitness as much as intervals did and did nothing for pain tolerance. If tolerating discomfort is trainable, the evidence so far says it is trained by discomfort.

### Reading the research: when nobody can be blinded

The paracetamol study and the spinal-block studies were double blind. The riders didn't know what they had been given and neither did the people testing them. That matters most when the outcome depends on a decision by the participant, and in this chapter every outcome does: how hard to ride a time trial, when to stop a ride to exhaustion, when to let go of the handgrip.

A training study can't be blinded. Nobody does six weeks of intervals without knowing it. So ask what the participants might have believed. Someone who has spent six weeks doing sessions that hurt may reasonably expect to be better at enduring things, and a person who expects to last longer in a test with no fixed end point will often last longer. The 41% in O'Leary's study is partly a measure of how long people were willing to keep squeezing, and willingness responds to belief.

The researchers did what can be done. The participants were not told the hypothesis, and neither programme was presented as the better one. The tester didn't know the groups. The clock was hidden. The steady-riding group is the most important control, because they also trained hard for six weeks under supervision and could equally have expected to improve, and they didn't. That makes simple expectation a less likely explanation than it would be against a group that did nothing, which was the weakness of the earlier Australian trial.

It doesn't remove the problem. The interval group knew their training hurt and the steady group knew theirs didn't, and that knowledge is exactly the thing that might shape expectations about a pain test. When a study's outcome is the moment a person chooses to stop, check who knew what, and treat the size of the effect with more caution than the direction.

## What about professionals?

The search for this chapter turned up no study of pain tolerance in professional cyclists. It did turn up one on a neighbouring question.

In 2016 a group at the University of Canberra and the Australian Institute of Sport tested 11 professional road cyclists and 9 recreational ones [@martin2016]. Each rider came in twice. On one visit they spent 30 minutes on the Stroop task, a test in which the names of colours appear in the wrong colour of ink and the person has to respond to the ink and ignore the word. It demands continuous suppression of an automatic response, which psychologists call inhibitory control, and half an hour of it is mentally tiring. On the other visit they did an easy task for 10 minutes. Then they rode a 20-minute time trial.

The professionals were better at the Stroop task itself, with 705 correct responses against 576. After it, the recreational riders' time-trial power fell from 226 watts to 216, a drop of about 4%. The professionals' power was 326 watts after the easy task and 323 after the hard one, which is no difference [@martin2016].

So professionals were better at overriding an impulse and less affected by mental fatigue. The link to riding is that holding a hard effort is itself a continuous act of overriding the impulse to ease off.

The same caution applies as to the athletes in the pain studies. The authors say the difference may be inherited, or developed through the training and life of a professional, or both, and their study can't tell. Twenty riders tested once is also a thin basis for a general claim. {grade C}

Whether Tadej Pogačar is in more pain than the riders he leaves behind, or less, nobody has measured, and nothing a spectator can see would tell you. What the studies in this chapter suggest about the best riders in general is narrower. They probably don't feel less: athletes' pain thresholds and pain ratings look like everyone else's. They can probably hold the same discomfort for longer and at a higher power, and they may be better at continuing to choose it. Most of the gap between a Tour winner and an amateur is still in Chapter 1.

## How much is held back?

If the brain regulates effort with a margin, the margin should sometimes show. In 2012 a group in the north of England had nine trained cyclists ride 4,000-metre time trials against an on-screen avatar, which they were told was a replay of their own best previous ride [@stone2012]. In one trial that was true. In another the avatar had been set, without their knowledge, to ride at 102% of the power of their previous best.

They beat their previous best in both, and they went faster against the dishonest avatar than against the honest one. Riders who believed they were already at their limit found more when they thought they had done it before. The authors concluded that cyclists keep a reserve even in a time trial ridden flat out.

It was nine riders, a short effort and a 2% deception. Nobody should conclude that belief can replace fitness. The study is evidence that what a rider treats as their maximum has some give in it [@stone2012]. {grade C}

## What to do with it

Most of the studies have between seven and twenty participants, most outcomes depend on a person deciding to stop, and the theories are still argued over. What can be said with some confidence is that the muscle is not the only thing that sets how hard you can ride, and that the rest is not fixed.


Treat the discomfort of a hard effort as information. The spinal-block experiments show what happens without it: riders start too fast, breathe too little and wreck their legs. Pacing by feel works because the feeling is tied to what the muscles and lungs are doing.

Don't take painkillers to train or race harder. The paracetamol result is interesting as evidence about how pacing works. As a practice it trades a warning signal for a few watts.

Arrive at hard sessions and events mentally fresh if you can. In the Canberra study, half an hour of concentrated mental work cost recreational riders 10 watts in a 20-minute time trial. A day of difficult meetings is not a Stroop task, and the comparison is loose, but the direction is the one most riders with jobs would recognise: the interval session after a draining day feels harder at the same power. If it does, the studies suggest that is a real effect on perceived effort and not a failure of character [@martin2016].

Hard sessions probably train something that easy sessions don't, apart from their effects on the heart and muscle. The only randomised comparison found that intervals raised pain tolerance and steady riding matched for work did not. It was one small trial in untrained people. It is also the first sign in this book that a rider who stays in Zone 2 may leave something untrained, and a reason not to drop hard riding entirely even if you are persuaded by the case for volume.
