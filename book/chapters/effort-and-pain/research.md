# Effort, pain and the brain: research notes

Written 10 October 2026. Tags: [FT] read in the full text; [Abs] stated in the paper's abstract; [Rep] reported by another source; [GK] general knowledge, not checked against a source for this book.

**How far each source was read.** O'Leary 2017: accepted manuscript read (introduction, methods, discussion, limitations); its tables and figures did not survive text extraction, so all its numbers are the abstract's. Martin 2016: abstract and participants section. Everything else: abstract only. Amann 2009, Noakes 2012 and Martin 2016 are free to read in full and should be at the revise stage.

## Amann 2009 [Abs]

- Eight cyclists; three 5 km time trials: control, placebo injection (saline into the interspinous ligament, L3-L4), intrathecal fentanyl (L3-L4). Double blind, placebo controlled.
- Fentanyl had no effect on quadriceps strength.
- Muscle activation (EMG) +12% in the first 2.5 km with fentanyl against placebo; similar in the second.
- Power +6 ± 2% in the first 2.5 km, −11 ± 2% in the second.
- Quadriceps twitch force after the ride: −46% with fentanyl, −33% with placebo.
- Authors: attenuated feedback "results in an overshoot" in central motor drive and power "normally chosen by the athlete", causing "excessive development of peripheral muscle fatigue".
- **Not in the abstract: overall finishing times.** The draft makes no claim about them. My memory is that times were similar and that riders had trouble walking afterwards; neither is used. Check in the full text (PMC2670040).
- University of Wisconsin. [GK]

## Amann 2011 [Abs]

- Seven men, constant 318 W (80% of peak power) to exhaustion, placebo against intrathecal fentanyl, double blind.
- Time to exhaustion 6.8 min with fentanyl, 8.7 min with placebo.
- Ventilation relative to CO₂ output 9% lower, haemoglobin saturation 3% lower with fentanyl.
- End-exercise twitch force −44% against −34%: "about one-third greater".
- Feedback "limits CMD but also minimizes locomotor muscle fatigue development by stimulating adequate ventilatory and circulatory responses".

## Anatomy and definitions

- Motor cortex, spinal cord, motor neuron, motor unit; recruitment and firing rate; motor unit sizes. [GK: textbook]
- Group III/IV afferents respond to pressure, heat and metabolites and carry nociceptive signals. [FT, O'Leary 2017 introduction]
- Central and peripheral fatigue; voluntary activation usually less than maximal; central fatigue develops during sustained maximal efforts. Closing line: "Human muscle fatigue does not simply reside in the muscle." [Abs, Gandevia 2001]

## Theories

- Noakes 2012: Hill's model called "this brainless model"; fatigue "predominantly an emotion"; sensations "illusionary since their generation is largely independent of the real biological state of the athlete". [Abs]
- Marcora & Staiano 2010: ten fit men; exhaustion at 242 ± 24 W (80% of peak aerobic power) after 10.5 ± 2.1 min; maximal voluntary cycling power 1,075 W before, 731 W immediately after. [Abs] 731 / 242 = 3.0 (my arithmetic). The duration of the maximal effort is not in the abstract; the draft says "a few seconds" only inside my own objection, which is [GK] and should be checked.
- Perception of effort as arising from the outgoing motor command: Marcora's position, contested. [GK] The draft says "seems to".
- Borg scale; 6 to 20 range. [Abs for the scale, Borg 1982; range as used in O'Leary FT]

## Effort and pain

- Marcora 2009: 16 subjects, randomised crossover; 90 min demanding cognitive task or neutral documentaries; then to exhaustion at 80% peak power: 640 ± 316 s against 754 ± 339 s. Physiological responses "largely unaffected". Perceived effort higher; rose similarly over time in both. [Abs] (754 − 640) / 754 = 15% (my arithmetic).
- Mauger 2010: 13 trained male cyclists, 10-mile (16.1 km) time trial, acetaminophen or placebo, randomised double blind. 26 min 15 s against 26 min 45 s. Mean power 265 against 255 W. Lactate 6.1 against 5.1 mmol/L; heart rate 87% against 84% of max. Perceived exertion 15.5 against 15.7; perceived pain 5.6 against 5.5 (no difference). [Abs] Dose not in the abstract. University of Exeter. [GK]

## Differences between people

- Tesarz 2012: 15 studies, 899 subjects; 12 on tolerance, 9 on threshold. Tolerance: Hedges' g = 0.87 (0.53 to 1.21). Threshold: g = 0.69 (0.16 to 1.21), not significant after excluding high-risk-of-bias studies. [Abs] "Above roughly four in five": normal distribution, 0.87 SD is the 81st percentile (my arithmetic).
- Astokorki & Mauger 2017: 32 recreationally active (23 men, 9 women). Cold pressor and pressure pain: no correlation with 16.1 km time trial. Exercise-induced pain tolerance at fixed RPE: R = −0.83. Regression: peak power adjusted R² = 0.739; exercise-pain tolerance added 0.075. [Abs]

## Training

- Jones 2014: 24 participants; 12 trained six weeks (cycling 3 × 30 min a week at 75% of oxygen uptake reserve), 12 usual activity. Ischaemic pain tolerance up in the training group (P = 0.036); pressure pain threshold and pain ratings during ischaemia unchanged. [Abs] Tolerance tested in the untrained upper limb; groups self-selected; control group unsupervised. [Rep, O'Leary 2017 introduction]
- O'Leary 2017:
  - 20 healthy adults (four women), recruited in Oxford, not endurance training (≤ 30 min, ≤ 2 sessions a week). Randomised 1:1, stratified by lactate threshold and VO₂max. [FT]
  - Intervals: 6 × 5 min at halfway between lactate threshold and VO₂max, 1 min rest, 8 repeats in weeks 4 to 6. Steady: 90% of power at lactate threshold, duration set to match the work (about 60 to 80 min). Three sessions a week, six weeks, 18 sessions, supervised. [FT; durations Abs]
  - Tourniquet test: cuff on the upper arm at 200 mmHg, handgrip contractions at 30% of maximum, 4 s on and 4 s off, to voluntary stop; participant blind to elapsed time. [FT]
  - Participants could not be blinded but were naive to the hypotheses; assessor blind to group until after analysis. [FT]
  - Aerobic fitness improved similarly (all P ≥ 0.081). Pain tolerance +41% intervals (P < 0.001), −3% steady (P = 0.72). Time to exhaustion at the same absolute power +148% against +38%; at the same relative intensity +43% against −4%. Correlations of change in pain tolerance with change in time to exhaustion: r = 0.44 and 0.51. [Abs] r² = 0.19 and 0.26 (my arithmetic).
  - Pain ratings during the test unchanged by training. [FT, Discussion]
  - Authors' limitations: ischaemic arm pain differs from exercise pain; one kind of pain tolerance may not transfer to another. [FT]
  - Oxford Brookes University. [GK: the participants were recruited in Oxford, FT; the affiliation is from memory]
  - "90% of lactate threshold is roughly the top of Zone 2": my gloss. Check against the Intensity chapter's definitions when it is written.

## Professionals and reserve

- Martin 2016: 11 professional and 9 recreational road cyclists; 30 min incongruent Stroop or 10 min easy task, crossover; then 20 min time trial. Stroop correct responses 705 ± 68 against 576 ± 74. Recreational: 216 ± 33 W after mental exertion against 226 ± 25 W control (P = 0.014). Professional: 323 ± 42 against 326 ± 35 W (P = 0.502). "May be either genetic and/or developed through the training and lifestyle." University of Canberra; Australian Institute of Sport. [Abs; affiliations FT] 10 / 226 = 4.4% (my arithmetic).
- Stone 2012: nine trained male cyclists, four 4000 m time trials; avatar said to be baseline; in one trial set to 102% of baseline power. Deception trial faster than baseline and than accurate-avatar trial; accurate faster than baseline. "Cyclists operate with a metabolic reserve even during maximal time trials." [Abs] Northumbria University. [GK]
- No study of pain tolerance in professional cyclists was found in the searches run for this chapter (Europe PMC, about 20 queries). That is not a systematic search.

## Not used, and gaps

- Brain endurance training (doing cognitive tasks to train resistance to mental fatigue): a 2026 meta-analysis exists (Wang et al., Front Psychol) but has no abstract in the database. Not read, not mentioned.
- Neural adaptations to endurance training itself (motor unit recruitment, voluntary activation after training): no source gathered. O'Leary's companion paper on central and peripheral fatigue after the same training would be the place to start.
- Caffeine and perceived effort; placebo studies; music; self-talk. Not covered.
- Sex differences in pain tolerance. Not covered.
- Tramadol: cross-referenced to the Drugs chapter, no numbers repeated here.
