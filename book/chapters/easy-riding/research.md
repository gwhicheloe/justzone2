## Storoschuk et al. 2025, "Much Ado About Zone 2"

Storoschuk KL, Moran-MacDonald A, Gibala MJ, Gurd BJ. *Sports Med.* 2025;55:1611–1624. **Read in full, 8 October 2026** (PDF from George, `papers/storoschuk2025.pdf`). All [FT]. Already cited in the website's evidence review (reference 11). `@storoschuk2025`

**What kind of paper.** A narrative review, and it says so: "A systematic literature search was not utilized". Sources included reference lists, "articles shared through social media" and papers known to the authors. Queen's University and McMaster, Canada.

**Its question, exactly.** Whether Zone 2 is the *optimal* intensity for mitochondrial capacity and fat oxidation **in the general population**, defined as non-endurance-trained people who are inactive or just meeting activity guidelines (about 150 minutes a week). It is not about trained cyclists, and says of elite athletes that "a lack of data does not disprove" a benefit of high volumes of easy training.

**How it defines Zone 2.** Below the first lactate threshold, taken as 2.0 mmol/L; or below VT1, below Fatmax, or under 45% of VO₂max. Notes that the top of Zone 2 ranges from about **24% to 80% of VO₂max** depending on fitness: a walk for a sedentary person, about 300 W for an endurance athlete.

**Where the popular claim came from (their account).** Podcasts and popular media; the claim rests on observing that elite endurance athletes do large volumes of easy training and have high mitochondrial capacity. Their two objections: those athletes also do a lot of high-intensity work, so cause can't be assigned; and they train over 20 hours a week, far beyond public-health volumes.

**Findings, by section.**
- *Signals inside the muscle (section 4.1–4.3):* Zone 2 causes small and inconsistent changes in the energy-stress signals (AMP/ADP, phosphocreatine) and in AMPK. PGC-1α results mixed; one study found a duration effect (60 and 90 min raised its expression, 30 min did not). The calcium-signalling theory for long easy exercise is "largely based on an unsubstantiated theory": they could find no study measuring calcium during Zone 2, and two that found no rise in CaMKII phosphorylation after 65 to 70 minutes.
- *Mitochondrial capacity after training (4.4):* "Few studies have explicitly investigated" it and "the available evidence is mixed". For: 10 weeks at Fatmax in obese people with type 2 diabetes raised citrate synthase; 12 weeks of low-intensity cycling improved a marker of mitochondrial capacity. Against: 4 weeks of Zone 2 did not raise citrate synthase in recreationally active men; 5 months of mostly Zone 2 (86% of volume) did not in elite athletes; a 42-day ski expedition at about 60% of maximum heart rate for 6 h a day *reduced* it. A meta-analysis of 56 studies (their ref 19, Granata and colleagues) suggests training below 60% of maximum work rate is not expected to raise mitochondrial content.
- *Is it optimal (4.5):* higher intensities produce larger signals and, in the meta-analyses they cite, larger adaptations. They note the Mølmen meta-analysis (the one in our Introduction) found endurance training only "slightly less" effective than intervals, but point out it counted everything below the second threshold as endurance training, so it can't isolate Zone 2. **This is the same caveat already in our notes on Mølmen.** Their suggestion: elite athletes' mitochondria may owe more to their time above Zone 2.
- *Fat oxidation (section 5):* Zone 2 **does** improve it in sedentary, overweight and diabetic people (one year of confirmed Zone 2 training raised maximal fat oxidation). Whether any intensity is best is "less clear"; studies split both ways. An older meta-analysis suggests trained people need higher intensities to move the first threshold.
- *Health and fitness (section 6):* for a fixed amount of exercise, higher intensity raises VO₂max more, and VO₂max is the fitness measure most strongly tied to mortality. In unfit people Zone 2 may fall below the "moderate" intensity that guidelines ask for.

**Their conclusion.** Zone 2 is not shown to be optimal for mitochondria or fat oxidation; people with limited time should prioritise higher intensities. Their own stated limitation: "few studies have explicitly examined the effect of Zone 2 training", so they included studies that fitted the definition as best they could.

**Declared interest.** Gibala is an adviser to and holds equity in Longevity League, a US company whose services "in part relate to exercise". He is also the researcher behind the 2006 sprint-interval study in our Introduction.

**How the book should use it.**
- It is the strongest published statement of the case against the Zone 2 fashion, and its title is close to ours. It must be engaged with directly in this chapter and acknowledged in the Introduction.
- It supports the book's line that the claim "Zone 2 is the best stimulus for mitochondria" outran the evidence. George's own correction in the Introduction is the same point.
- It does **not** answer the book's question for its readers. Its population is people doing 150 minutes a week; its Zone 2 can be as low as 45% of VO₂max. A cyclist riding 6 to 10 hours a week at the top of Zone 2 is outside its scope, and the review says the athlete question is open.
- Reading-the-research angles: a narrative (not systematic) review; "not shown to be optimal" against "shown not to work"; evidence of absence against absence of evidence (few direct studies); an author with a declared commercial interest and a research career on the other side of the argument. None of that makes it wrong.
- Primary studies to read before drafting: Granata's meta-analysis (their ref 19); the 4-week Zone 2 study in active men (ref 78); the 5-month elite study (ref 79); the ski expeditions (refs 80, 81); the one-year Zone 2 study (ref 117); the capillary meta-analysis (ref 111, which may be Mølmen again).

## Fat max, weight, and how easy is easy enough (George's question, 11 October 2026)

George asked in conversation (his words are in `george/notes.md`): he rides at the top of where he thinks his Zone 2 is, partly because he believes that is "fat max", and because managing his weight is a goal. Is the top of Zone 2 really fat max? Would he get the same benefit lower in the zone, or in zone 1? Could he protect his hard rides by riding easier? **This is a question the chapter has to answer.** Sources looked up that day; all [Abs] unless marked.

**Where peak fat burning sits.**
- Meixner 2025 [FT], 50 trained cyclists, Table 2: peak fat oxidation (FatMax) at a median of 125 W, 130 ± 12 bpm, 72.3 ± 5.7% of maximum heart rate, 56.0 ± 10.7% of VO₂peak. First ventilatory threshold at 200 W, 146 ± 11 bpm, 81.4%, 69.8% of VO₂peak. The first lactate rise (baseline + 0.5) at 199 W, 146 bpm. Authors: FatMax "may overlap in some cyclists" with VT1 but "is generally ~25% lower". FatMax was no different from 72% of maximum heart rate, the *bottom* of the Norwegian Zone 2. [Own] On these averages peak fat burning is 75 W and 16 beats below the top of Zone 2.
- Achten, Gleeson & Jeukendrup 2002: 18 moderately trained cyclists. Fatmax at 64 ± 4% of VO₂max, 74 ± 3% of maximum heart rate. The "Fatmax zone" (fat oxidation within 10% of its peak) ran from 55 to 72% of VO₂max. "At exercise intensities above Fatmax, fat oxidation rates drop markedly"; negligible above 89% of VO₂max.
- Achten & Jeukendrup 2003: 55 endurance-trained men. Peak rate 0.52 ± 0.15 g/min at 62.5 ± 9.8% of VO₂max. Large variation between individuals even in a homogeneous group. Typical error of the Fatmax estimate about 9.5%.
- Venables 2005: 300 healthy men and women on a treadmill. Peak fat oxidation at 48.3% of VO₂max, 61.5% of maximum heart rate. Fitness, activity and sex explained only 12% of the variation between people.
- Croci 2014 (open access, not yet read in full): 15 recreationally trained men tested twice. Within-person CV of Fatmax 16 to 21%; limits of agreement about ±27 to 32 percentage points of VO₂max. A person's fat max is not a sharp or repeatable point.
- [Own] 0.52 g/min is about 31 g of fat an hour, about 280 kcal. If the top of Zone 2 sits at the upper edge of the fat max zone, the rate there is perhaps 10 to 20% below peak: a few grams of fat an hour.

**Whether fuel mix during the ride matters for body fat.**
- Melanson 2002: 16 adults (8 men, 8 women) in a whole-room calorimeter on three days: sedentary, 400 kcal of exercise at 40% of VO₂max, 400 kcal at 70%. 24-hour energy expenditure and carbohydrate oxidation were higher on both exercise days; "24-h fat oxidation was not different across conditions". "Exercise intensity has no effect on 24-h EE or nutrient oxidation." How the subjects were fed is not in the abstract.
- Melanson, MacLean & Hill 2009, a review: exercise of an hour or less "has little impact on 24-h fat oxidation".
- Keating 2017, meta-analysis of 31 training studies: no difference between interval training and moderate continuous training for body fat percentage or fat mass; comparisons where intervals used less time or energy "tended to favour" continuous training (p = 0.09); neither produced clinically meaningful fat loss.
- [Own] Energy: 200 W against 125 W is 270 kJ more work an hour, roughly 270 kcal more burned (the usual one-for-one rule of thumb, which assumes about 24% efficiency [GK]). That is several times the difference in fat burned.

**Whether lower in the zone gives the same training benefit.**
- No study I know compares the top of Zone 2 with the bottom, or with zone 1, at equal time in trained cyclists. **Check before the chapter is drafted.**
- Sitko 2025: adaptations "might not be unique" to Zone 2 and could come from "slightly higher and lower intensities" (expert opinion).
- Storoschuk 2025 [FT, notes above]: lower intensities give smaller signals; a meta-analysis it cites suggests training below 60% of maximum work rate is not expected to raise mitochondrial content.
- [Own] At a fixed number of hours, riding lower means less work. For a time-limited rider the likelier result is less stimulus, not the same.

**Whether riding easier protects the hard sessions.**
- Seiler 2007 (Finding your zones notes): below the first threshold, recovery of heart rate variability took 5 to 10 minutes after one hour or two; above it, 30 minutes. If the ride is truly below the threshold, going easier still buys little by this marker. Nine highly trained runners; one marker.
- The risk is being above the threshold without knowing: a ceiling set by proxy is uncertain by about 10 beats (Finding your zones), and the threshold falls during a long ride (Stevenson 2022: 21 W after two hours).
- Black 2017: riding 20 W below the threshold for three and a half hours emptied muscle glycogen by 83%. [Own] Harder easy riding uses more carbohydrate, so fuelling is the more direct way to protect the next hard session.

**Draft answer (corrected later on 11 October: see "Correction" in the chapter research below).** In fasted tests peak fat burning falls at about the first lactate rise, so the top of Zone 2 can be fat max; after a carbohydrate meal it falls well below. Either way it is a broad, unrepeatable plateau. For body fat the fuel burned during the ride matters little and the energy burned matters more, which favours the top of the zone. Nothing shows the same training benefit from riding lower at the same hours. A few beats of margin under an estimated ceiling is cheap insurance. Grade C throughout except the 24-hour calorimeter finding (B).

## Correction to what I told George earlier the same day

I told him the top of Zone 2 was "probably not" fat max, on Meixner 2025 alone. Two further papers change that:
- Achten & Jeukendrup 2004 [Abs]: 33 moderately trained endurance athletes, cycle ergometer, 35 W every 3 minutes. Fat max at 63 ± 9% of VO₂max; the first rise of lactate above baseline at 61 ± 5%; "no statistical difference"; the two correlated. "The first rise of lactate concentration occurred at the same intensity as the intensity which elicited maximal fat oxidation rates." Feeding state is not in the abstract; this laboratory's other fat max studies tested after an overnight fast (Achten & Jeukendrup 2003, both papers).
- Achten & Jeukendrup 2003 (J Sports Sci) [Abs]: 11 moderately trained people, fasted overnight, tested 45 minutes after a placebo drink and after 75 g of glucose. Peak fat oxidation fell from 0.46 to 0.33 g/min (28%); fat max fell from 60.1% to 52.0% of VO₂max.
- Meixner 2025 [FT]: riders were told to eat "a carbohydrate-rich meal (i.e., a banana and a jam sandwich) no less than 3 h before each visit". Fat max at 56.0% of VO₂peak; VT1 at 69.8%; first lactate rise at 69.4%.
- Bircher & Knechtle 2004 [Abs]: 20 endurance athletes; of three intensities tested (55, 65, 75% of VO₂peak) fat oxidation was highest at 75%, and their lactate threshold was at 76 to 77%.
- **So:** tested fasted, trained athletes' peak fat burning sits at about the first lactate threshold. Tested after carbohydrate, it sits well below. [Own] The feeding explanation for the gap between Achten 2004 and Meixner 2025 is my inference; protocols also differed. George's belief was closer to right than I said. The practical conclusion (for body fat, energy burned matters more than fuel mix; ride near the top with a margin) does not change.

## What an easy ride asks of the muscle

- Order of recruitment (slow fibres first, fast fibres with rising force or fatigue): textbook physiology [GK]; the size principle is Henneman's.
- Gollnick, Piehl & Saltin 1974 [Abs]: cycling at 30 to 150% of VO₂max, pedalling rates 30 to 120 rpm; glycogen by PAS staining. Glycogen use 2.7 and 7.4 times greater at 64% and 84% of VO₂max than at 31%. "Slow twitch, high oxidative (ST) fibres were the first to lose glycogen at all workloads below VO₂max. Progressive glycogen depletion occurred in fast twitch (FT) fibres as work continued. Large quantities of glycogen remained in the muscle after 3 hr of exercise at low exercise intensity. This was almost exclusively found in FT fibres." Pedalling rate made no difference to the pattern. Number of subjects not in the abstract. That the work was done in Stockholm: [GK].
- Vøllestad 1984 [Abs]: five subjects, exhaustive cycling at 75% of VO₂max. Type I and IIA depleted at the same rate from the start; IIAB and IIB later.
- Storoschuk 2025 [FT], sections 4.1 to 4.3: understanding of signals in Zone 2 "is limited to relatively small and/or inconsistent changes". AMPK activity unchanged after 2 h of Zone 2 exercise, "elevated at exhaustion (~3.5 h)". PGC-1α expression: no change after 30 min (two studies) and about 90 min (one study); the figure lists Popov and colleagues as "No" at 30 min and "Yes" at 60 and 90 min. Prolonged Zone 2 exercise (2 to 3.5 h) lowers muscle glycogen, and glycogen depletion is a mediator of AMPK activation. **My earlier note above ("60 and 90 min raised its expression, 30 min did not") was half the picture: another study found no rise at 90 min.**
- Egan 2010: in the Adaptation notes.
- Dudley, Abraham & Terjung 1982 [Abs]: rats, treadmill, 8 weeks, 5 days a week, 19 protocols; six speeds from 10 to 60 m/min; cytochrome c in three fibre types. "Daily run times resulted in a maximal adaptive change for each running intensity"; asymptotes "dependent on the intensity of running"; "as running intensity was increased, the length of daily run time required to achieve the peak adaptive response was shortened". Fast-twitch white fibres: response "began at 30 m/min (approx 80% VO₂max) and increased exponentially" (200% at 60 m/min). First name Gary: [GK].

## Mølmen, Almquist & Skattebo 2025 [FT]

- Systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science and SPORTDiscus to 2 February 2022: 5,224 records, 557 full texts, 425 articles in the review. Mitochondrial analysis: 353 studies, 506 training groups, 5,650 participants (the abstract's 5,973 is the total across mitochondrial and capillary analyses). Capillaries: 131 articles.
- **Definitions.** ET: "low- or moderate intensity continuous endurance training", below the second ventilatory threshold / 4 mmol/L / 87% of HRmax / 87% of VO₂max / 75% of Wmax. HIT: above that. SIT: maximal efforts of 4 to 90 s. Programmes mixing ET and HIT were classed as HIT. Two authors checked each classification.
- **Fitness classes** (mean VO₂max): untrained 34.8, moderately trained 48.8, well trained 62.2 mL/kg/min.
- **Table 1.** Studies: 237 untrained, 84 moderately trained, 32 well trained. ET training groups: 209, 47, 14; participants in them 2,659, 424, **104**. Men: 74%, 90%, 97%. 36% of untrained groups had a disease. ET programmes averaged 11.4, 6.6 and 7.6 weeks; 42, 28 and 26 sessions; **35.5, 37.7 and 93.2 total hours**. HIT: 23.6, 19.9, 29.2 hours. SIT: 9.5, 6.1, 12.0 hours. Markers: 943 observations, 593 from untrained groups, 254 moderately trained, 96 well trained [Own: 63%, 27%, 10%]. 313 of 506 groups cycled.
- **Mitochondrial content** (five markers pooled: volume density by microscopy and four enzymes), adjusted for weeks, frequency, fitness, muscle mass, disease, sex, age: ET +22.7 ± 4.6%, HIT +27.0 ± 5.1%, SIT +27.0 ± 6.7%; no significant difference (P > 0.138). After 2 weeks: 13.5, 18.7, 21.5%. Log-linear with weeks. Frequency: six sessions a week more than four or two. Not affected by age, sex or disease.
- **Fitness:** well-trained +6.6 ± 6.5% (P = 0.046) across all training; moderately trained 14.7 points more than well trained; untrained 5.8 points more than moderately trained.
- **Per hour:** SIT about 3.9 times ET and 2.3 times HIT; HIT about 1.7 times ET. "Well-trained individuals did not exhibit any change in mitopooled when expressed per hour of exercise training after ET (P = 0.933) and HIT (P = 0.075), but they significantly increased after SIT."
- **Capillaries.** Per fibre: ET +15.0%, HIT +13.3%, SIT +10.4%; no difference (P = 0.556). Density (per mm²): ET +13.3%, HIT +6.8%, SIT no significant change; ET greater than HIT (P = 0.017), which the authors put down to less fibre growth with ET. Gains present by 4 weeks with no further rise. Well trained: +7.1% per fibre (P = 0.102), +2.8% density (P = 0.525).
- **VO₂max**, unadjusted: ET +12.5%, HIT +12.1%, SIT +6.6%. Per hour SIT 4.9 times ET; well trained showed no change per hour in any category.
- **Limitations stated:** short studies, so findings are "most of all generalizable to initial responses"; biopsy variability; "the potential impact of publication bias has not been specifically addressed". No funding; no conflicts.
- Key point in the paper: "training load (intensity × volume) is the most suitable predictor".
- [Own] The ET category runs up to the second threshold, so it includes riding between the thresholds. It cannot isolate easy riding. Storoschuk makes the same criticism.
- First names (Knut Sindre, Nicki Winfield, Øyvind) are in the conflict statement [FT].

## Studies that kept the riding easy

- Granata 2016 [Abs]: 29 healthy men, 4 weeks, 12 sessions: sprint intervals, 4-minute intervals at about 90% of peak power, or continuous riding below the lactate threshold (20 to 36 min at about 65% of peak power, work matched to the interval group). Mitochondrial respiration rose only after sprints (25%). Citrate synthase activity "remained unchanged throughout" in all three.
- Helgerud 2007 [Abs]: 40 moderately trained men, four groups, 3 days a week for 8 weeks, matched for total oxygen consumption: long slow distance at 70% HRmax; lactate threshold at 85%; 15 s on, 15 s off at 90 to 95%; 4 × 4 min at 90 to 95%. VO₂max rose 5.5% (15/15) and 7.2% (4 × 4), significantly more than in the other two groups; stroke volume rose about 10% after interval training. The abstract gives no figures for the two continuous groups. Still paywalled (papers-wanted).
- Matomäki 2023 [FT]: Durability notes. 35 sedentary or recreationally active adults, 10 weeks, 6.8 h/week easy or 1.6 h/week hard. Durability improved similarly; VO₂max rose only in the hard group.
- Scharhag-Rosenberger 2010 [Abs]: 17 sedentary adults (7 men, 10 women, age 42), 12 months of jogging or walking 3 × 45 min a week at 60% of heart rate reserve. Body weight unchanged; body fat −3.4 ± 2.1 percentage points. Peak fat oxidation +0.07 ± 0.08 g/min, at a higher intensity (35, 44, 50% of VO₂max at 0, 6, 12 months). No control group mentioned. Storoschuk calls this "confirmed Zone 2 training" [Rep].
- Evertsen 1999 and 2001 [Abs]: 20 elite junior cross-country skiers (11 men, 9 women), 12 to 15 h a week for 5 months, at moderate intensity (60 to 70% of VO₂max) or high (80 to 90%, "close to the lactate threshold"). Storoschuk: 86% of the moderate group's volume was at the moderate intensity [Rep]. High-intensity group improved more on a 20-minute run; SDH +6%; running speed at the lactate threshold +3.2%. Moderate group: no change in citrate synthase or SDH; lactate threshold speed unchanged (P = 0.54); MCT1 **fell 12%** (P = 0.01). MCT1 unchanged in the high group. [Own] "High" here is between the thresholds or at the second, not above it. What the skiers did before the study is not in the abstracts; a fall in MCT1 could mean the moderate group was doing less intensity than they were used to.
- Schantz, Henriksson & Jansson 1983 [Abs]: six subjects skiing 30 km a day with backpacks, 6 days a week for 8 weeks, at about 45% of VO₂max. "Increases in oxidative enzyme activities and capillarization during training were limited to the" triceps: citrate synthase +90%, capillaries per fibre +40%. Nothing in the thigh. "Prolonged duration of training is not, in itself, sufficient to elicit an adaptive response in the leg muscles." Two weeks of energy deficiency during the trip.
- Boushel 2015 [Abs]: nine volunteers (7 men, 2 women), 42 days of skiing at 60% of HRmax (6 h a day, per Storoschuk [Rep]). Peak VO₂ unchanged (3.52 L/min before and after). Thigh mitochondrial capacity fell from 90 to 70 pmol/s/mg and citrate synthase from 40 to 34 [Own: −22% and −15%]. Capillary density unchanged. A companion paper (2014) reports gains in the arms [Rep, from this abstract].
- Liu 2022 [Abs]: 57 trials from 38 studies, 90% in sedentary people. In sedentary subjects, compared with low intensity (below 50% of VO₂max), capillaries per fibre rose 21% more after moderate continuous training and 54% more after high-intensity intervals. "In already trained subjects, no additional increase in capillarization was observed with various types of training."
- Londeree 1997 [Abs]: 85 study groups from 34 studies. Sedentary subjects raised their lactate or ventilatory threshold at any training intensity (effect size 2.32 against 0.15 in controls); conditioned subjects showed non-significant gains (0.63) and "tended to respond better to high intensity training (ES of 1.1 vs 0.4)".
- Granata 2018 [Rep, Storoschuk]: "exercise performed below 60% maximum work rate … is not expected to improve mitochondrial content or mitochondrial respiratory capacity".
- [Own] I know of no study that compares riding at the top of Zone 2 with riding lower, at equal hours, in trained cyclists, or that adds easy hours to a trained cyclist's programme and measures muscle. Absence from my searches, not proof.

# Fibre types (George's comment on the draft page, 11 October 2026)

George, on the paragraph that introduces slow and fast fibres: "Can we have a bit more detail on these types of fibres - do they look different, are they intertwined with each other, do some people have more of a certain type, is there a spectrum or do they neatly fall into fast and slow twitch". Five paragraphs were added at the top of "What an easy ride asks of the muscle". An automatic reply on the comment gave short answers from general knowledge; what follows is what I could source.

**What the types are, and how they look**
- Moreno-Justicia 2025 [FT], introduction: fibres are "commonly classified into three fiber types in humans: one 'slow' (type 1) and two 'fast' (type 2A and type 2X)", defined by myosin heavy chain isoform, "first based on its ATPase pH lability, and later by its molecular MYH expression".
- Plotkin 2021 [FT], methods section: oxidative-enzyme stains (NADH, SDH), "where darker myofibers indicate a more oxidative phenotype"; myosin ATPase stains give "distinct staining patterns of type I, IIa, and IIx fibers"; antibodies to each myosin; single-fibre gels. Costill 1976 [Abs] stained calf-muscle sections for myosin ATPase and two oxidative enzymes.
- [GK] Slow fibres contract more slowly, have more mitochondria and tire more slowly; slow fibres hold more myoglobin and are redder; unstained fibres cannot be told apart under a microscope; a chicken's dark leg meat and white breast meat. Textbook physiology with no source in the list. Chapter 2 already has the PGC-1α mice whose pale muscles "turned redder" (Lin 2002).
- [GK] A fibre is a single long cell; myosin is the protein that produces force.

**Are they intertwined**
- Downham 1987 [Abs]: "Human skeletal muscles are composed of two distinguishable types of fibres, which in healthy muscles appear to be randomly arranged. Large groups of one fibre type are commonly regarded as evidence of a neuropathological process affecting the peripheral nerves or the nerve cells in the spinal cord."
- Nerve sets type: Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011 [Abs], "Nerve activity is a major control mechanism of the fiber type profile"; Moreno-Justicia 2025 [FT], "The classic view … is that motoneurons define the typology of the fibers within a motor unit".
- [GK] Each nerve cell drives many fibres of one type, scattered among those of other nerve cells (the motor unit). No number is given in the draft.

**Do people differ**
- Coyle 1992 [Abs], already in Chapter 1: 19 trained cyclists, 32 to 76% type I in the thigh.
- Simoneau & Bouchard 1995 [Abs]: "about 25% of North American Caucasian men and women having either less than 35% or more than 65% of type I fiber in their vastus lateralis"; variance in % type I about 15% sampling and technical error, about 40% environmental, about 45% inherited. First names (Jean-Aimé, Claude) from memory [GK].
- Costill 1976 [Abs]: sprint- and endurance-trained athletes "are characterized by distinct fiber compositions". No percentages in the abstract, so none in the draft.
- Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011 [Abs]: proportion "in humans shows significant variability between individuals".

**Can training change the mix** (grade C in the draft)
- Plotkin 2021 [FT]: "longitudinal data have not clearly converged on patterns in fiber type shifts over time, particularly between slow and fast fibers"; resistance, sprint and power training shift IIx and hybrids towards IIa; "Endurance training generally induces a fiber type shift toward a more oxidative phenotype"; Luden: 13 weeks of marathon training in novice runners, type I 42.6 to 48.6% (not used). The review's own conclusion is more positive than my sentence: "Current evidence using the most appropriate techniques suggests a clear ability of fibers to shift between hybrid and pure fibers as well as between slow and fast fiber types." It is a narrative review.
- Bathgate 2018 [Abs]: one pair of male identical twins aged 52, more than 30 years of endurance training against none; the trained twin had "55% more MHC I fibers".
