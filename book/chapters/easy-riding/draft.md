# Chapter 7: What easy riding does to the body

The winter in the Introduction is the reason this book exists: four or five easy rides a week on a trainer in the garage, and then a climb up Sa Calobra more than two minutes faster than I had ever ridden it. I put it down to Zone 2 and the mitochondria it was said to build. The training studies in this chapter do not bear that out, and they point to a plainer explanation.

Since about 2019 a great deal has been claimed for easy riding: that it is the best way to build mitochondria, that it trains fat burning and lactate clearance, and that its upper edge, Zone 2, has effects no other intensity does. That is a lot to ask of a pace at which you can hold a conversation. The training studies support a simpler account. Easy riding provides a small stimulus that can be repeated in large amounts.

A professional can train for most of the day; an amateur with a job and a family may have a handful of hours a week. Easy riding has a different place in those two weeks.

## What an easy ride asks of the muscle

A muscle is a bundle of fibres, each a single long cell, and the fibres are not all alike. What sets them apart is the kind of myosin they build, the protein that produces force. Human limb muscle makes three kinds, which gives three named fibre types: type I, called slow-twitch, and two fast-twitch types, IIA and IIX {shadow @morenojusticia2025}. Slow fibres contract more slowly, are rich in mitochondria and are slow to tire. Fast fibres produce force quickly and tire sooner.

The two kinds differ in colour, because slow fibres hold more myoglobin, the red protein that stores oxygen in muscle. A chicken shows it at the table. The legs, which carry the bird all day, are dark meat, and the breast, which powers a few seconds of flapping, is white. Human muscles are not sorted that way. Slow and fast fibres lie mixed together through the same muscle, in an arrangement that looks random, and a thin slice has to be stained before the types can be told apart under a microscope [@plotkin2021]{shadow @downham1987}. A fibre takes its type from the nerve cell that drives it {shadow @schiaffino2011}, and each nerve cell drives many fibres of one type, scattered among the fibres of its neighbours.

The mix varies widely from person to person. Chapter 1 described 19 trained cyclists whose thigh muscle ran from 32% slow fibres to 76% {shadow @coyle1992}.

How far training can change the mix is not settled [@plotkin2021], and a fibre's type is a separate matter from its contents. The mitochondria inside it, which are the subject of this chapter, increase with training without the type having to change [@plotkin2021].

The nervous system calls on these fibres in a fixed order. Slow fibres are used first, and fast fibres are added as the force required goes up, or as the slow fibres tire. The classic demonstration was published in 1974. Philip Gollnick, Karin Piehl and Bengt Saltin had people ride at intensities from 30% of VO₂max upward, took samples of thigh muscle, and stained them for glycogen, the muscle's store of carbohydrate. A fibre that has been working uses up its glycogen and stains pale. At every intensity below VO₂max the slow fibres emptied first, and the fast fibres began to empty only as the ride went on. After three hours at low intensity a large amount of glycogen was still in the muscle, nearly all of it in fast fibres [@gollnick1974]. At 75% of VO₂max, by contrast, a later study found the slow fibres and the IIA fibres working together from the first minute {shadow @vollestad1984}.

Chapter 2 ended on the rule that a fibre adapts only if it was disturbed. An easy ride disturbs the slow fibres. It reaches the fast ones only if it goes on long enough for the slow ones to run down.

The disturbance is also small. Below the first threshold the cell's chemistry settles into a steady state, so the energy sensor AMPK, which responds to a shortfall, has little to respond to. Chapter 2 described the Dublin experiment in which a ride at 40% of VO₂max did not activate AMPK and raised the messenger RNA for PGC-1α less than half as far as a ride at 80% {shadow @egan2010}. In 2025 four Canadian physiologists collected the measurements of this kind that they could find for exercise at Zone 2 intensity, in a review they called *Much Ado About Zone 2*, and concluded that the signals are small, inconsistent, or both [@storoschuk2025].

Duration seems to help, up to a point. In the studies that review lists, AMPK activity was unchanged after two hours of easy exercise and raised at three and a half. The message for PGC-1α rose after 60 and 90 minutes in one study and not after 30, and did not rise after 90 in another. Two to three and a half hours of easy exercise also lowers muscle glycogen, and low glycogen is itself a signal to AMPK [@storoschuk2025].

How intensity and duration combine was mapped in rats in 1982. Gary Dudley and colleagues trained groups of rats on treadmills for eight weeks on 19 different schedules, covering six running speeds and several daily durations at each, and measured a mitochondrial protein, cytochrome c, in each type of muscle fibre. At any one speed, running for longer each day produced more adaptation up to a ceiling and then no more. The height of the ceiling was set by the speed. The faster the running, the higher the ceiling and the shorter the daily run needed to reach it. The least-used fast fibres did not adapt at all until the speed reached about 80% of VO₂max, and above that their response rose steeply [@dudley1982].

Those were rats, and their minutes and speeds do not translate to a cyclist. The shape is what matters, and the human evidence below is consistent with it. More easy riding adds adaptation at a diminishing rate, the rate depends on how easy it is, and some fibres are barely touched by it.

## What it builds

### One study, up close: 353 training studies

The largest test of what continuous riding builds over a training programme was published in 2025 by Knut Sindre Mølmen, Nicki Winfield Almquist and Øyvind Skattebo, working in Norway [@molmen2025].

They searched three databases for every study that had trained people and measured their muscle before and after. Of 5,224 records, 425 papers met their criteria. The analysis of mitochondria used 353 studies, containing 506 separate training groups and 5,650 people. For each group they recorded how long, how often and how hard the training was, and how fit the people were at the start. The outcome was the percentage change in the muscle's mitochondrial content, pooled from five markers: the volume of mitochondria seen under an electron microscope, and four mitochondrial enzymes.

Each group's training was put in one of three classes. Sprint training meant all-out efforts of 4 to 90 seconds. High-intensity training meant work above the second threshold. Everything continuous below the second threshold went into a single class that the authors call endurance training, at low or moderate intensity [@molmen2025].

All three raised mitochondrial content, and by similar amounts: 23% for continuous training, 27% for high-intensity training and 27% for sprints, differences that were not statistically significant. Gains were visible after two weeks. Training six times a week produced more than four, and four more than two. Age, sex and disease made no difference to the response [@molmen2025].

Per hour of training the three were far apart. High-intensity training raised mitochondrial content about 1.7 times as much per hour as continuous training, and sprints about 3.9 times as much. The same held for VO₂max, where sprints were about 4.9 times as efficient per hour as continuous training [@molmen2025].

Capillaries told a slightly different story. The number of capillaries around each fibre rose by 15% with continuous training, 13% with high-intensity training and 10% with sprints, with no significant difference. Capillary density, the number per square millimetre of muscle, rose more with continuous training (13% against 7%), which the authors put down to the fibres themselves growing less. Most of the gain had arrived within four weeks [@molmen2025].

The authors sorted the groups into untrained (an average VO₂max of 35 mL/kg/min), moderately trained (49) and well trained (62). The untrained gained most. Well-trained people gained about 7% in mitochondrial content, averaged over every kind of training. Expressed per hour, well-trained people showed no measurable gain from continuous training or from high-intensity training, and a gain only from sprints. Their capillaries did not change significantly with any training, and nor did their VO₂max per hour [@molmen2025].

What the analysis says about easy riding in particular is limited, first of all by its own categories. The continuous class runs all the way up to the second threshold, so it mixes easy riding with riding between the thresholds. The 23% belongs to that mixture. The analysis cannot say how much of it easy riding alone would produce, and the Canadian review makes the same objection [@storoschuk2025].

The studies are short and small in dose. The average programme of continuous training lasted 11 weeks in untrained people and seven in the others, and in untrained and moderately trained people it added up to about 36 hours of training in total. A cyclist riding eight hours a week does that in a month. The authors say their findings describe mainly the first response to a change in training [@molmen2025].

And the well trained are barely represented. Of 270 groups doing continuous training, 14 were well trained, with 104 people between them. Nearly two-thirds of all the measurements came from untrained people, 36% of whose groups had a disease, and three-quarters or more of the participants in every class were men [@molmen2025]. The authors did not test for publication bias, which they acknowledge. They declared no funding and no conflicts of interest.

What the analysis establishes is that continuous riding below the second threshold builds mitochondria and capillaries in people who are not yet well trained, about as much per programme as harder training and much less per hour. {grade A} For riders who are already well trained it found no measurable gain from more of it, on thin evidence. {grade B}

## Studies that kept the riding easy

The studies that kept training clearly below the first threshold are small, but let us separate easy riding from the broader continuous-training category.

At low volume, four weeks produced no measurable gain in mitochondrial content. In a 2016 study from David Bishop's laboratory, 29 men did 12 sessions of sprints, four-minute intervals, or continuous riding below the lactate threshold for 20 to 36 minutes. Citrate synthase, the usual marker of mitochondrial content, did not change in any group, and the mitochondria's capacity to use oxygen rose only after sprints [@granata2016].

At equal work, easy training does not raise the ceiling. A Norwegian trial assigned 40 moderately trained men to four ways of running, three days a week for eight weeks, with the total oxygen consumed matched between groups: slow running at 70% of maximum heart rate, steady running at 85%, and two kinds of interval. VO₂max rose by 5.5% and 7.2% in the interval groups, significantly more than in the other two, and the heart's stroke volume rose by about 10% after intervals {shadow @helgerud2007}. The summary of the paper gives no figures for the slow group.

With enough hours, easy training does improve some things as well as hard training does. In a Finnish trial that Chapter 9 describes, 35 sedentary or recreationally active adults did ten weeks of either easy riding, averaging 6.8 hours a week, or intervals, averaging 1.6 hours. Durability, the ability to hold a steady effort for three hours without drifting, improved by about the same amount in both groups. VO₂max rose only in the interval group {shadow @matomaki2023}. The easy group needed four times the hours to match the interval group on one measure and did not match it on the other.

In athletes who are already elite, easy training holds what they have. Twenty elite junior cross-country skiers trained 12 to 15 hours a week for five months, one group mostly at 60–70% of VO₂max and the other mostly at 80–90%, close to the lactate threshold. The harder group improved more in a 20-minute run and raised their speed at the lactate threshold by 3.2%. The easier group's threshold speed did not change, and neither group's citrate synthase did {shadow @evertsen1999; @evertsen2001}.

The study furthest from ordinary training is a ski expedition. In 1983 six people skied 30 kilometres a day with backpacks, six days a week for eight weeks, at about 45% of VO₂max. In their arms, which were doing work they were not used to, citrate synthase rose by 90% and the number of capillaries per fibre by 40%. In their thighs nothing changed. The authors concluded that long duration is not, by itself, enough to make the leg muscles adapt {shadow @schantz1983}.

What counts as easy is relative to what the muscle is already used to. The skiers' legs were already trained for work at walking pace, so many hours a day of it gave them nothing new to adapt to, while their arms had never worked so hard and for them the same expedition was a training programme. The Canadian review presses this point hardest, citing an earlier analysis by Bishop's group which suggests that exercise below about 60% of a person's maximum work rate is not expected to raise mitochondrial content at all [@storoschuk2025]{shadow @granata2018}. An older pooled analysis of 34 studies agrees: sedentary people raised their thresholds at any training intensity, while people who were already trained showed gains that were not statistically significant and tended to respond better to high intensity {shadow @londeree1997}.

Capillaries follow the same pattern. A 2022 analysis of 57 training groups found that in sedentary people, capillaries per fibre grew 21% more after moderate continuous training than after low-intensity training, and 54% more after high-intensity intervals. In people who were already trained, no kind of training added any {shadow @liu2022}.

I know of no study that adds easy hours to a trained cyclist's week and measures the muscle, or that compares riding at the top of Zone 2 with riding lower at the same hours. The evidence that exists says that easy training builds a great deal in the untrained, and that in the trained, at the volumes and durations anyone has studied, it maintains more than it builds. {grade B}

## Three claims

### That Zone 2 is the best way to build mitochondria

It is not. Per hour, continuous riding builds less mitochondrial content than harder riding, and the continuous riding in that comparison was harder than Zone 2 on average [@molmen2025]. The signals from a single easy session are small [@storoschuk2025]. In the one four-week study that isolated riding below the threshold, mitochondrial content did not move [@granata2016]. The claim described in the Introduction, that easy riding is the single most effective way to build mitochondria, has no trial behind it.

The opposite claim, that easy riding does nothing for mitochondria, is also wrong. Continuous training raised mitochondrial content by nearly a quarter across 270 training groups [@molmen2025], and enough of it, in a muscle that is not yet used to it, clearly works. The Canadian review's conclusion is carefully limited: Zone 2 has not been shown to be the optimal intensity, and people with little time should put harder riding first. It is about the general population, and says of athletes that the question is open [@storoschuk2025].

### That Zone 2 is where you burn the most fat

> **From the saddle.** I ride at the top of where I think my Zone 2 is, partly because I think that is the "fat max". Managing my weight is definitely a goal, so that's why I do the top of Zone 2. But is that really fat max? Could I protect my hard rides by riding even easier?

Fat burning rises with intensity to a peak and then falls as carbohydrate takes over. Where the peak sits depends on what you have eaten. In 33 trained endurance athletes tested in Asker Jeukendrup's laboratory, the intensity of peak fat burning and the intensity of the first rise in lactate were the same, 63% and 61% of VO₂max [@achten2004]. That laboratory's practice was to test after an overnight fast, though the paper's summary does not say so of this study. When it gave 11 trained people 75 g of glucose 45 minutes before the same test, the peak rate of fat burning fell by 28% and the intensity at which it occurred fell from 60% of VO₂max to 52% [@achten2003feeding]. In the 50 cyclists of Chapter 5, who had been told to eat a carbohydrate meal three hours beforehand, fat burning peaked at 56% of VO₂max and the first threshold came at 70%. The gap was 75 W and 16 beats per minute [@meixner2025].

![Three separate studies, four feeding conditions. Only the middle pair is a direct comparison: the same people tested fasted and after glucose, in the same laboratory, whose summary gives no lactate figure [@achten2003feeding]. The top group is from Achten and Jeukendrup, with the feeding not stated [@achten2004], and the bottom from Meixner and colleagues, where the lactate marker is a rise of 0.5 mmol/L above baseline [@meixner2025]. The studies tested different people in different ways, so the gaps between groups are not the effect of feeding alone.](figures/fatmax-feeding.svg)

Peak fat burning may sit near the Zone 2 ceiling when fasted and lower after eating. Feeding is my explanation for the difference between studies, but their test protocols differed too. The peak is broad: in moderately trained cyclists, fat burning stayed within 10% of its peak from 55% to 72% of VO₂max {shadow @achten2002}. When 15 men were tested twice, each man's peak moved by 16–21% {shadow @croci2014}.

For body fat, where the peak sits matters much less than it seems. Sixteen adults each spent three days in a sealed room that measured everything they burned. On one day they rested, on one they did 400 kcal of exercise at 40% of VO₂max, and on one they did 400 kcal at 70%. Over the full 24 hours, the fat they burned did not differ between the three days [@melanson2002]. A review by the same group concluded that exercise of an hour or less has little effect on fat burned over a day {shadow @melanson2009}. The likely reason is that the body settles its fuel account over the day, so that a ride which burned mostly fat is followed by hours that burn a little less. {grade B}

Training trials agree. A pooled analysis of 31 studies found no difference in body fat between people assigned to intervals and people assigned to steady moderate training, and neither produced a loss the authors considered clinically meaningful [@keating2017]. Training at fat max does reduce weight in people with obesity, by about 4 kg across 11 trials of 8 to 20 weeks {shadow @chavezguevara2020}, though the summary of that analysis does not say what the comparison groups did.

What differs between the top of Zone 2 and the bottom is the energy. On the averages of the 50 cyclists, the first threshold is 75 W above the fed-state fat max [@meixner2025]. An hour at the higher power is 270 kilojoules more work, which is roughly 270 kcal more burned, against a difference in fat burned during the ride of probably a few grams. A rider who wants to lose weight has a reason to ride at the top of Zone 2, and the reason is the energy.

### That Zone 2 trains the clearing of lactate

The idea comes from the 2018 paper described in Chapter 6, in which professional endurance athletes carried less lactate and burned more fat than moderately active people or patients with metabolic syndrome {shadow @sanmillan2018}. Its reasoning is sound as far as it goes. Mitochondria are where both fat and lactate are burned. A muscle with more mitochondrial capacity can meet a given power while burning more fat and letting less lactate build up, which is the explanation Chapter 2 gave for why trained riders have higher thresholds. So the lactate and fat-burning curves from an exercise test are a window on how much mitochondrial capacity a person has, and the authors' name for a body that switches easily between fuels is metabolic flexibility.

The step from there to a training prescription is the weak one. The paper compared three groups of different people at one moment. It did not train anybody. Professionals differ from patients in years of training of every kind, in how much of it they do, and in whatever made them professionals in the first place, and a comparison of that sort cannot say which of those produced the difference. It is a description of what a very fit body looks like, and it is used as if it were evidence about how to build one.

The one training study I found that measured the machinery directly points the other way. In the elite skiers described above, the researchers also measured MCT1, the transporter that carries lactate into muscle fibres to be burned. After five months of mostly easy training it had fallen by 12%. In the group that trained near the lactate threshold it was unchanged, and theirs was the group whose threshold speed improved {shadow @evertsen2001}. The study had 20 skiers, and the summary does not say how they had trained before, so the fall may reflect a step down from harder training. It does not support the idea that easy riding is what builds lactate clearance. {grade C}

### Reading the research: who chose the studies?

Two reviews run through this chapter, and they sound as if they disagree. One says that continuous training builds as much mitochondrial content as intervals. The other says Zone 2 is not the best way to build mitochondria. They rest on much the same body of studies.

They are different kinds of document. The Norwegian analysis is a systematic review. Its authors wrote down in advance which databases they would search and which studies would qualify, screened 5,224 records, and report how many they excluded and why [@molmen2025]. Another team following the same rules should arrive at nearly the same set of studies. The Canadian paper is a narrative review, and says so: its authors did not run a systematic search, and drew on reference lists, on papers they knew, and on articles shared through social media [@storoschuk2025]. A narrative review is an argument made by experts. It can be right, and this one mostly is, but the reader has no way to know which studies were left out.

It also helps to ask what question each was answering. The Norwegian authors asked how much each kind of training changes muscle, per programme and per hour. The Canadian authors asked whether Zone 2 is the *optimal* intensity for people who are inactive or doing about two and a half hours of exercise a week. "Not shown to be optimal" is a much weaker statement than "shown not to work", and the paper is often quoted as if it had made the second.

And look at who is writing. One of the Canadian authors declares an advisory role and shares in a longevity company whose services relate in part to exercise, and has spent much of his career showing what short hard sessions can do [@storoschuk2025]. That does not make the review wrong. The Norwegian authors declare no interests, did not test for publication bias, and put easy and moderate riding in one category, which happens to be the choice that decides what their headline number means for Zone 2. Both papers need reading past the summary. What survives is the ground they share: easy riding builds mitochondria, and harder riding builds more per hour.

## What easy riding is good for

Easy riding earns its place when recovery, rather than time, limits how much you can train.

In Seiler's runners, the heart-rate-variability marker returned to normal within 5 to 10 minutes of an easy session, against 30 minutes after harder sessions {shadow @seiler2007}. Chapter 12 examines how many hard sessions a rider can absorb in a week. Easy riding lets you add work around them.

So easy riding is how a rider with time turns that time into training. The Norwegian authors found that training load, the product of volume and intensity, was the best predictor of the gain in mitochondrial content [@molmen2025], and easy riding is the only way to raise the volume term very far. This is why the athletes of Chapter 8 do so much of it. It does not follow that a rider with four hours a week should spend them the same way.

Easy riding may also build things that short studies cannot see. Chapter 2 described changes to the heart over months and years; nearly every trial here lasted weeks. The case that years of easy volume build an engine that intervals alone cannot rests on mechanism and what elite athletes do. No trial has run long enough to test it. {grade C}

## How easy, and how long

This book's rule for an easy ride is that it should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions. For a rider on six to ten hours a week that means the top of the easy range, Zone 2. At equal hours it is more work, more energy burned and a larger disturbance to more of the muscle than riding lower, and the ski expedition shows what can happen when the intensity is too low for a muscle that is already trained. No trial has compared the top of the range with the bottom.

The best evidence against the rule is that Olympic champion skiers do most of their easy training well below Zone 2. Chapter 8 gives their figures, and my reason for thinking they follow the same rule with different numbers: they fit about 15 easy hours a week around their hard sessions {shadow @tonnessen2014}, where a seven-hour rider has five or six and a day or more between hard sessions. {grade C}

If the ride is truly below the first threshold, Seiler's runners suggest that riding easier still buys very little extra recovery {shadow @seiler2007}. Nine runners and one marker of recovery are enough to show a direction and not much more.

The risk at the top is of being over the threshold without knowing. Chapter 6 put the uncertainty of a ceiling found without a lactate test at about 10 beats {shadow @olieslagers2026}, and the threshold itself falls during a long ride {shadow @stevenson2022}. A margin of a few beats under the estimated ceiling costs little and covers both.

The better way to protect a hard session is to arrive at it fuelled. Riding near the top of Zone 2 uses more carbohydrate than riding lower: in the 1974 study, glycogen was used 2.7 times as fast at 64% of VO₂max as at 31% [@gollnick1974]. Chapter 13 covers how to replace it. That last step is my inference. No study here tested whether eating more or riding easier does more for the next day's intervals.

On duration, the mechanism favours long rides over many short ones, and nothing more precise can be said. The signalling studies suggest that an easy ride has to last well over an hour before the sensors register much [@storoschuk2025], the glycogen staining suggests that fast fibres join in only late in a long ride [@gollnick1974], and the rat study shows a ceiling beyond which more minutes add nothing [@dudley1982]. None of that tells a cyclist whether one three-hour ride is worth more than three of an hour. It does suggest that a week of 45-minute easy rides is the form of easy riding with the least to recommend it on mechanism, and I say that as someone who spent a winter doing exactly that and got faster.

> **From the saddle.** Before that winter I'd been doing mainly high-intensity sessions, mostly racing online on Zwift, and rather inconsistently. At the weekends I rode with friends, and those rides were generally high intensity too. It came to maybe three or four hours a week. That winter it was about three to five hours a week. I generally felt better, and I fell ill less often with coughs and colds.

My hours changed little, from three or four a week to three to five. What changed was the regularity: four or five sessions every week, a frequency at which the Norwegian analysis found larger gains [@molmen2025]. Since the evidence favours harder riding per hour, I think regularity explains my improvement better than the lower intensity. One rider and one climb cannot establish that. Easy riding was simply something I could do week after week and feel better for.

## Who it is for

A new or returning rider gains from easy riding in almost any form, because a muscle that is not used to training adapts to nearly anything, and most of the 23% in the Norwegian analysis was measured in people like that [@molmen2025].

For anyone riding to manage their weight, the hours and the power matter and the fuel mix does not.

A trained rider with only a few hours a week gets the least from it. The muscle is already used to the stimulus, the hours are too few for volume to do its work, and per hour every harder kind of riding does more. For that rider easy riding fills the days between the hard sessions, and the hard sessions are where most of the training is.

That reading comes from studies that were mostly not designed to sort riders this way, and Part III tests it against the trials that compared easy riding with intervals directly. {grade C}

A trained rider with many hours can use easy riding to add work once the hard sessions are in place. That is the position of the best endurance athletes in the world, who do most of their training easy, year after year.
