## The question

What does riding below the first threshold change in the body, how much, and do the claims made for Zone 2 hold?

## The argument (to approve)

1. **The chapter's position, stated in its first paragraph:** easy riding is a small stimulus, smaller per minute than any harder riding, that costs so little it can be repeated in very large amounts. What it does, it does through the amount. So what is true of it for a professional can be poor advice for a rider with a few hours a week.
2. An easy ride recruits the slow fibres and reaches the fast ones only late in a long ride (Gollnick 1974). The signal to the cell is small; duration helps up to a ceiling set by intensity (Storoschuk 2025; Dudley 1982, in rats).
3. The largest evidence (Mølmen 2025, read in full, the close-up): continuous training raises mitochondrial content 23% against 27% for intervals and sprints, and per hour intervals do 1.7 times and sprints 3.9 times as much. **But its "continuous" class is everything below the second threshold, its average programme totals about 36 hours, and only 104 well-trained people did continuous training, with no measurable gain per hour.** Grade A that it works in the not-yet-trained; grade B that it adds little in the well trained.
4. The few studies that kept the riding clearly easy: no change in four weeks at low volume (Granata 2016); no rise in VO₂max at matched work (Helgerud 2007); durability improved, VO₂max did not, on 6.8 h a week (Matomäki 2023); fat burning improved over a year in sedentary adults (Scharhag-Rosenberger 2010); elite skiers held but did not improve on five months of mostly easy training (Evertsen 1999, 2001); on ski expeditions the arms adapted and the legs did not, or lost mitochondrial capacity (Schantz 1983, Boushel 2015). What is easy is relative to what the muscle is used to. Grade B.
5. **Three claims, each answered plainly:**
   - Best way to build mitochondria: no. (Nor is "does nothing" right.)
   - Where you burn the most fat: true fasted, false after carbohydrate; and for body fat the fuel mix during a ride matters little, the energy matters (grade B). George's own question and words open this section.
   - Trains lactate clearance: rests on a comparison of different people, not on training; the one training study with lactate transporters points the other way (grade C).
6. What easy riding is good for is cost: it lasts for hours and leaves little to recover from, so it is how a rider with time raises training load. Per hour it ranks last; per unit of recovery it ranks first. The claim that years of easy volume build what intervals cannot is untested (grade C).
7. How easy and how long: **the book's rule (11 October, to confirm): an easy ride should carry as much work as it can without taking anything from the hard sessions; on six to ten hours that is Zone 2, on 15 or more it is lower, which is what champion skiers do (Chapter 8).** Grade C, no trial; keep a few beats of margin; protect hard sessions by fuelling; short easy rides have the least to recommend them on mechanism.
8. Who it is for: the untrained (anything works), least the trained rider with few hours, most the trained rider with many. For weight, hours and power matter, fuel mix does not. Grade C.

## Weak points

- **Every primary training study except Matomäki 2023 is abstract only.** The ones that carry most weight and should be read in full: Evertsen 1999 and 2001, Schantz 1983, Boushel 2015, Helgerud 2007, Achten & Jeukendrup 2004, Melanson 2002.
- **The central gap:** I know of no study that adds easy hours to a trained cyclist's week and measures muscle, or compares the top of Zone 2 with lower at equal hours. "The top beats the bottom" and "who it is for" are reasoning, graded C.
- The feeding explanation for where fat max sits is my inference; the three studies also differ in protocol. Whether Achten & Jeukendrup 2004 tested fasted is not in its abstract.
- Granata 2018's "below 60% of maximum work rate" is taken from Storoschuk, not from the paper.
- The ski-expedition explanation (legs already trained for walking) is the likely one, and the draft says "likely". Both expeditions involved some energy deficit.
- The Evertsen skiers' earlier training is unknown, so the fall in MCT1 may be detraining from harder work. The draft says so.
- Dudley 1982 is in rats.
- Gollnick 1974: number of subjects not known.
- "For most cyclists it is most of what they do" has no source.
- First names and "working in Norway" for Mølmen's group are from the paper; other first names (Gollnick, Dudley, Jeukendrup) are from memory.
- Living people named: San-Millán (as author of the 2018 paper, described as sound reasoning with a weak final step) and, without his name, the Canadian review's author with a declared interest. Both statements come from the papers. No rider or team is named.
