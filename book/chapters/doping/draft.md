## Ninety-six finishers

The 1998 Tour de France started in Dublin on 11 July with 189 riders. Three days earlier a car belonging to the Festina team had been stopped at the French-Belgian border carrying EPO, steroids and other drugs. The team was expelled after the sixth stage. Six more teams left the race before the end, and 96 riders reached Paris [@festina; @tour1998].

Fourteen years later the United States Anti-Doping Agency banned Lance Armstrong, the rider who won the next seven Tours, for life and struck out his results. Its published decision described a team-wide doping programme [@usada2012]. He admitted it the following January. No winner is listed for those seven races {shadow @tour1998}.

I assume today's riders are clean. Nothing here is meant to suggest otherwise about anyone, and nobody is connected with doping in this chapter unless an official body has found it or the rider has admitted it. What I do think is that professional teams look for every advantage the rules allow, and that the rules keep having to catch up with them.

## What they were trying to achieve

The commission that investigated cycling's doping history in 2015 summarised decades of it in one sentence. The aims, it wrote, had stayed the same throughout: to raise the blood's capacity to carry oxygen, and to build muscle and speed recovery [@circ2015]. The drugs changed as new ones reached the market and as tests caught up with old ones, while those two targets stayed fixed.

The second aim covers testosterone, growth hormone and the corticosteroids, which riders told the commission were used both to dull pain and to strip weight. The first is the one that defined the era, and it maps directly onto the model in Chapter 1. Among well-trained riders, VO₂max is limited mainly by how much oxygen the heart and blood can deliver to the muscles. Training raises delivery slowly, over years, and by amounts that shrink as a rider approaches their ceiling. A drug that raises it by a tenth in a month is acting on the one link in the chain that an elite athlete can no longer improve much by working harder.

One way to do it was to transfuse blood: a rider had a bag or two of their own blood taken out and stored, waited some weeks for the body to replace it, and had the red cells put back before a race. The other, from about 1990, was a drug that made the body produce the extra red cells itself.

## How it works: the biology of EPO

Erythropoietin, EPO for short, is a hormone that everyone makes. Its job is to keep the number of red blood cells matched to the body's need for oxygen.

It is made mainly in the kidneys, by cells in the outer layer called the cortex [@jelkmann2011]. Those cells contain a sensor for oxygen. The sensor is a protein, hypoxia-inducible factor or HIF, which the cell manufactures continuously and, as long as oxygen is plentiful, destroys almost as fast as it makes it. The enzymes that mark HIF for destruction need oxygen to work. When the oxygen reaching the kidney falls, because you have lost blood, or moved to altitude, or become anaemic, those enzymes slow down, HIF survives, and it switches on the gene for EPO.

EPO then travels in the blood to the bone marrow, where red cells are made. The marrow constantly produces red cell precursors, many of which die before maturing. EPO is the signal that lets them live. More EPO means more precursors survive, multiply and mature into red cells, each packed with haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen [@jelkmann2011]. As the red cell count rises, more oxygen reaches the kidney, HIF is destroyed again, and EPO production falls back. It is a thermostat for the blood.

Altitude and heat, covered elsewhere in this book, work through the same thermostat. Altitude training lowers the oxygen in the blood, so the kidney makes more EPO for as long as you stay high. Heat training, on one theory, dilutes the blood until the kidney reads it as short of red cells. Both are slow and both stop when the stimulus stops.

People whose kidneys fail lose the ability to make EPO and become severely anaemic. In the 1980s the human gene was inserted into cultured cells to manufacture the hormone, and recombinant EPO was approved in the United States as a treatment for the anaemia of kidney failure in June 1989 {shadow @epogenlabel}. For those patients it replaced regular blood transfusions, and it remains one of the most useful drugs biotechnology has produced.

Injected into a healthy person, the same drug overrides the thermostat. The marrow receives a signal to make red cells that the body doesn't need. What happens next was measured in 2007 by Carsten Lundby, who appears in the heat training chapter, in eight healthy volunteers given EPO for 15 weeks [@lundby2007]. Their red cell volume rose from 2,933 mL to about 3,200 mL. Their plasma volume fell, from 3,645 mL to about 3,270 mL. Total blood volume barely changed. The body, in other words, made room for the new red cells by removing liquid, and between 38% and 54% of the rise in the blood's oxygen content came from that concentration, not from the extra cells.

That is the reverse of what heat does, which is why a team scientist in the heat training chapter describes altitude and heat as opposites. It is also why the measure everyone talked about was haematocrit, the percentage of the blood's volume taken up by red cells. Riders told the 2015 commission that their natural levels were in the low to middle 40s [@circ2015]. EPO pushed the number up from both directions at once, by adding cells and by taking away plasma, and blood with that many cells in it is thicker and harder to pump.

## How much it was worth

In the laboratory the effect is large. In a second study from the same group, eight healthy non-athletes given EPO raised their VO₂max by 12.6% in four weeks, and the time they could ride at 80% of it rose by 54% {shadow @thomsen2007}. A 2025 review of 10 studies in well-trained athletes found the same direction in all of them: haemoglobin up, VO₂max up, peak power usually up [@alberdi2025]. {grade A} for the claim that EPO raises VO₂max.

Transfusion works too, and it takes very little blood. In a double-blind study, highly trained men who were given back about 135 mL of their own red cells, roughly half of what one donated bag contains, improved their power in a time trial from 213 W to 223 W, about 5%. After a sham transfusion nothing changed {shadow @bejder2019}.

What these numbers meant in a professional race is harder to establish, because nobody can run a trial in the Tour. The commission's report says it "was told" that gains had been 10% to 15% in the years of unrestricted use, and perhaps 3% to 5% from the small doses riders moved to once blood values were being tracked [@circ2015]. Those figures are testimony from people inside the sport. They aren't measurements, and the report doesn't say who offered them.

Race speeds give indirect support. One analysis took the fifth-placed finisher in each Tour, Giro and Vuelta from 1990 to 2009, to avoid depending on any one champion. Average speed rose by 0.16 km/h a year until 2004 and then fell by 0.22 km/h a year [@perneger2010]. Its author read the turn as compatible with anti-doping efforts taking hold. But a second analysis, of the winning times of 181 Grand Tours going back to 1947, found no sharp break in the 1990s and concluded that the effect of EPO on results may have been overestimated [@lodewijkx2011]. Speed depends on the route, the weather, the tactics and the tarmac as well as the riders, and two careful people looking at similar data reached different conclusions.

### One study, up close: EPO up Mont Ventoux

In the spring of 2016 a clinical research unit in Leiden tested EPO on amateur cyclists the way a new medicine is tested, and ended the trial with a race up Mont Ventoux [@heuberger2017].

The race came about 12 days after the last injection, with haemoglobin still 12% above where it started, and 40 of the 44 riders who started it finished [@heuberger2017]. The authors' conclusion is easily shortened to "EPO doesn't work", and the trial does not show that. For the avoidance of doubt, this book is not suggesting that you find out.

### Reading the research: when a trial finds nothing

A result of "no difference" can mean that there is no effect, or that the study could not have seen one. The way to tell is the confidence interval, the range of true effects the data are compatible with.

In their planning, the authors worked out the size of effect that would matter: a gain in sustained power of a little over 3%, which they estimated was worth about two minutes on a climb like Ventoux [@heuberger2017]. Now look at what the race could detect. The difference in time between the groups was 0.3%, with a 95% confidence interval from 8.3% faster on EPO to 9.6% slower. On a 100-minute climb that is a range of roughly eight minutes either way. A two-minute effect is well inside it. So is a five-minute one.

The paper itself gives the reasons the race was so imprecise: an 85 km/h wind and 5 °C at the summit that afternoon, four riders giving up from exhaustion, and one attempt each, at the end of a long day [@heuberger2017].

The 45-minute laboratory test was far better controlled, and it is the stronger evidence. Its result was a difference of 5.9 W in favour of EPO, with an interval from 0.9 W the wrong way to 12.7 W the right way. As a percentage, that runs from about zero to about 4.5%. The best estimate is a gain of 2%. The commission was told that micro-dosing was worth 3% to 5%. The trial's own laboratory time trial does not rule that out [@heuberger2017; @circ2015].

Who was studied matters too. These riders trained five to six hours a week, which the authors acknowledge is far below a professional's load. For an amateur, a 2% change in sustained power is lost among everything else that varies from one rider to the next on the day. For a professional racing against riders who have all spent years removing those other differences, the same 2% is a decisive margin.

Two months of a professional's dose raised these recreational riders' VO₂max measurably, and most who received it could not tell. The sustained-power result remained uncertain. {grade B} for the claim that EPO improves sustained performance in the laboratory; {grade C}, resting on testimony and race speeds, for its size on the road in professionals.

## The risks

The risk everybody knows about is that EPO thickened riders' blood until their hearts stopped in the night. The story usually comes with a number: 18 Dutch and Belgian cyclists dead between 1987 and 1990.

The 2015 commission repeats it, and its wording is careful. "The media speculated", it says, that the deaths of 18 European cyclists were related to EPO [@circ2015].

In 2011 a Spanish academic, Bernat López, went looking for the evidence behind the number [@lopez2011]. He collected 61 texts that mention the deaths, 36 of them academic. Only two cited anything resembling an original source, and both of those were newspaper articles. The rest cited one another, or nobody. The details changed from text to text: the number of dead ran from "half a dozen" to "around 40", the nationalities from Dutch and Belgian to Scandinavian, and the dates across two decades. One paper put the deaths between 1997 and 2000 [@lopez2011].

López then searched newspaper archives for every sudden death of a competitive cyclist he could find. Between 1987 and 1992 there were 17, nine of them Dutch and six Belgian, which is about one a year in each country. Several had retired before EPO was available, some died in the off-season, and seven were amateurs with little access to an expensive new drug. He was left with two cases that could even plausibly involve it. In one of the best-known cases, a Dutch professional who died in his sleep in 1990, the rider's widow said the post-mortem had found nothing linking his death to the drug. Studies of sudden death in young athletes, which López also reviewed, attribute about nine in 10 to underlying heart conditions [@lopez2011].

His paper has an argument to make. He calls the story anti-doping propaganda, and he concedes that his own count is not watertight and that nothing he found proves EPO played no part. But the central finding stands on its own: a claim repeated in dozens of scholarly works had no traceable evidence behind it.

That doesn't make the drug safe. In one large trial that López cites, dialysis patients with heart disease whose haematocrit was pushed up to 42% with EPO had more heart attacks than those held at 30% [@lopez2011]. Those were sick people, and the finding can't simply be transferred to athletes, but it shows the concern about thick blood was not invented. Riders described to the commission how they got up in the night to turn the pedals on a stationary bike and keep their blood moving [@circ2015]. Whether that fear was justified by the physiology or by the rumour is hard to know now. What the episode shows is how a plausible mechanism, repeated often enough, comes to be treated as an observed fact, and that is the thing this book keeps warning about.

In 1997 the governing body brought in a rule that any rider with a haematocrit above 50% would be stood down for 15 days. It was presented as a health check, because there was then no test for EPO itself. The commission thought it probably prevented deaths [@circ2015]. It was also told what the rule did to behaviour. A rider whose natural level was 43% now had seven points of headroom, and riders described carrying centrifuges to stay inside it. One former rider said the rule "was perceived by riders as legalising EPO up to a certain limit" [@circ2015]. Its best-known application came in June 1999, when Marco Pantani, the winner of that 1998 Tour, was removed from a Giro d'Italia he was leading by more than five minutes after a reading of 52% [@pantani]. Because the rule was a health measure, that was a two-week stand-down and not a doping sanction.

In the Leiden trial, with weekly blood checks and the dose held back whenever haematocrit reached 52%, there was no difference in adverse events between the groups and no sign of clotting. Two blood markers involved in clot formation did rise in the EPO group, and the authors point out that a study of 48 people could not have detected a rare event such as a stroke [@heuberger2017].

The costs that are fully documented are of a different kind. Careers ended in bans and some in court. Seven Tours have no winner. And for every rider caught there were others who didn't dope and finished where nobody remembers, in a generation of results that can't be read.

## What the era teaches

Tests catch only a fraction of doping. A review of prevalence studies across elite sport estimated that between 14% and 39% of athletes had doped intentionally, during years in which 1% to 2% of tests came back positive [@dehon2015]. The German blood-doping ring was broken in 2019 by police acting on a skier's confession, and the cyclists who then admitted using it had not been caught by a test [@aderlass]. A clean test record, on its own, shows little.

Since January 2008 professional riders have had a biological passport, a running record of their blood values against which each new sample is compared. In its first two years the programme covered more than 850 riders and almost 20,000 samples [@zorzoli2010]. It aims to catch the effect of doping when the drug itself has gone. In 2011 researchers gave 10 volunteers small, frequent doses of EPO for up to 12 weeks. Their haemoglobin mass rose by 10%, about the equivalent of two bags of blood, and the passport software flagged none [@ashenden2011]. The 2015 commission named micro-dosing as its biggest concern. It also judged that forcing riders to use less had shrunk the gain enough for a clean rider to win again [@circ2015].

The 50% rule shows how a safety limit can become a target. Riders who treated it as permission to raise their haematocrit changed the purpose of the rule.

Race performances cannot establish guilt or innocence. Climbing times depend on the wind, the pace into the foot of the climb, the tactics and the road surface. The prevalence review concluded that performance data cannot be used to estimate how much doping there is [@dehon2015]. Even people inside the sport disagreed: one "respected cycling professional" told the commission 90% of the peloton still doped, another said 20%, and many said they didn't know [@circ2015].

The commission also wrote that doping in amateur cycling was "becoming endemic", and that masters races were "said to have middle-aged businessmen winning on EPO" [@circ2015]. It blamed cheap drugs, easy access through gyms and the internet, and the near absence of testing at that level. The Leiden trial found no measurable mountain-race gain in amateurs, although its wide uncertainty leaves a small gain possible. That is a poor basis for risking health and a ban for an age-group result.

## Getting faster without it

Several legal practices could contribute to modern racing speeds, although they cannot establish how much has changed since the EPO era.

Fuelling has a clear effect on late-ride power. In the Durability trial, critical power after three hours was 236 W on water and 266 W on 120 grams of carbohydrate an hour {shadow @norte2026}. That is about 13%, but it compares fuelling with water, not modern practice with the 1990s. Chapter 13's older food diaries show why we should not treat the whole gain as a recent discovery.

Haemoglobin can be raised legally. Three weeks at 3,000 metres raised it by 3.5% in elite cyclists, and five weeks of heat training by about the same or more {shadow @cubel2026}. Those are smaller than the 10% a course of EPO micro-doses produced in the Australian study, they fade within a fortnight, and they have to be timed. They are also available to every team.

Equipment I can't put a number on from the sources I have read for this book, and I would rather say so than quote a manufacturer. Tyres roll more easily and frames and clothing move less air than they did, and on any climb ridden at over 20 km/h some of the rider's power still goes into the air. Training has changed too, in the ways the rest of this book describes.

I find these plausible contributors to modern speeds. Adding up their separate effects would not show how much faster they have made the peloton, or prove anyone clean.

## The edge of the rules

The line between clean and doped is drawn by a list, and the list moves.

Tramadol is an opioid painkiller. For years it was legal, and the 2015 commission was told it was "used widely" in the peloton for that reason [@circ2015]. In one trial, cyclists produced 220 W in a 20-minute time trial on tramadol against 209 W on a placebo, although a second experiment in the same paper found no difference [@holgado2018]. Cycling banned it in competition from March 2019 and the World Anti-Doping Agency followed in 2024 {shadow @uci2019tramadol; @usada2024list}. A rider who took it in 2018 broke no rule. The same act six years later is a doping offence.

Carbon monoxide is a more recent case. Inhaling a small, measured amount is the standard way to measure haemoglobin mass, and it is how the heat training studies in the previous chapter got their numbers. Used once, it is a measuring tool. Used repeatedly, in theory, it could starve the body of a little oxygen and prompt the kidney to make more EPO, which would be altitude training from a cylinder. In February 2025 the sport's governing body banned possession of carbon monoxide rebreathing kits outside a medical facility, allowed a single supervised measurement with a second no sooner than two weeks later, gave rider health as its reason, and asked the World Anti-Doping Agency to take a position on it [@uci2025co]. I know of no evidence that any rider used it for anything except measurement. The rule was written because the possibility existed.

Ketone drinks are legal. In October 2025 the governing body said there was "no compelling evidence" that they help and that it did not recommend them, but it did not ban them [@uci2025ketones].

Tramadol, carbon monoxide measurement and ketones illustrate how the rules change around practices that were permitted. Looking for legal gains is what a professional team is for; describing that search is not an accusation of doping. As the 50% rule showed, riders can also interpret a limit as permission to approach it.

For anyone riding below that level the conclusion is simpler. The gains this chapter has described as certain are the legal ones: eating enough on the bike, training consistently for years, and for those with the time, altitude or heat. The one trial of the era's signature drug in riders like us found nothing on the road. Whatever is true of the professionals, there is nothing in that pharmacy for an amateur.
