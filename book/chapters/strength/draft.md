# Chapter 14: Strength training

In 1980 Robert Hickson published a study with three groups of volunteers. One lifted weights five days a week. One did endurance training six days a week. The third did both programmes in full. After ten weeks the endurance and combined groups had raised their VO₂max by the same amount, about 25% on a bike test. Strength told a different story. The combined group kept pace with the lifters for seven weeks, then stopped improving, and in the ninth and tenth weeks went backwards [@hickson1980].

The paper's title called this an interference, and the word stuck. For lifters it was a warning that endurance work costs strength. Many endurance athletes took it to mean that the two kinds of training don't mix, and stayed out of the gym with a clear conscience.

The study never showed that. Its endurance result was that lifting did no harm: VO₂max rose as much with weights as without. Eight years later Hickson tested the question the right way round. Eight runners and cyclists who had stopped improving added heavy leg training three days a week for ten weeks and changed nothing else. Their leg strength rose by 30%. Their VO₂max did not change. But they lasted 11% longer in a hard effort of four to eight minutes on the bike, and their time to exhaustion at 80% of VO₂max went from 71 minutes to 85 [@hickson1988].

Eight people, no comparison group and time-to-exhaustion tests leave the result uncertain. But it raised a useful question: could lifting make a cyclist faster without raising VO₂max?

## What the cycling trials found

Most of the modern evidence comes from Scandinavia, and the largest share of it from one group in Norway led by Bent Rønnestad.

Their first trial took 20 well-trained cyclists. Eleven added heavy strength training to their normal riding, and nine carried on riding only. The lifting was four leg exercises, three sets of four to ten repetitions with a weight that could only just be lifted that many times, twice a week for 12 weeks. The lifters' thigh muscles grew, their power at a fixed blood lactate level rose, and their average power in a 40-minute all-out test improved. The riders who only rode came close to improving in that test too, falling just short of statistical significance. So the gap between the groups was smaller than the headline suggests. VO₂max rose equally in both groups [@ronnestad2010a].

The same riders were also tested at the end of a long ride, and that result is in the Durability chapter.

A Danish study gave 14 young elite cyclists 16 weeks of endurance training with or without strength work. Short efforts of five minutes improved by 3 to 4% in both groups. Performance in a 45-minute test improved by 8%, and only in the group that lifted [@aagaard2011].

The Norwegian group also repeated its design in women. Nineteen well-trained female cyclists were randomly assigned to 11 weeks with or without heavy strength training. The 11 who lifted improved their 40-minute power, their cycling economy and the fraction of VO₂max they could sustain. The eight who did not lift did not change [@vikmoen2016].

Not every trial agrees. In 1999 an Australian group randomly assigned 21 trained female cyclists to 12 weeks of heavy squats twice a week or to a control group. The squatters' strength rose by 36%. Their power in a one-hour test did not change, nor did their lactate threshold or anything measured in their muscles [@bishop1999]. A 2010 systematic review that admitted only randomised trials in highly trained road cyclists found five, of which two showed no benefit [@yamamoto2010].

The Norwegian review offers reasons for two other trials that found nothing: one used light, explosive training instead of heavy loads, and the other lasted only six weeks [@ronnestad2014]. It has no such explanation for the Australian one, which used heavy squats for 12 weeks.

## The pooled result

A meta-analysis published in 2026 gathered every controlled study it could find of heavy strength training in cyclists, heavy meaning loads of at least 80% of the most a person can lift once. There were 17 studies with 262 participants, 60 of them women, and programmes lasting from 5 to 25 weeks at one to three sessions a week [@llanoslagos2026].

![The pooled effect of adding heavy strength training to cyclists' normal training, compared with endurance training alone, on six measures. Filled circles mark effects whose confidence interval excludes zero. Drawn from the main results of Llanos-Lagos and colleagues (2026). Threshold power is the paper's "maximal metabolic steady state"; sprint power is peak power in tests shorter than 75 seconds.](figures/strength-effects.svg)

Lifting had no effect on VO₂max, on the power at which VO₂max is reached, or on threshold power. It had a small effect on efficiency, the oxygen cost of riding at a given power, and moderate effects on sprint power and on performance in time trials and tests to exhaustion [@llanoslagos2026].

The authors rate the certainty of all of this as low. Their assessment found a moderate risk of bias across the studies, "mainly due to a lack of group randomisation", and the pooled estimates are imprecise. Nine studies measured performance, and four of those nine came from the Norwegian group. The authors also state that they registered their protocol after analysing the data [@llanoslagos2026], which is the opposite of what registration is for.

So the best summary available says that heavy lifting probably makes trained cyclists somewhat faster in efforts from a few minutes to about an hour. The studies behind that conclusion are small, mostly unrandomised and concentrated in a few laboratories. {grade B}

## How it works

Of Chapter 1's three original factors, lifting appears to change only efficiency: the pooled results show no effect on VO₂max or threshold power. It also improves the muscle's ability to produce force.

A review by Rønnestad and Iñigo Mujika sets out the mechanisms that have been proposed [@ronnestad2014]. Rønnestad led most of the Norwegian trials, so this is the field's main contributor summarising work that is largely his own. The simplest mechanism is that a stronger muscle uses a smaller share of its maximum on every pedal stroke. The slow-twitch fibres, which are the efficient ones, can then do more of the work before the less efficient fast-twitch fibres have to be called in. The review cites a study in which, among cyclists of equal VO₂max, the stronger ones showed less electrical activity in their muscles at a given power.

A second is a change within the fast-twitch fibres themselves. Both the Danish study and the Norwegian women's study took muscle biopsies and found that lifting shifted fibres from the most fatigable fast-twitch type, IIX, towards the more enduring IIA type [@aagaard2011; @vikmoen2016]. In the women, the riders whose fibres shifted most were the ones whose 40-minute power improved most.

A third is in how force is applied. Strength training raised the rate at which the elite Danish riders could develop force by 12 to 20% [@aagaard2011]. In the 25-week Norwegian study the lifters came to produce their peak torque earlier in the pedal stroke, and that change tracked their improvement in 40-minute power [@ronnestad2015].

These are proposals supported by correlations in groups of ten to twenty riders. Chapter 2's caution applies: a change seen in a biopsy alongside a change in performance has not been shown to be its cause.

### Reading the research: one group of riders, two papers

The first Norwegian trial and the long-ride result described above have the same design in every detail: 20 cyclists, 11 lifting and nine not, seven men and two women in the comparison group, four leg exercises twice a week for 12 weeks [@ronnestad2010a; @ronnestad2011]. They read as two reports from one group of riders.

One experiment can reasonably produce several papers. Count the independent groups of riders, though, and "several trials from Norway" may amount to fewer experiments than the reference list suggests.

The abstracts of those two papers also say only that the riders "were assigned" to their groups, where the women's trial says "randomly assigned" [@vikmoen2016]. Cyclists who choose to spend a winter in the gym may be the more motivated or the more ambitious, and Chapter 4 described how the same problem undid an early study of pain tolerance. The meta-analysis identified missing randomisation as the main weakness of this whole literature. What survives is the summary above: heavy lifting probably makes trained cyclists somewhat faster, on fewer independent experiments than the reference list suggests.

## Will it make me heavier?

In the first Norwegian trial the lifters' thigh muscles grew, and their body mass did not increase [@ronnestad2010a]. In Hickson's 1988 study, leg strength rose 30% with no change in thigh girth or muscle-fibre size [@hickson1988]. Lean mass in the lower body did increase in the 25-week elite study {shadow @ronnestad2015}; across the meta-analysis, body mass made no difference to the size of the benefit [@llanoslagos2026].

The Norwegian review summarises the wider literature the same way [@ronnestad2014]. Across trials of 8 to 16 weeks in cyclists, runners, triathletes and skiers, added strength training did not increase total body mass, while the muscles being trained grew by about 3 to 6%.

Hickson's original finding works in the cyclist's favour here. Heavy endurance training holds back the muscle growth that lifting would otherwise produce [@hickson1980]. The review gives a likely reason, and it is one Chapter 2 introduced. AMPK, the energy sensor that endurance exercise switches on, suppresses the pathway through which lifting builds muscle protein [@ronnestad2014]. On ten hours a week on the bike and two sessions in the gym, a rider is unlikely to gain much muscle. Strength can rise a long way without size, because much of the early gain comes from the nervous system learning to use the muscle that is already there.

## Is size the point?

Probably not, though there is a tempting argument that it is.

Lifting may enlarge W′, the reserve above critical power that an attack or a short, steep climb draws on (Chapter 1). In an eight-week trial it raised young men's W′ without changing their critical power, though not by every method of calculating it [@sawyer2014]. People with bigger thigh muscles also tend to have a bigger W′ on a bike [@miura2002]. Put the two together and you get a rule you may hear in a gym or from a coach: build bigger muscles and you'll build a bigger W′.

The second finding is observation, Chapter 4's second kind of evidence. It says that people who differ in muscle size differ in W′. It doesn't say what happens when one rider's muscles grow. In that study, thigh muscle size accounted for about a third of the differences in W′ between 17 men, which leaves two thirds to something else. And size travels with strength. In 11 elite cyclists, W′ tracked the force of the muscles that straighten the knee much more closely than the size of the thigh, and the volume of the quadriceps themselves was unrelated to it [@kordi2018]. The trials that raised W′ raised strength as well, so they can't say which did the work.

The cycling trials trained for force, with heavy weights and few repetitions, and the muscle that grew was a by-product. A rider who sets out to get bigger is chasing something those trials never aimed for, and will carry it up every climb. {grade C}

## How much, and for how long

The programmes in the 17 studies were similar enough to describe an average. They used about four exercises a session, loads of about 84% of the one-repetition maximum, which is a weight that can be lifted about seven times, and ran for about 14 weeks. The squat was the commonest exercise, used in 12 of the studies, followed by calf exercises, leg curls and the leg press [@llanoslagos2026]. All of it is heavy lifting for the legs. The review by Rønnestad and Mujika concludes that for cycling the evidence favours heavy loads over light, explosive work, and it adds a detail about how to lift them. The weight will move slowly because it is heavy, but the rider should try to push it up as fast as possible. In one study it cites, lifting with that intent produced larger gains in strength and in the rate of force development than lifting at a deliberately moderate speed [@ronnestad2014].

Its recommended programme is two sessions a week for 12 weeks, with two or three sets of four to ten repetitions and two to three minutes' rest between sets. The exercises should work the same muscles through a similar movement to pedalling. It warns that at first the legs feel heavy and sore for a few days after each session, and advises cutting back the riding for the first two or three weeks. The suggested time to begin is straight after the end of the season, when riding matters least [@ronnestad2014].

Strength gained in the winter can be kept through the season on very little. The Norwegian group had six cyclists lift twice a week for a 12-week preparation period and then once a week for the first 13 weeks of the racing season. They kept the muscle and the strength they had gained, and by the end of the 25 weeks had improved their 40-minute power more than six riders who had not lifted at all {shadow @ronnestad2010b}. Twelve riders is very few, but the finding fits the pattern from Chapter 2 that an adaptation is cheaper to keep than to build.

Nobody has compared replacing riding with lifting against adding lifting on top. The 2010 review suggested that the trials which replaced some riding did better than those which added to it [@yamamoto2010]. The Norwegian trials added it and found a benefit. The question is open, and it matters to a rider with a fixed number of hours.

> **From the saddle.** I've started lifting recently, mainly for upper-body strength. I'm getting older, and I want to keep my muscle. I use dumbbells, and one of my aims this year is to add leg exercises.


## The case that has nothing to do with speed

Cycling is weight-supported. The skeleton does not carry the rider's weight on a bike the way it does in running or walking, and bone responds to load.

A study in California compared 27 male masters cyclists, average age 51, who had raced for an average of 20 years, with normally active men of the same age and weight who were not athletes. The cyclists had lower bone mineral density at the spine and the hip. Four of them, 15%, had values in the range used to diagnose osteoporosis. None of the non-athletes did {shadow @nichols2003}.

A 2012 systematic review of 31 studies reached the same conclusion. Adult road cyclists have low bone density in key regions, the lumbar spine in particular, and road cycling "does not appear to confer any significant osteogenic benefit" [@olmedillas2012]. Mountain bikers and cyclists who did other sports as well were better off. {grade C}

These comparisons cannot show that cycling caused the difference. Nor have I found a trial showing that lifting restores bone in cyclists. Loading the skeleton is standard advice for bone health, and a squat loads the spine and hips. For a rider over 40 whose only exercise is the bike, this may be a better reason to lift than anything in a time trial.

## What to do with it

**Lift heavy, for the legs.** The trials used squats, leg presses and similar exercises at weights that could be lifted four to ten times, for about three sets. Light weights for many repetitions are not what was tested.

**Twice a week for about three months builds it. Once a week keeps it.** Start just after your season ends, and ride less for the first two or three weeks, because your legs will be sore.

**Push the weight up as fast as you can.** It will still move slowly. The intent is what the evidence supports.

**Expect a small gain where it matters and none in VO₂max.** The benefit showed up in efforts of a few minutes to an hour, and in one trial at the end of a three-hour ride.

**Train for strength, not size.** Don't expect to bulk up either: riders doing a full endurance programme gained strength with little or no gain in weight.

**Learn the lifts properly.** The Norwegian review advises developing technique with lighter loads before lifting heavy ones [@ronnestad2014]. The trials do not give injury figures I can cite. If you have not lifted before, get the movement taught.

**If you are over 40 and only ride, lift for your bones as well.** The evidence that road cyclists have low bone density is consistent, and the bike will not fix it.
