# Knowing it's working (Chapter 20): research notes

Written 15 October 2026 in the Part V batch. Tags: [FT] full text, [Abs] abstract, [Rep] second hand, [GK] general knowledge, [Own] my arithmetic or reasoning.

## Heart rate and power at submaximal effort

- **Buchheit 2014** [Abs], Front Physiol review, "do all roads lead to Rome?": contradictory findings on HR monitoring are mostly methodological; 5-minute resting recordings of beat-to-beat variability (parasympathetic indices) and submaximal exercise heart rate (30–60 s averages) are "likely the most useful monitoring tools"; changes should be judged against the error of measurement and the smallest important change, and against the training context; HR cannot inform on all aspects; combine with training logs, questionnaires and cheap performance tests.
- **Lamberts 2011** [Abs], LSCT, 17 well-trained competitive male road cyclists, four peak power tests and four 40 km time trials, each preceded by the submaximal test: three stages at fixed predetermined heart rates; power, speed, cadence, RPE measured; heart rate recovery after stage 3. Stages 2 and 3 highly reliable (ICC 0.85–1.00), typical error 1.3–4.4%. Mean power in stage 2 correlated r = 0.80–0.89 with performance, stage 3 r = 0.91–0.94; heart rate recovery r = 0.55–0.68. Conclusion: reliable; predicts peak and endurance performance from submaximal power, RPE and HRR. Stage heart rates (60%, 80%, 90% of HRmax [GK]) not in the abstract: don't state them.
- **Lamberts 2024** [Abs], 14 male cyclists, six LSCTs; recovery LnRMSSD over 60 s most reliable (ICC 0.97, typical error 5.8%); in 50 men and 20 women, LnRMSSD60s correlated with peak power (r = 0.93 men; 0.85 women), VO₂peak (0.71; 0.63), 40 km TT time (−0.83; −0.63).
- Lamberts 2004 [Abs] (Finding your zones, shadow there): 44 people, same submaximal running test on five consecutive days; at the most consistent stage (about 90% of maximum) heart rate varied by 5 ± 2 beats/min; standard error of measurement 1.1–1.4%; lower stages varied more.
- [Own] The practical version: a fixed warm-up and a fixed steady stretch on the same route or trainer, same time of day, cool conditions; note average power at a fixed heart rate (or heart rate at a fixed power). The LSCT shows the method can work; a home version with a trainer and a chest strap has not been validated [Own: say so].

## Decoupling

- **Smyth 2022** [Abs], 82,303 marathon runners (13,125 female); internal load = % of maximum heart rate, external = speed relative to estimated critical speed. Decoupling magnitude = 35–40 km segment relative to 5–10 km: low (< 1.1), moderate (1.1–1.2), high (≥ 1.2). Overall 1.16 ± 0.22, first detected at 25.2 ± 9.9 km. Low group (34.5% of runners): faster relative speed (88 ± 6% CS), marathon 217.3 ± 33.1 min, onset at 33.4 ± 9.0 km; moderate (32.7%): 86%, 224.9 min, 22.6 km; high (32.8%): 82%, 238.5 min, 19.1 km. Women's decoupling smaller (1.12 vs 1.17) and later (26.3 vs 25.0 km). Adding decoupling to a model with CS and D′ cut prediction error from 6.45% to 5.16%.
  - [Own] Observational; data from runners' own watches; % of max HR from the watch's recorded maximum. Durability chapter's drift test is the cycling version.

## How athletes feel

- **Saw 2016** [Abs], systematic review, 56 studies with concurrent subjective and objective measures. Subjective and objective measures generally did not correlate; subjective measures reflected acute and chronic training load "with superior sensitivity and consistency" than objective measures; subjective well-being impaired with an acute increase in load and with chronic training, improved with an acute decrease.

## HRV-guided training

- **Manresa-Rocamora 2021** [Abs], meta-analysis to October 2020: HRV-guided training better for vagal HRV indices (SMD 0.50, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.91), not resting HR (0.04, −0.34 to 0.43). Small non-significant SMDs favouring HRV-guided training for VO₂max (0.20, −0.07 to 0.47), capacity at VT2 (0.26, −0.05 to 0.57) and endurance performance (0.20, −0.09 to 0.48). No heterogeneity. If superior for fitness and performance, "only by a small margin". Number of studies not in the abstract.
- **Javaloyes 2019** [Abs], close-up. Seventeen well-trained cyclists; an evaluation week, then 4 baseline weeks of standardised training to establish resting HRV; then split into HRV-guided and traditional periodisation groups for 8 weeks. Evaluation: graded test (VO₂max, peak power, VT1, VT2 and their powers) and a 40-minute simulated time trial. HRV-guided group improved peak power 5.1% (SD 4.5; P = .024), power at VT2 13.9% (8.8; P = .004), 40-min TT 7.3% (4.5; P = .005); VO₂max and power at VT1 unchanged. Traditional group "did not improve significantly". "There were no differences between groups." Magnitude-based inference: "likely beneficial" (TT) and "possibly beneficial" (peak power) for the HRV group. Authors' conclusion: HRV-based daily prescription "could result in a better performance enhancement than a traditional periodization". Aim stated as a purpose ("to examine the effect of training prescription based on HRV in road cycling performance"), no hypothesis: label "Aim". Group sizes per arm not in the abstract. Lamberts is an author.
  - [Own] Two lessons from Chapter 4 and Chapter 13 in one paper: within-group significance with no between-group difference (How many hard sessions' "difference in significance"), and magnitude-based inference (How to read a training study: Sainani 2018).

## Wearable estimates

- **Molina-Garcia 2022** [Abs], meta-analysis of 14 validation studies. Devices using resting information overestimated VO₂max: bias 2.17 mL/kg/min, limits of agreement −13.07 to 17.41. Devices using exercise information: bias −0.09, limits −9.92 to 9.74. Exercise-based estimation "optimal for measuring VO₂max at the population level, yet the estimation error at the individual level is large".
  - [Own] Limits of agreement: 95% of individual estimates lie within that band of the measured value; for the exercise-based devices, within about 10 mL/kg/min either way: for a rider at 50, anything from 40 to 60.

## Fitness–fatigue models

- **Banister 1975** [exists; citation from Hellard 2006's reference list: Banister EW, Calvert TW, Savage MV, Bach A. A system model of training for athletic performance. Aust J Sports Med 1975;7:170–176]. **Calvert 1976** [exists; OpenAlex: Calvert TW, Banister EW, Savage MV, Bach T. A systems model of the effects of training on physical performance. IEEE Trans Syst Man Cybern 1976;SMC-6:94–102].
- **Hellard 2006** [FT, read on PMC 15 October]. Model: performance = initial level + a fitness term minus a fatigue term, each the sum of past training loads decaying exponentially with its own time constant (τa fitness, τf fatigue, in days) and scaled by a magnitude factor (ka, kf). Nine elite swimmers, one season, competition performances. Fit R² = 0.79 ± 0.13. But the 95% bootstrap confidence intervals of the parameters most useful for monitoring were wide: fitness decay τa = 38 days (17 to 59), fatigue decay τf = 19 (6 to 32), time to recover performance tn = 19 (7 to 35), time to peak performance after training stops tg = 43 (25 to 61). Some parameters highly correlated, "making their interpretation worthless". Also cites Taha & Thomas 2003's criticisms: inability to predict future performance accurately; mismatch with observed time courses; poor link to physiology.
  - [GK] Training software charts "fitness", "fatigue" and "form" from this model with the same fixed time constants for everyone. Don't name products or give default numbers (not verified).

## Detraining

- **Mujika & Padilla 2000 Part I** [Abs] (< 4 weeks): in highly trained athletes a rapid decline in VO₂max and blood volume; exercise heart rate rises but not enough to offset lower stroke volume; endurance performance impaired; more carbohydrate used; lactate threshold, capillary density and oxidative enzymes fall. Changes more moderate in recently trained people.
- **Mujika & Padilla 2000 Part II** [Abs] (> 4 weeks): athletes' VO₂max declines markedly but stays above control values; "recently acquired VO₂max gains are completely lost"; lactate threshold lowered but stays above untrained values in the highly trained; capillarisation and oxidative enzymes decline in athletes and are "completely reversed in recently trained individuals". Avoidable or limited by reduced training "as long as training intensity is maintained and frequency reduced only moderately"; volume can be "markedly reduced". Cross-training may help.
- Coyle 1984 (How bodies adapt; figure there): seven endurance-trained people; VO₂max fell over 84 days but stayed above untrained controls (50.8 vs 43.3). Hickson 1981/1985 and Spiering 2021 (How many hard sessions): intensity keeps it.
- George's own experience (How bodies adapt, From the saddle): after up to two weeks ill, the first ride feels terrible and it comes back in two or three rides.

## Signal and noise (figure `noise.csv`) [Own, simulation]

Random noise from a fixed seed; the CSV holds the values. Single readings and a four-week rolling average are plotted.
