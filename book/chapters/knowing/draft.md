# Chapter 19: Knowing it's working

Start with a ride you already do every week. On the same steady stretch, after the same warm-up and in similar conditions, note your power at a fixed heart rate, or your heart rate at a fixed power. Look at the average over a month. It is a practical way to follow your training without adding another test to the week.

The reason is noise. Every measure of fitness a rider can take at home moves from day to day for reasons that have nothing to do with fitness: sleep, heat, food, caffeine, a cold coming on, the calibration of a power meter. Only the last can be blamed on the manufacturer. A real change of a few per cent over a couple of months is the size that training produces in a trained rider. It is also about the size of the noise in a single reading. The practical skill is to measure something repeatable often enough that the change shows through.

## Power at a fixed heart rate

A 2014 review by Martin Buchheit favoured two heart-rate measures for following training: variability recorded at rest for a few minutes, and heart rate during a fixed submaximal effort, averaged over 30 to 60 seconds [@buchheit2014]. A change needs to exceed measurement error before it tells you much about your fitness.

The best-tested version of the fixed effort for cyclists is the Lamberts Submaximal Cycle Test. Riders ride three short stages at fixed heart rates, and the power they produce at each is recorded, with their rated effort and how fast the heart rate falls afterwards. In 17 well-trained road cyclists who did it four times, the power in the second and third stages was highly repeatable, with a typical error of 1.3% to 4.4%. The power in the third stage tracked their performance in a 40 km time trial and a peak power test closely, with correlations of 0.91 to 0.94 [@lamberts2011]. A later study from the same group added heart-rate variability in the minute after the test, which was repeatable to within about 6% and also tracked performance [@lamberts2024].

A laboratory protocol is not the same as a stretch of road or a session on a trainer. I know of no study that has validated a rider's own version, and it will be noisier. But the principle is the test's own: hold the strain on the body fixed, and see what power comes out.

To do it at home:

1. Choose a fixed heart rate a little under the top of your Zone 2, or a fixed power that sits there.
2. Ride the same warm-up each time, and measure over the same 10 to 20 minutes of the ride: on a trainer, or on a flat road or a steady climb without junctions.
3. Keep the conditions as similar as you can. A cool room with a fan indoors; similar weather outdoors; the same time of day; not the day after a hard session.
4. Note the average power at the heart rate, or the average heart rate at the power, and write it down.

Heart rate moves from day to day even when nothing else has changed. Forty-four people who repeated the same submaximal running test on five consecutive days varied by about 5 beats a minute at the stage where they were most consistent, and more at easier stages {shadow @lamberts2004}. A single week's reading carries that wobble with it.

> **From the saddle.** My main indicator of how my training is going is the average watts I can hold while keeping my heart rate in Zone 2. My other measure is how my heart rate responds to the same interval sessions. They are close to maximal efforts for me, and when I see my maximum heart rate for a session going down, or the effort feeling easier, I ratchet the intervals up by 5 W.


## Signal and noise

![A simulated rider whose true power at a fixed heart rate rises by 5% over twelve weeks, measured once a week with a typical error of 3%. Single readings (dots) jump about, and five of the twelve after the first fall below the true starting value, including the last; the four-week average (line) climbs with only small dips. The numbers are simulated, not measured. The 3% error sits within the range found for a laboratory version of the test.](figures/noise.svg)

In this simulation, power at a fixed heart rate truly rises by 5% over twelve weeks. A typical error of 3% makes several weekly readings look like losses; even the final one is lower than the first. Reacting to each reading would lead the rider to change training that was working.

Averaging four independent readings halves their noise, from 3% to about 1.5%, making the 5% gain easier to see. That is why I suggest measuring weekly and judging monthly. {grade C: the method is mine, built on the published error of the laboratory test}

## Best efforts from ordinary riding

The same logic applies to maximal efforts. A rider who races, or rides hard in groups, already produces best efforts over a range of durations without doing a test. One validation study took riders' best 3-, 7- and 12-minute powers from their ordinary training and racing files, worked out critical power from them, and compared it with critical power measured in the laboratory [@karsten2015]. The standard error was 5.2%, and the limits of agreement ran from 34 W too low to 44 W too high. A dedicated test of three all-out efforts in one session did a little better, 4.5% and −26 W to +29 W.

That makes best efforts a useful but rough measure. They track fitness when they come from rides of the same kind. They drift when the season changes what kind of rides you do: a spring of hard group rides will produce better best efforts than a winter on the trainer, whatever is happening to your fitness.

## Drift on long rides

Chapter 9 described a simple test of durability: on a long ride at steady power, compare your heart rate in the last half hour with the half hour after you warmed up. The more it rises, the more you are fading. Repeated over a season, on cool days, the drift should shrink as durability improves.

The largest study of this kind is of marathon runners. In 82,303 of them, the researchers compared heart rate with pace in the last 5 km of the race against the early kilometres [@smyth2022]. The third of runners whose heart rate stayed most closely coupled to their pace ran faster relative to their estimated critical speed. They finished in 217 minutes on average against 225 and 239 for the moderate and high groups. They also began to decouple later, at 33 km against 23 and 19. Adding decoupling to a model of marathon performance reduced its prediction error from 6.5% to 5.2%.

It was an observational study of runners in one kind of race. It shows that decoupling goes with performance, not that training to reduce it will make anyone faster. It does show that the measure carries information, and a rider can take it from every long ride without any extra effort. {grade B that decoupling goes with performance; grade C for using it to judge training}

## How you feel

A 2016 systematic review collected 56 studies comparing athletes' questionnaires about mood, stress, fatigue and soreness with objective measures such as blood markers and heart rate [@saw2016]. The two generally did not agree. The questionnaires tracked training load "with superior sensitivity and consistency": well-being fell during heavy training and recovered when training was reduced.

Write down how you feel, how you slept and how your legs were during the hard session. Using a simple scale makes it easier to compare weeks. {grade B}

## Heart-rate variability

Heart-rate variability is the variation in time between one beat and the next. At rest it is higher when the parasympathetic nervous system, which slows the heart, has more control, and it tends to fall after hard training, poor sleep or illness. Many watches and chest straps now measure it each morning, and some training plans adjust the day's session to it: hard if the reading is normal, easy if it is low.

### One study, up close: training by the morning's heart-rate variability

In 2019 a Spanish group tested this in cyclists [@javaloyes2019].

The direct comparison found no significant difference between groups. As in Chapter 12, one group's significant improvement does not establish that it beat the other. There were only 17 riders, and the authors also used magnitude-based inference to call the time-trial effect "likely beneficial". Chapter 4 described how that method produced false positives at two to six times the rate of standard tests {shadow @sainani2018}.

The wider evidence agrees with the cautious reading. A 2021 meta-analysis of heart-rate-variability-guided training found that it improved heart-rate variability itself. Its advantages for VO₂max, threshold power and endurance performance were small and not statistically significant, with effect sizes of 0.20 to 0.26 [@manresarocamora2021]. If it is better than a sensible plan, the authors concluded, it is only by a small margin.

Morning heart-rate variability is still worth having if your watch records it. A reading far below your normal, on a morning when you also feel poor, is a reason to make the day easy. Used that way it is one more input to how you feel, which is the measure with the best record. {grade B}

## The watch's VO₂max

Many watches estimate VO₂max from heart rate, pace or power and personal details. A 2022 meta-analysis of 14 validation studies found that devices whose estimates used exercise data were accurate on average, with a bias of almost zero. But their limits of agreement ran from about 10 mL/kg/min too low to 10 too high [@molinagarcia2022]. Devices that relied on resting data overestimated by about 2 on average, with limits from 13 too low to 17 too high.

![Estimates of VO₂max by consumer wearables against laboratory measurement: the average error (dot) and the range that contains 95% of individual estimates (bar). Devices using exercise data are right on average and can be about 10 mL/kg/min out for one person. From a 2022 meta-analysis of 14 validation studies.](figures/watch-vo2max.svg)

For a rider with a measured VO₂max of 50, that is an estimate anywhere from about 40 to 60. The authors' summary was that such estimates are good enough to describe a population and not yet good enough for an individual. A change in the watch's number from one month to the next is more likely to be a change in your recent rides than in your VO₂max. {grade A that the error for individuals is large}

### Reading the research: a model is not a measurement

Training software often draws a chart of fitness, fatigue and form. The idea goes back to a model that Eric Banister and his colleagues published in 1975 {shadow @banister1975}. Each session adds to two quantities: a fitness that builds slowly and fades slowly, and a fatigue that builds quickly and fades quickly. Performance is fitness minus fatigue. The speed at which each fades is set by a time constant, a number of days.

The chart's curves depend on the model's settings. In 2006 a French group fitted the model to a season of training and competition in nine elite swimmers [@hellard2006]. It explained about 79% of the variation in their performances, yet the settings were highly uncertain.

![The four settings of the fitness–fatigue model that matter most for planning, as estimated for nine elite swimmers, with the range that contains the true value with 95% confidence. Each could be anywhere across a span of four to five weeks. From Hellard and colleagues, 2006.](figures/banister-uncertainty.svg)

The time it took fitness to fade was estimated at 38 days, but the true value could have been anywhere from 17 to 59. Fatigue's was 19 days, from 6 to 32. The time to recover performance after training, 19 days, could have been 7 or 35. The time to peak after training stopped, 43 days, could have been 25 or 61. Some of the settings were so closely tied to each other that, in the authors' words, it made "their interpretation worthless". These were elite swimmers with a full season of competition results to fit the model to. A recreational rider has far fewer.

A chart using preset values assumes those settings suit you. It can show how training load has changed, including sharp jumps or falls during a break. Its estimate of your "form" on a given day depends on the assumptions, however precise the line looks. Check what the software measured and what it assumed.

## When you stop

Fitness built over months fades over weeks, and how fast depends on how long it took to build.

A 2000 review of detraining by Iñigo Mujika and Sabino Padilla described two stages [@mujika2000a]. In the first four weeks without training, highly trained athletes quickly lose VO₂max and blood volume. Their heart rate during exercise rises but does not quite make up for a smaller stroke volume. Their muscles rely more on carbohydrate, and their lactate threshold falls. In people who had trained only recently, the losses were more moderate. Over longer breaks, athletes' VO₂max kept falling but stayed above that of people who had never trained, while "recently acquired VO₂max gains are completely lost" [@mujika2000b]. The capillaries and mitochondrial enzymes of the trained declined, and in the recently trained they went back to where they started. Chapter 2's figure shows the same pattern in seven endurance-trained people who stopped completely for 84 days.

The same review gave the remedy, which Chapter 12 reached from the training trials. The losses "can be avoided or limited" by training less, provided the intensity is kept and the frequency is reduced only moderately. Volume can be cut a great deal [@mujika2000b]. For a busy spell, I would keep one hard session a week alongside whatever easy riding there is time for. As Chapter 12 explains, this is a practical inference from reduced-training studies, which did not test that exact mix. {grade C}

Coming back is quicker than building the first time, though nobody has measured how much quicker in amateur cyclists. After a short illness, my own experience, which Chapter 2 records, is that the first ride feels terrible and the legs come back within two or three rides. After a longer break, come back on easy riding for a week or two before the first hard session, and expect the first few of your weekly readings to be down. That is what they are for. {grade B for the losses; grade C for the advice on returning}

## What to do with it

Once a week, record power at a fixed heart rate, or heart rate at a fixed power, over the same stretch of a ride in similar conditions. Judge it by the four-week average.

On every long ride, the drift between the first steady half hour and the last. Compare cool days with cool days.

Whenever they happen, the best efforts: five minutes, twenty, an hour, from rides of the same kind.

And after each hard session, a line on how it felt and how you slept.

Give the routine two or three months before judging progress. {grade C for the routine as a whole}

If they don't move, look first at Chapter 17's order of the week. Are the hard sessions being ridden hard enough and recovered from, and is the easy riding staying easy?
