# Chapter 4: How to read a training study

In 2006 Chris Beedie and colleagues at Canterbury Christ Church University told six well-trained cyclists that they were taking part in a study of caffeine [@beedie2006]. Each rider would do three 10-kilometre time trials, the researchers explained, after a capsule containing either no caffeine, a moderate dose or a high dose. Before each ride the rider was told which one he had been given.

Every capsule was a placebo. When the riders believed they had taken the high dose, they produced 3.1% more power than in their baseline rides. When they believed they had taken the moderate dose, 1.3% more. When they believed they had been given nothing, 1.4% less. All six reported feeling the effects of caffeine [@beedie2006].

That is easily retold as "belief alone is worth 3%". But the range of uncertainty around every result includes zero: for the 3.1%, it runs from a loss of 0.4% to a gain of 6.7%. The paper's wording, that the effect was "likely beneficial", comes from a statistical method that sports science later had to abandon [@beedie2006].

The order of the results fits the evidence in Chapter 3 that expectation changes performance. A placebo effect on cycling performance probably exists; six riders cannot say how large it is. {grade C}

## Three kinds of evidence

The first is *mechanism*. A study shows that a session switches on a pathway, or raises a marker, and the claim follows by reasoning: this should make you fitter because of what it does in the cell. The single-session studies of PGC-1α in Chapter 2 are mechanism evidence. So are most of the arguments for any new training idea in its first few years, because mechanism studies are quick and cheap and a training study takes months.

The second is *observation*. Researchers measure people as they are, without changing anything, and look for patterns: professional cyclists who do more easy riding fade less late in a race; athletes tolerate more pain than non-athletes. Observation can study people and timescales that experiments can't, including elite riders over whole seasons. What it can't do is separate cause from effect. Riders who are naturally durable may be the ones who can tolerate big volumes, and people who tolerate pain may be the ones who take up endurance sport.

The third is the *experiment*, in which the researchers decide who does what. The strongest form is the randomised controlled trial. Volunteers are allocated by chance to the training being tested or to a comparison, so that the two groups differ in nothing systematic except the training, and any difference at the end can be credited to it.

Randomisation matters more than it might seem. In the Australian pain study in Chapter 3 the volunteers chose whether to join the training group or the control group, and the two groups turned out to differ in fitness before anything had happened. The Oxford trial that followed allocated people by a computer-generated list, balanced for fitness, and its result can be trusted further for that reason alone.

The evidence grades used in this book follow from this division. Grade A needs several randomised trials, or a good pooled analysis of them, pointing the same way. Grade B is consistent evidence from trials that are small or short or from a single laboratory, or from one well-designed trial. Grade C is observation, mechanism, case studies or expert opinion. Training fashions typically arrive at grade C and are talked about as though they were grade A.

## Who was studied

The commonest volunteers are untrained or lightly active young men, often students, and exercise physiology knows the young male student about as well as it knows the laboratory rat. In untrained people almost any training works, and works fast: the nine men in the biopsy study in Chapter 2 raised their peak oxygen uptake by 12% in two weeks {shadow @perry2010}. A trained cyclist does not gain 12% from anything. So a comparison of two methods in untrained people mostly shows that both work, and a method that wins among beginners may do nothing for someone with five years of riding behind them.

Studies of trained athletes are rarer, smaller and shorter, because trained athletes are few and unwilling to hand over their season to a research protocol. Studies of professionals are almost always observational. Women are under-represented at every level, and several of the studies in this book had none.

Will Hopkins, a sports scientist in New Zealand who has written a great deal about how performance should be measured, put the rule in one line of a 1999 paper: outcomes can be generalised only to athletes with abilities and practices represented in the study [@hopkins1999].

## What was compared

A training group that improves has shown very little by itself, because people who are tested twice tend to do better the second time. In a pooled analysis of 101 studies of how repeatable performance tests are, Hopkins and colleagues found that people improved by 1.2% between their first and second attempt at a test with no training in between, and by 0.2% between later attempts [@hopkins2001]. That is why good studies include a familiarisation trial that doesn't count, and why a study with no comparison group can't separate training from practice.

When there is a comparison group, look at what it did. Doing nothing is a weak comparison: it shows the training beats no training. The informative comparisons are between two kinds of training, and there the detail to find is what was held equal.

Two methods can be matched for total work, time spent, or neither. In Chapter 2, intervals beat steady riding for mitochondrial enzymes when the work done by each leg was matched to within a kilojoule. That comparison leaves out the advantage of easy riding: far more of it can be done. Matching time instead gives the interval group more training stress. Read the methods to find out which comparison the researchers made.

Then there is everything around the test itself. A review from the Australian Institute of Sport lists influences on a performance test that studies often fail to control or report: how the test was explained, how much the rider was shouted at, whether they knew how far they had to go, who was watching, whether music was playing, and whether they arrived mentally tired {shadow @halperin2015}. Chapter 3 showed that several of these change performance by a few per cent, which is as large as the training effects most studies are looking for.

## What was measured

Outcomes come at three levels. The lowest is a marker inside the body, such as an enzyme, a messenger molecule or haemoglobin mass. Above that is a physiological capacity like VO₂max, the lactate threshold or efficiency, and at the top is performance: how fast or how far.

A change at one level does not guarantee a change at the next. The heat-training chapter describes trials in which haemoglobin mass rose and performance didn't measurably follow. When a study reports only a marker, it has shown that a mechanism was engaged and nothing more.

Performance itself is measured in two ways that produce very different-looking numbers. In a *time trial* the rider covers a set distance, or does a set amount of work, as fast as possible. In a *time to exhaustion* test the rider holds a fixed power for as long as possible. The first resembles a race and the second does not: nobody wins a race by lasting longest at 300 watts.

Time to exhaustion is also much noisier. Kevin Currell and Asker Jeukendrup reviewed the reliability of performance tests and found that time-to-exhaustion results vary by more than 10% from one attempt to the next in the same rider, where time-trial results vary by less than 5% [@currell2008].

And time to exhaustion exaggerates. The reason is in Chapter 1. Above critical power, the time a rider can last is the fixed reserve W′ divided by how far above critical power they are riding. Take a rider with a critical power of 250 watts and a W′ of 20 kilojoules. At 300 watts they are 50 watts over, and last 400 seconds. Raise their critical power by 4%, to 260 watts, and at the same 300 watts they are only 40 over, and last 500 seconds. A 4% gain in fitness has become a 25% gain in the test.

That arithmetic is an illustration with round numbers. It explains a result like the one in Chapter 3, where six weeks of intervals raised time to exhaustion by 148% {shadow @oleary2017}. Nobody got 148% fitter. When a study reports that a method improved endurance by half or doubled it, check which test was used before being impressed.

## How many people

Of the 22 studies in people described in Chapters 2 and 3, the median had 11 participants. Only three had more than 50. Recruiting is expensive when every participant needs weeks of supervised training and several muscle biopsies.

Imagine that one training method really is better than another, by a fixed amount. People vary, so any one trial will measure that advantage with some luck added or subtracted. With few people, the luck is large.

To see how large, I ran a simulation. Suppose the better method truly raises some measure by 3 points more than the other, and that individual results scatter around the group average with a standard deviation of 5 points. That is a modest amount of spread for a training response: in the six-week study in Chapter 2, VO₂max rose by 9% on average with a standard deviation of 8 {shadow @montero2015}. I simulated 20,000 trials with 10 people in each group, analysed each one with the standard statistical test, and did the same for trials with 50 in each group.

With 10 in each group, the trials observed everything from a small advantage for the wrong method to more than double the true effect. Nine per cent of them pointed the wrong way. Only a quarter reached statistical significance, so three times out of four a real effect would have been reported as "no significant difference".

To be significant with so few people, the observed difference has to be big, so the significant trials were the lucky ones: on average they reported an advantage of 5.7 points, almost twice the truth. Journals publish significant results far more readily than others, so the small trials a reader gets to see are drawn mostly from that lucky quarter.

With 50 in each group, 84% of trials reached significance, and those reported an average of 3.3.

In 2013 a group led by Katherine Button showed that studies in neuroscience were typically too small to detect the effects they were looking for, and set out what follows: real effects are missed, the effects that are found are overestimated, and results fail to repeat [@button2013]. Everything in their argument applies to training studies of 10 people a side.

A small study that finds nothing has shown very little, because it would probably have found nothing even if the effect were real. And a small study that finds a large effect has probably overestimated it. The surprising, dramatic result from 16 volunteers is the kind most likely to shrink when someone repeats it with 60.

## How big, and how sure

Most results in a training paper come from one calculation. The researchers take the difference they observed between the groups and divide it by its *standard error*, an estimate of how much that difference would vary by chance if the study were run again with different volunteers. The answer is a *t-statistic*: the signal measured in units of the noise. A t of 1 means the difference is no bigger than chance routinely produces. By convention a result counts once t passes about 2, and with 10 people in each group, as in the simulation, the threshold is 2.1.

The noise shrinks with the square root of the number of people, so halving it takes four times the volunteers.

The t-statistic is then converted into a *p-value*, the number most papers report, and "statistically significant" by convention means the p-value is below 0.05. The p-value answers one narrow question: if the training truly made no difference, how often would chance alone produce a difference at least as large as the one observed? Below 0.05 means less than one time in twenty.

It does not say how big the effect is, and it does not say whether the effect matters. With enough people, a difference too small for any rider to notice will be significant. With too few, a difference that would decide a race will not be.

The size of the effect in real units, such as watts, seconds or per cent, is more useful, and so is the *confidence interval* around it, the range within which the true effect plausibly lies given the data. A wide interval means the study couldn't pin the effect down.

Currell and Jeukendrup point out that first and second place in sport are typically separated by less than 1% [@currell2008]. Hopkins calculated that to detect the smallest improvement that would change an elite athlete's results, a study needs somewhere between 16 and 65 athletes if each one tries both treatments, and between 65 and 260 if separate groups are used [@hopkins1999]. Compare that with the median of 11 in the preceding chapters.

The best tests available vary by about 1 to 2% from one attempt to the next in the same person [@hopkins2001]. So an improvement of 1%, which would be worth having, is smaller than the noise in a single measurement, and small studies cannot see it. They can detect large effects in untrained people. They are close to blind to the small effects that matter to trained ones.

The trial of EPO on Mont Ventoux, described in the chapter on drugs, shows what reporting looks like when it is done well: 48 riders, randomly allocated, neither riders nor testers knowing who had the drug, and each result given as a difference with its confidence interval. The reader can see which effects were pinned down and which were not {shadow @heuberger2017}.

## Choices made after the data are in

A researcher analysing a training study has many decisions to make: which of the dozen outcomes measured is the main one, whether to leave out the rider who fell ill, whether to compare two intervention groups separately or pool them, whether to adjust for starting fitness.

Each decision is defensible. The trouble comes when they are made after seeing the data, because it is then very easy, without any dishonesty, to settle on the set of choices that yields the more interesting result. In 2011 three psychologists showed by simulation and experiment that this flexibility pushes the rate of false positives far above the one in twenty that the p-value promises [@simmons2011]. The practice is now called *p-hacking*.

There are signs of it in sports science. In 2020 a group in Dublin looked at 129 studies published over five months in four leading sports medicine journals and counted how many reported that their main hypothesis had been supported. The answer was 82% [@buttner2020]. Researchers are not right that often. The likelier explanation is that hypotheses which fail go unpublished, or are quietly replaced by ones the data happen to support.

For about a decade many sports science papers used a home-grown statistical method called magnitude-based inference, which described results in phrases such as "likely beneficial" and "possibly harmful" and was promoted as better suited to small studies than conventional tests. In 2018 Kristin Sainani, a statistician at Stanford, re-ran the method's own published simulations and showed that at the sample sizes its developers recommended it produced false positives at two to six times the rate of standard testing [@sainani2018]. Her paper ends: "Magnitude-based inference should not be used." A reader who meets "likely beneficial" in a paper from those years should read it as "not statistically significant by ordinary standards".

The defence against all of this is *preregistration*: the researchers publish their hypothesis, their main outcome and their analysis plan before collecting data, so that they cannot adjust them afterwards. It is required for clinical trials in medicine and still unusual in training research. When a study is preregistered, the paper says so, and it counts in its favour.

Without it, look for the signs. Be wary when the result appears only in a subgroup, or only after groups were pooled, or only on one of many outcomes, and when the headline measure is not the one the methods section seemed to be building towards.

## When studies are repeated

Repeating published studies tests how well their findings hold up.

Psychology did this first on a large scale. In 2015 a collaboration of psychologists repeated 100 studies from three of the field's journals, using the original materials where they could and designs with more statistical power [@osc2015]. Of the original studies, 97% had reported a significant result. Of the repeats, 36% did, and the effects they measured were half the size. The reading of those figures has been disputed, and one group of critics argued that the project's statistics were flawed and the true rate of replication was high {shadow @gilbert2016}. The problem it pointed to is now known as the *replication crisis*, and other fields have since checked their own work.

Sports science did so in 2025. A project led from Technological University Dublin recruited volunteer laboratories to repeat 25 studies published between 2016 and 2021 in the field's highest-ranked journals, each drawn at random from the studies a laboratory had the equipment and expertise to repeat [@murphy2025]. Fourteen of the 25 repeats found a significant effect in the same direction as the original. Seven of them, 28% of the total, also found an effect of a size compatible with the original, which was the project's test of a successful replication. In 22 of the 25 the effect was smaller the second time, and the median shrinkage was 75%.

Twenty-five studies cannot stand for a field. Many of the repeats simply doubled the original sample, which the authors say left them with less statistical power than planned, so some real effects will have been missed. And the project covered biomechanics, psychology, physiology, nutrition and injury prevention, in experiments a volunteer laboratory could fit in. A training study that runs for months is far harder to repeat, and I know of no project that has repeated a set of them.

The shrinkage is the simulation's result appearing in real data. The simulated small trials that reached significance reported almost twice the true effect, because they were the lucky ones, and a repeat is a second draw that has not been selected for luck. When a single small study reports a striking effect, the reasonable working assumption is that the true effect is a good deal smaller than the published one. {grade B}

## Individual results

Many papers plot each participant's change and discuss who responded and who didn't. Measurement noise can produce those apparent differences.

Greg Atkinson and Alan Batterham made the problem clear with a simulation [@atkinson2015]. They created an imaginary trial in which every person's true response to a treatment was exactly the same, added ordinary day-to-day measurement variation, and plotted the results. The plot showed what looked like strong responders, weak responders and non-responders. All of the difference between individuals was noise.

Their rule is that individual differences in response can be claimed only by comparison with a control group. If the changes in the training group are no more spread out than the changes in people who did nothing, there is no evidence that anyone responded differently from anyone else. Very few studies, they note, make that comparison.

This is the statistical reason behind the caution in Chapter 2 about non-responders, and it applies to your own training too. One good or bad test result is one draw from your own day-to-day variation.

### One study, up close: a trial that gets most of it right

The two-year Dallas trial in Chapter 2 shows how to apply these questions to a study [@howden2018].

*What kind of evidence?* A randomised experiment. A statistician allocated the volunteers in blocks, balanced for sex.

*Who was studied?* Sixty-one healthy, sedentary adults aged 45 to 64, chosen from 262 who were screened. People with high blood pressure, a body mass index of 30 or more, or a habit of regular exercise were excluded. The authors say that volunteers willing and able to take on an intensive programme may not represent everyone, and that most of them were white.

*What was compared?* Training against a control group that was also given something to do: yoga, balance and stretching three times a week for the same two years. Both groups wore heart-rate monitors, and each person in either group had an exercise physiologist following their progress. A control group that is simply sent home knows it is the control group. This one got the same attention as the trainers, which removes one explanation for any difference.

*What was measured?* The main outcome was named before the trial began: the stiffness of the left ventricle, worked out from a catheter in the heart while the amount of blood returning to it was lowered and then raised. That is a capacity and not a performance, but it is measured about as directly as is possible in a living person, and the cardiologist who measured the heart's volume did not know the pressures. VO₂max was measured by collecting the air each person breathed out.

*How many, for how long?* The researchers calculated in advance that they needed 21 people in each group. They recruited 34 and 27, and 28 and 24 finished the two years. The training group completed 88% of the sessions it was set, each one recorded by a heart-rate monitor [@howden2018].

*How big, and how sure?* VO₂max rose by 5.3 mL/kg/min in the training group, an 18% gain, with a confidence interval from 4.15 to 6.40. In the controls it changed by −0.3, with an interval from −1.3 to 0.7. The first interval is nowhere near zero and the second sits across it. The result for stiffness, the main outcome, is less emphatic: the difference between the groups had a p-value of 0.04. The gain in fitness is beyond reasonable doubt, and the change in the heart cleared the conventional bar narrowly [@howden2018].

*Could expectations have produced it?* Everyone knew which group they were in, because nobody can be blinded to two years of intervals. That matters little for a pressure reading from a catheter. It matters more for VO₂max, which depends on a person being willing to go to their limit.

*Choices after the data were in?* The trial was registered, with one main outcome, and overseen by an independent monitoring board. A comparison of men and women is labelled as unplanned, and the authors say it "should be interpreted with caution". The analysis covers the 52 who finished and not all 61 who were randomised, which can flatter a treatment if the people who drop out are the ones it didn't suit. Six left the training group and three the control group. The paper also states that the data will not be shared with other researchers.

*Has anyone repeated it?* Not at this length. The authors describe it as the longest randomised trial of structured training in healthy middle-aged adults.

The programme mixed easy sessions, sessions near the threshold, four-minute intervals and strength training. The trial shows that this combination changed the heart, but cannot tell us how much each kind of training contributed.

One comparison in 61 people took two years, a staff of exercise physiologists and catheters in 52 hearts, twice. There are few training trials like it for that reason, and most of the evidence in this book is weaker.

## The questions

1. What kind of evidence is it: mechanism, observation or experiment?
2. If an experiment, were people allocated at random?
3. Who was studied, and are they like you?
4. What did the comparison group do, and what was held equal?
5. Was the outcome a marker, a capacity or a performance, and if a performance, a time trial or a time to exhaustion?
6. How many people, for how long?
7. How big was the effect in real units, and how wide was the range of uncertainty?
8. Could the participants' expectations have produced the result?
9. Was the analysis fixed in advance, or does the result depend on a subgroup, a pooling or one outcome out of many?
10. Has anyone repeated it?

The last question is the one that most often settles the matter. One small study is one draw. When several independent groups, using different volunteers and different protocols, find the same thing, the luck averages out, and that is what a grade A means in this book.

Most of the studies in this book are small, short and done on people fitter or less fit than you. That still leaves useful evidence. Hold the well-supported claims firmly, treat the rest as reasonable bets, and know which is which.
