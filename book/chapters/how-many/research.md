# How many hard sessions, and how often (Chapter 13): research notes

Written 15 October 2026 with the first draft, the third of three Part III chapters done in one batch at George's request. Tags as elsewhere: [FT], [Abs], [Rep], [GK], [Own].

**Read in full:** Tønnessen, Hisdal & Rønnestad 2020 (methods, results, limitations); Hatle 2014 (abstract and training protocol); Lenk 2025 (results table); Yang 2025 (the frequency sections). Everything else abstract only. George's website article (`website/research/how-many-hard-sessions.html`) was read as a map. **Two of its statements need correcting** (see the end): Tønnessen 2020's sessions were threshold intervals, not VO₂max intervals, and its time-trial difference between groups was trivial.

## 1. Chapter 8's question
- Tønnessen 2014 [FT, Chapter 8]: eleven champion skiers kept about 1.3 h a week above the first lactate threshold in every phase, while easy hours changed from 15.2 to 9.8. Chapter 8 left open whether that steady dose is a ceiling, and where it lies for a rider on six to ten hours.
- Seiler & Kjerland 2006 and Seiler 2010 [Abs, Chapter 8]: about four easy sessions for every hard one in elite athletes.

## 2. Adding hard sessions to easy training
- **Lindsay et al. 1996** [Abs]: 8 competitive cyclists, ~300 km a week, replaced 15% with **6 sessions over 4 weeks** (1.5 a week) of 6 to 8 × 5 min at 80% of peak power: 40-km time trial 56.4 → 54.4 min.
- **Stepto et al. 1999** [Abs]: 20 cyclists, **6 sessions over 3 weeks** (2 a week) added to base training: best sessions improved the 40-km time trial 2.4% to 2.8%.
- **Driller et al. 2009** [Abs]: 10 rowers, **7 sessions over 4 weeks**: HIT beat continuous training (2,000 m time 1.9%).
- [Own] 1.5 to 2 hard sessions a week, added to a base, improved trained athletes by about 2% to 4% in 3 to 4 weeks.

## 3. Trials of frequency

### Tønnessen, Hisdal & Rønnestad 2020, Int J Environ Res Public Health [FT] — the close-up
- Hypothesis (stated): "two longer sessions would elicit greater performance improvements and physiological adaptation than four shorter sessions at the same intensity".
- 20 national-level cross-country skiers and biathletes, randomised; **15 completed**: low-frequency (LF) 9 of 10 (one ill); high-frequency (HF) 6 of 10 (three ill, one accident). Median VO₂max 70.7 (LF) and 67.8 (HF); 650 and 565 h of training in the previous year (not significantly different).
- 12 weeks, May to August, dry land (roller-skis).
- **All intervals in Norwegian I-zone 3: 82% to 87% of HRmax, lactate 2.5 to 4.0 mmol/L** (threshold intervals), 136 min a week in both groups.
- LF: one session of 8 × 8 min (2 min recovery) and one of 6 × 12 min (3 min recovery) a week; at least one day, usually two, between sessions.
- HF: two sessions of 4 × 8 min and two of 3 × 12 min; two of them on consecutive days.
- Other training: low intensity, and at most one extra hard session a week; training load did not differ between groups.
- Compliance: everyone >80% of sessions; skipped sessions mostly at the athletes' request "due to a feeling of tiredness".
- **Results:** 8-km roller-ski time trial: LF median 31.8 → 30.6 min (p = 0.04; 7 of 9 improved); HF no significant change (p = 0.16). **Effect size of LF against HF: 0.17, "only a trivial effect".** VO₂max: no change in either group. % of VO₂max at 4 mmol/L: LF 80.7 → 85.4% (p = 0.04), HF no change; ES 0.52 (small). Oxygen cost at a fixed speed: LF 41.0 → 39.6 mL/kg/min (p = 0.01); ES 1.14. Submaximal heart rate LF 144 → 134 bpm; ES 0.95.
- Authors' conclusion: "Two longer interval sessions appear superior to four shorter sessions per week"; "longer time between training sessions ... may also have allowed athletes to recover more effectively".
- Authors' practical note: threshold sessions "appear to elicit the best effect when the total effective duration is ≥60 min". Limitation they name: 20 participants.
- [Own] The comparison the hypothesis asks about (LF against HF) is the effect size of 0.17 for the time trial; the "superior" reading rests on one group's within-group change reaching significance while the other's did not. The HF group also lost four of ten athletes.

### Billat et al. 1999 [Abs]
- 8 runners (VO₂max ~72); 4 weeks with **one** session a week at the speed at VO₂max (5 repetitions at 50% of the time to exhaustion), then 4 weeks with **three**.
- One a week: speed at VO₂max 20.5 → 21.1 km/h (p = 0.02), through better economy (50.6 → 47.5 mL/kg/min at a submaximal speed); VO₂max unchanged (71.6 vs 72.7).
- Three a week: no change in performance or its determinants; noradrenaline at the end of the test **11.1 → 26.0 nmol/L** (p = 0.002); resting noradrenaline unchanged.

### Lenk et al. 2025, Physiol Rep [FT, results table]
- 26 recreationally active people (14 women; age 23; VO₂max 51.3) completed, of 44 recruited (others left for time or illness); 6 weeks of 4 × 4 min running, once, twice or three times a week; adherence 100%, 96%, 91%.
- VO₂max: **1×: 53.0 → 53.6** (d = 0.09); **2×: 53.5 → 57.6** (d = 0.48); **3×: 48.5 → 51.2** (d = 0.61). Group × time interaction **p = 0.167** (not significant). Time to exhaustion: 598 → 601 s; 601 → 667 s; 575 → 626 s (interaction p = 0.080).
- Authors: exploratory, no power calculation, "limited precision"; "no clear additional benefit from increasing the frequency to three sessions per week".

### Hatle et al. 2014, PLoS One [Abs; protocol FT]
- 21 healthy young adults (10 women, age 23), 24 sessions of uphill treadmill running, each 10 min warm-up, 4 × 4 min at 90% to 95% of HRmax with 3 min at 70%, 5 min cool-down, 40 min in all, supervised by a personal trainer [FT] either over **8 weeks (3 a week)** or **3 weeks (8 a week)**, then 9 weeks without training.
- Four days after training: 8-week group VO₂max **+10.7%** (p < 0.001); 3-week group **+3.0%** (p = 0.126); group difference p = 0.035. Twelve days after: 3-week group **+6.1%** (p = 0.026), 8-week group down to **+7.9%** (group difference p = 0.609).
- Authors: "The cardiovascular adaptation following a HF programme of high-intensity exercise is however delayed".

### Yang, Wang & Guan 2025 [FT, frequency sections]
- 51 studies, 1,261 athletes. HIIT against control: **3 sessions a week g = 1.21 (p < 0.01); 2 a week g = 0.69 (p = 0.07); more than 3 g = 1.02 (p = 0.15)**. In HIIT-versus-continuous designs, a third session added g = 0.48 (p = 0.10). Many studies were of team-sport and running athletes.
- [Own] The two-a-week and three-a-week effects point the same way, with overlapping uncertainty. One subgroup crossing p < 0.05 and the other not is not evidence that three beats two.

### Helgerud 2007 [Abs]: 3 sessions a week of intervals, nothing else, 8 weeks: VO₂max +7.2% (4 × 4).

## 4. Arranging the same work
- **Rønnestad, Hansen & Ellefsen 2014** [Abs]: trained cyclists, 4 weeks. Block (n = 10, VO₂max 62): **5 high-intensity sessions in week 1, then 1 a week for 3 weeks**. Traditional (n = 9, VO₂max 63): **2 a week**. Same volumes. Block: VO₂max **+4.6%**, peak power +2.1%, power at 2 mmol/L **+10%**; traditional: no changes. Effect sizes 1.34, 0.85, 0.71 in favour of the block.
- **Sylta et al. 2016** [Abs]: order of interval types over 12 weeks, 24 sessions (2 a week): no difference; all improved 5% to 10% (Chapter 12).
- Hatle 2014 above: compressing 24 sessions into 3 weeks delayed the gain.

## 5. Recovery
- **Stanley, Peake & Buchheit 2013** [Abs]: complete cardiac autonomic recovery after one aerobic session: **up to 24 h after low intensity, 24 to 48 h after threshold intensity, at least 48 h after high intensity**; faster in fitter people; duration "unlikely to be the greatest determinant".
- **Seiler, Haugen & Kuffel 2007** [Abs, Chapters 6 to 8]: short-term heart-rate variability recovery after the session: 5 to 10 min after easy sessions; about 30 min after threshold or interval sessions; 90 min or more after intervals in eight trained subjects. [Own] A different timescale from Stanley's (minutes to hours, not days).
- **Meeusen et al. 2013** [Abs]: functional overreaching (a short-term drop followed by improvement after recovery); non-functional overreaching; overtraining syndrome ("prolonged maladaptation"); no marker meets all criteria for diagnosis.
- [Own] With at least 48 h after each hard session, a seven-day week holds three at most, with no room for a long ride on fresh legs; two leave 48 to 72 h between them.

## 6. The Norwegian threshold model (Chapter 8)
- Casado 2023 [Abs, Chapter 8]: three to four lactate-guided threshold interval sessions a week (2 to 4.5 mmol/L, measured every one to three repetitions) and one at VO₂max intensity, within 150 to 180 km a week.
- Casado 2022 [Abs]: elite distance runners do continuous tempo or threshold intervals and faster intervals each at least once a week, on a hard day/easy day basis.
- [Own] Threshold sessions recover in 24 to 48 h (Stanley), so a week can hold more of them than of VO₂max sessions; Tønnessen 2020 also suggests that fewer, longer threshold sessions do at least as well as more, shorter ones.

## 7. Maintenance
- **Hickson & Rosenkoetter 1981** [Abs]: 12 young adults, 10 weeks cycling and running 40 min a day, 6 days a week (VO₂max +25% cycling, +20% treadmill); then 4 or 2 days a week at the same intensity and duration for 15 weeks: **VO₂max maintained** in both.
- **Hickson et al. 1985** [Abs]: same design but cutting intensity by a third or two thirds with frequency and duration unchanged: VO₂max **not maintained**; long-term endurance down 21% (184 → 145 min) and 30% (202 → 141 min); left-ventricular mass back to control levels.
- **Spiering et al. 2021** [Abs]: endurance maintained up to 15 weeks with as few as 2 sessions a week or 33% to 66% less volume (13 to 26 min a session) if intensity is kept; "exercise intensity seems to be the key variable".
- **Borges et al. 2016** [Abs]: in masters athletes, the effect of age on recovery "may be smaller than originally suggested"; sedentary lifestyles "appear to play a larger role"; "there may be an age effect on recovery kinetics", more research needed.

## 8. The dose compared with the champions [Own]
- Work per week in the trials that worked: Seiler 2013 4 × 8 twice = 64 min; Rønnestad 2015 30/15 twice ≈ 2 × 19.5 min of 30-s efforts (3 × 13 × 30 s) = 39 min; Helgerud 2007 4 × 4 three times = 48 min; Lindsay 1996 about 1.5 sessions of 30 to 40 min = 45 to 60 min; Tønnessen 2020 threshold 136 min.
- The champions' 1.3 h a week above the first threshold (Tønnessen 2014) is time in zones 3 to 5 as the athletes recorded it, probably including some recovery minutes between repetitions (Chapter 8's weak points). It is the same order as the 40 to 65 min of hard work in the cycling trials, and below the 136 min of threshold work in Tønnessen 2020.
- So the hard dose that works in short trials of amateurs and trained cyclists is close to what the champions kept for whole seasons. Nothing tests more over months. **My inference; grade C.**

## Corrections to the website article (for George)
- It describes Tønnessen 2020 as "matched total interval time" with "6 × 12 minutes"; correct, but it does not say the sessions were threshold intervals (82% to 87% of HRmax), and it reports "Only the two-session group improved its time-trial performance" without the between-group effect size of 0.17. The book says both.
- It says Yang 2025 found "a moderate but non-significant effect at two (p = 0.07)", which is accurate; the book adds that this is not evidence that three beats two.

## Added while expanding the draft (15 October)
- **Tønnessen 2014** [FT, Chapter 8]: hard sessions 2 a week in general preparation, 3 in the peaking phase.
- **Fiskerstrand & Seiler 2004** [Abs, Chapter 8]: Norwegian international rowers 1970 to 2001: volume +20%; race-pace and supra-maximal training from 23 to about 7 h a month; performance almost 10% better.
- **Stanley 2013** [Abs]: metaboreflex (muscle and blood acidosis) drives parasympathetic reactivation in the first 0 to 90 min; baroreflex (changes in plasma volume) over 1 to 48 h; cardiac parasympathetic recovery "does not appear to coincide with the recovery of all physiological systems (e.g. energy stores or the neuromuscular system)"; limited data.
- **Halson & Jeukendrup 2004** [Abs]: overreaching recovers in about 2 weeks; overtraining syndrome months to years; "no evidence aside from anecdotal information to suggest that overreaching precedes overtraining".
- **Breil et al. 2010** [Abs]: 21 elite junior alpine skiers (6 women); interval group (n = 13) did 15 sessions of 4 × 4 min at 90% to 95% HRmax with 3 min recovery in 11 days; controls continued mixed training. Seven days after: VO₂max **+6.0%** (men +7.5%, women +2.1%); peak power +5.5%; power at VT2 **+9.6%**; time to exhaustion unchanged; jump peak power down (CMJ −4.8%, SJ −4.1%); authors: "might be associated with persisting muscle fatigue". [Rep, Hatle 2014 introduction] Swiss.
- **Hatle 2014** introduction [FT]: "two earlier studies have reported a decrease in VO2max and work performance after two weeks of highly intensified training in competitive cyclists" [Rep]; those studies not looked up.

## Weak points
- Every frequency trial is small (8 to 26 people) and short (4 to 12 weeks); none in time-crunched amateur cyclists over a season.
- Tønnessen 2020 tested threshold sessions in elite skiers; Lenk 2025 and Hatle 2014 are in young recreational people and running; Billat in runners.
