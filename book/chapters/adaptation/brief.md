## The question

How does a ride, repeated often enough, become a bigger heart, more blood, more capillaries and more mitochondria, and how fast does each of those come and go?

## The argument (to approve)

1. The body keeps only the capacity it is asked for. Adaptation runs in both directions: three weeks in bed cost five young men more than thirty years of ageing did (the Dallas study).
2. A session disturbs the muscle's chemistry (energy state, calcium, mechanical stress). Sensors read the disturbance and switch on genes; the co-activator PGC-1α links them to mitochondrial genes. Each session produces a burst of messenger RNA that is gone within a day; protein accumulates across sessions. Training works by repetition.
3. The muscle adapts fast (enzymes within three sessions, capillaries within weeks). Blood adapts in two stages: plasma in days to weeks, red cells over weeks. The heart takes months to years.
4. In people starting from untrained, the early rise in VO₂max comes mostly from blood, not muscle: remove the red cells gained and the VO₂max gain disappears (Montero 2015). The muscle changes matter for the fraction of VO₂max that can be sustained, which is Chapter 1's division between delivery and use.
5. A molecular signal is not an adaptation. Single-session signalling studies are the raw material of most training fashions, for hard intervals and for Zone 2 alike, and they cannot settle which training works better. This is the chapter's Reading the research skill.
6. Adaptations are lost at different speeds: stroke volume and half the gain in mitochondrial enzymes within weeks, capillaries hardly at all in 12 weeks.
7. People respond very differently to the same programme, about half of that difference runs in families, and at low doses much "non-response" is too little training.
