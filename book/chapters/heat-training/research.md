# Heat training: research notes

5 October 2026. Tags as elsewhere: **[FT]** full text read, **[Abs]** abstract, **[Rep]** news report. Summaries in our own words.

## Origin and the short studies

- **Lorenzo et al. 2010** (J Appl Physiol 109:1140–1147; Oregon, Minson's lab) [Abs]: 12 trained cyclists, 10 days of exercise at about 50% of VO₂max in 40 °C; 8 matched controls did the same in 13 °C. In the cool (13 °C): VO₂max **+5%** (66.8 → 70.2 mL/kg/min), time-trial work **+6%**, lactate-threshold power **+5%**. In the heat (38 °C): VO₂max +8%, time trial +8%. Plasma volume **+6.5%**; maximal cardiac output up. Controls unchanged. `@lorenzo2010`
- **CrossTalk debate 2016** (J Physiol 594): Minson & Cotter for [Abs: benefit "unresolved and may be modest or absent in some settings and individuals"; longer is better; not yet studied in truly elite athletes]; Nybo & Lundby against [no abstract; title only]. `@minsoncotter2016`, `@nybolundby2016`
- **Karlsen et al. 2015** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 25 Suppl 1:269–276) [Abs]: 18 trained male cyclists, 2 weeks training in about 35 °C (n = 9) or cool (n = 9). Time trial in the heat **+16%**. In the cool (5 to 13 °C, 43.4 km outdoors): VO₂max 4.79 → 4.82 L/min, time-trial power **300 → 302 W**. No transfer. `@karlsen2015`
- **Keiser et al. 2015** (Am J Physiol Heart 309:H750–761) [Abs]: 7 men, crossover, 10 days of 90 min in 38 °C or 18 °C. In 38 °C: VO₂max **+9.6%**, 30-min time trial **+10.4%**. In 18 °C: both unchanged. Plasma volume **+6%**, sweat output +26%. `@keiser2015`

## Five-week trials

- **Mikkelsen et al. 2019** and **Oberholzer et al. 2019** (Front Physiol 10:1372 and 10:1379; Copenhagen; two papers, one trial) [Mikkelsen FT; Oberholzer Abs]: randomised; 24 sub-elite male cyclists recruited, 21 finished (12 heat, 9 control); age 38, VO₂max about 58 mL/kg/min, at least 3 years' cycling. Heat group replaced part of normal training with **1 h at 60% of VO₂max in 40 °C, 5 days a week, 28 ± 2 sessions over 5½ weeks**; total training similar (**509 against 576 min/week**). Rectal temperature at the end of sessions above 39 °C throughout (**39.9 °C first session, 39.4 °C last**). Acclimation confirmed (lower sweat sodium, lower heart rate, longer endurance in heat). In the cool (14 °C): VO₂max **60.0 → 59.8**; 15 km time-trial power **298 → 315 W heat, 294 → 311 W control**. Plasma volume up in both (+303 mL heat, +188 mL control). Haemoglobin mass **+34 ± 36 g heat, +2 ± 33 g control**; group difference p = 0.061 (a trend). Blood-volume hormones and reticulocytes unchanged. Authors [FT]: a bigger blood volume may be cancelled by less oxygen per litre of diluted blood. `@mikkelsen2019`, `@oberholzer2019`
- **Rønnestad et al. 2021** (Exp Physiol 106:316–327) [Abs]: 23 elite male cyclists (VO₂max 76), "assigned" to heat (11) or control (12). **1 h light exercise at 37.8 °C, 65% humidity, 5 a week for 5 weeks.** Haemoglobin mass **893 → 935 g (+42 g)** against +6 g; interaction significant. VO₂max gain not greater. Lactate-threshold power +2.8% against −0.4%, 15-min power +6.9% against +3.4%: "small to intermediate effect sizes", not statistically significant. Our arithmetic: 42/893 = 4.7%. `@ronnestad2021`
- **Rønnestad et al. 2022a** (MSSE 54:1515–1526) [Abs]: elite cyclists; **5 × 50 min a week for 5 weeks**, in a heat chamber or wearing a heat suit in normal temperatures. Haemoglobin mass **+2.6% (suit), +2.4% (chamber), −0.7% control**. Power at 4 mmol/L lactate and 1-min power improved more than control in the suit group only; chamber not significant. Pooling the two heat groups, a composite performance index rose **4.9% against 1.7%** (p < 0.05). Then 3 weeks at 3 sessions a week kept haemoglobin mass up (**+3.3%** against **+0.8%** for those who stopped). Group sizes not in the abstract. `@ronnestad2022a`
- **Rønnestad et al. 2022b** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 32:1089–1098; open access) [Abs]: 25 elite male cross-country skiers; heat suit 5 × 50 min a week, 5 weeks (13) or matched control (12). Haemoglobin mass **30 g higher** than control (95% CI 8.5 to 51.7). **No group differences** in VO₂max, economy, lactate-threshold speed or a 15-min performance test. `@ronnestad2022b`
- **Lundby et al. 2023** (J Appl Physiol 135:217–226) [Abs]: 20 elite women and 27 elite men, randomised to heat (5 × 50 min, 5 weeks) or control. Haemoglobin mass **+4.0% women, +3.5% men**. Muscle (citrate synthase, fibre type, capillaries) unchanged. Lactate threshold, VO₂max and 15-min power "enhanced" after heat; the abstract gives no figures for these. `@lundby2023`
- **Cubel et al. 2024** (Temperature 11:350–362; open access) [FT in part]: 20 elite male cyclists "divided" into heat (10) and control (10) (randomisation not stated). **6 × 1 h a week for 5 weeks.** Time to exhaustion at 40 °C **+15 min**; sweat rate **+0.44 L/h**; sweat sodium down. Haemoglobin mass **+30 g (3%) at 3 weeks, +40 g (4%) at 5 weeks**; 7 of 10 riders had most of the gain by week 3. **Back to baseline 2 weeks after stopping**, as was heat performance. Peak power in an incremental test **+12 W**; VO₂max not significant (p = 0.094). `@cubel2024`
- **Cubel et al. 2026** (Exp Physiol 111:820–833; open access) [Abs]: 12 elite male cyclists, altitude camp (21 days at 3,000 m): haemoglobin mass **+3.5%**, gone 10 days after return, no performance gain. Seven of them also did 5 weeks of heat in the off-season: **+5.4%**, not different from altitude. `@cubel2026`
- **Maunder et al. 2021** (Physiol Rep 9:e14849; open access) [Abs]: 17 trained men randomised to 3 weeks of training in 18 °C or 33 °C, matched by heart rate (so lower power in the heat). 30-min time trial after 2 h of easy riding, in 18 °C, improved **30 ± 13 W (heat, n = 7) against 16 ± 5 W (n = 6)**, p = 0.04. Citrate synthase rose in the heat group only. Three weeks, so too short for the haemoglobin explanation. `@maunder2021heat`
- **Waldron et al. 2021** (Sports Med 51:1509–1525; open access) [Abs]: meta-analysis, 28 studies. VO₂max in normal temperatures, heat acclimation against control: Hedges' g = **0.30** (95% CI 0.06 to 0.54), a small effect. A published comment disputes the analysis (Borg & O'Brien 2022, not read). `@waldron2021`

**Pattern:** every five-week trial found haemoglobin mass up 2.4 to 5.4% (30 to 42 g). Performance: better than control in Lundby 2023 and the pooled Rønnestad 2022a; not better in Mikkelsen 2019, Rønnestad 2021 or the skiers. Carsten Lundby is an author on all of the five-week trials above: one network of labs (Copenhagen, Lillehammer). Nearly all participants elite; the only amateurs are the Copenhagen 21.

## Mechanism

- Plasma volume expands in the first week (about 6 to 7%: Lorenzo 6.5%, Keiser 6%, Mikkelsen about 7% [FT]).
- "Critmeter" theory (Cubel 2024 [FT, Introduction]): diluted blood may then stimulate red-cell production. Oberholzer [Abs] calls the link unproven: the hormones they measured did not change; the rise in haemoglobin mass correlated only weakly with the rise in plasma volume (r = 0.49).
- Lundby 2023: no change in muscle. Maunder 2021: citrate synthase up. Conflict noted.

## The CORE sensor

- Made by greenTEG, Rümlang, Switzerland. Worn on the chest; estimates core temperature from skin temperature and heat flux, optionally heart rate (Verdel 2021 [FT]). CORE 2 is a smaller successor [Rep]; **no independent validation of CORE 2 found in this search.**
- **Verdel et al. 2021** (Sensors 21:5932; open access) [FT]: against a rectal probe during cycling. Study I: 12 men, two identical 60-min rides at 19 °C. Repeatability good: mean difference between the two rides **0.02 °C**. Against rectal: CORE read **0.23 °C higher** on average; only **51%** of readings within 0.3 °C; limits of agreement **−0.38 to +0.72 °C**. Study II: 13 men, 90 min at 31 °C: CORE **0.10 °C lower** on average; **45%** within 0.3 °C; limits **−0.62 to +0.59 °C**. Authors: results do not support the maker's claim of a valid measure. `@verdel2021`
- **Goods et al. 2023** (Eur J Sport Sci 23:1509–1517) [Abs]: 19 Australian international hockey players (women), four hot sessions, against an ingested pill. "Poor" agreement; CORE read low by **0.06 to 0.34 °C** on average, **more so as temperature rose**; **41 to 60%** of samples differed by more than 0.3 °C. `@goods2023`
- **Jolicoeur Desroches et al. 2023** (J Therm Biol 115:103622) [Abs]: 12 people, 5 km running time trial at 30 °C, against a pill. Mean absolute difference **0.47 °C**; CORE underestimated the rate of rise. `@jolicoeur2023`
- **Januário et al. 2024** (J Therm Biol 123:103907) [Abs]: the Calera Research Sensor (relation to CORE not verified: **check before saying it is the same maker**), 15 cyclists at 32 °C against a pill: bias 0.01 °C, 95% limits about ±0.36 °C, judged valid against a ±0.4 °C criterion. `@januario2024`
- **None of the trials above used the sensor.** Mikkelsen used rectal probes [FT]; the others prescribed time, temperature and intensity.
- Target range: Mikkelsen sessions ended at 39.4 to 39.9 °C. Heat illness is usually associated with core temperature above 40 °C (Dolson 2022 [Abs]). So the working range is about a degree wide and the sensor's error is about half a degree either way.

## Racing in the heat and cooling (added at George's request, 5 October 2026)

- **Tour de France 2026** [Rep; pages read through a summarising tool, so check wording and dates on the pages]: AFP, 8 July 2026: start in Barcelona above 30 °C; stage 3 Granollers to Les Angles in **36 °C**, won by Pogačar, who called it "a logistic nightmare"; **up to 40 °C** forecast for stage 4 from Carcassonne (another report gives 37 °C at the Foix finish); frozen vests, ice socks (banned during racing), ice lollies, Netcompany Ineos riders immersing forearms in iced water, Alpecin frozen carbohydrate gels; wildfires near Perpignan. `@afp2026`. Stage 9 (12 July, Malemort to Ussel) cut from **185.5 to 155.5 km** under a red heatwave alert for the Corrèze. `@cyclinguptodate2026`. Team names as reported; "Ineos" and "Alpecin" used as short forms in the draft.
- 2022 Tour, stage 15 Rodez to Carcassonne in about 40 °C, Pogačar in an ice vest at sign-on [Rep]: not used; available if wanted.
- **Bongers et al. 2015** (Br J Sports Med 49:377–384) [Abs]: meta-analysis, 28 trials in men at above 30 °C. Cooling before exercise **+5.7%**, during **+9.9%** (difference not significant), overall +6.7%. Ice vest most effective during; combinations most effective before. No correlation between core temperature and performance. `@bongers2015`
- **Siegel et al. 2010** (MSSE 42:717–725) [Abs]: 10 men, ice slurry (7.5 g/kg) or cold water before running to exhaustion at 34 °C. **50.2 against 40.7 min**. Rectal temperature before the run down **0.66 against 0.25 °C**; at exhaustion **39.36 against 39.05 °C**. `@siegel2010`
- **Burdon et al. 2013** (IJSNEM 23:458–469) [Abs]: 10 men, 90 min cycling at 32 °C then a time trial. Sports drink as slushy **18:28**, warm **20:24**, warm plus slushy mouthwash **19:45** (slushy significantly better than warm, not than mouthwash). `@burdon2013`
- **Schmit et al. 2017** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 27:55–65) [Abs]: 13 male triathletes, 20 km time trial at 35 °C before and after an 8-day heat camp, with and without ice-vest pre-cooling. Vest **+10 W** before the camp; camp **+28 W**; vest **+4 W**, "unclear", after it. `@schmit2017`
- Not sourced: "ice absorbs a great deal of heat as it melts" (school physics); what an ice sock is (our description).

## Our arithmetic and reasoning, labelled as such in the draft

- 5 h of heat a week out of about 8.5 h (509 min) total in the Copenhagen trial.
- 42 g on 893 g = 4.7%.
- The "one network of labs" caveat; the judgement that the dose is a poor trade for a 6 to 10 hour rider; the comparison of sensor error with the width of the target range.
