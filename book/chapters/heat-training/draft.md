## Ice vests on the start line

The 2026 Tour de France began in Barcelona in temperatures above 30 °C and got hotter. Stage 3, from Granollers over the border to Les Angles, was raced in 36 °C while wildfires burned near Perpignan. Tadej Pogačar won it and called the day "a logistic nightmare", by which he meant the quantity of water and ice his team had to get to its riders. The next stage, out of Carcassonne, had up to 40 °C forecast. Riders that week waited for the start in frozen vests. Ineos had its riders stand with their forearms in iced water. Alpecin handed out carbohydrate gels that had been frozen solid. Ice socks, stockings packed with ice and pushed down the back of a jersey, are banned once the racing starts and were everywhere before and after it {shadow @afp2026}. Four days later the organisers cut 30 km from stage 9, between Malemort and Ussel, after the Corrèze was put on a red heatwave alert {shadow @cyclinguptodate2026}.

Cooling a rider on the day is one answer to heat. The other is to send him to the race already adapted to it. In January 2025 Jeroen Swart, a sports scientist who has spent six years at UAE Team Emirates, described part of how the team had prepared Pogačar the previous season. They had been running training sessions at 35 °C and 90% humidity. Altitude training, he explained, thickens the blood by shrinking its liquid part, and heat does the opposite: plasma volume, he is quoted as saying, "can be increased by up to 25 per cent" [@tourmagazin2025b].

The practice has spread well beyond one team. A group of Danish researchers who work with professional riders wrote in 2024 that heat training had become widespread among them. Riders were using it to go faster in ordinary temperatures as well as to prepare for hot races [@cubel2024]. The equipment has followed. A Swiss company sells a sensor the size of a matchbox that clips to a heart rate strap and shows an estimate of core body temperature on a bike computer. Its maker has announced partnerships with 10 professional teams {shadow @cyclingnews_core}.

Preparing for a hot race is well supported. Two weeks of training at about 35 °C improved cyclists' time-trial performance in the heat by 16% in one study [@karlsen2015]; 10 days improved it by 10% in another [@keiser2015]. {grade A}

The disputed claim is that heat also makes you faster on a cool day, acting as a training stimulus much as altitude does.

## Where the idea came from

The modern interest dates from a single paper. In 2010 Santiago Lorenzo and Christopher Minson at the University of Oregon took 12 trained cyclists and had them ride for 10 days at an easy intensity, about half of VO₂max, in a room at 40 °C. Eight matched controls did the same riding at 13 °C [@lorenzo2010].

When the heat group was tested afterwards in the cool room, their VO₂max had risen by 5%, from 66.8 to 70.2 mL/kg/min [@lorenzo2010]. The work they could do in a time trial had risen by 6%, and their power at the lactate threshold by 5%. The control group had not changed. Plasma volume in the heat group was up 6.5%, and the heart's maximum output had risen with it.

Other labs tried to repeat it, and the first attempts did not go well. The two-week study mentioned earlier tested its cyclists in the cool as well as the heat. After two weeks their VO₂max had gone from 4.79 to 4.82 L/min, and their power in a 43 km outdoor time trial from 300 W to 302 W [@karlsen2015]. The 10-day study used a crossover, in which the same seven men did 10 days of training at 38 °C and, separately, 10 days at 18 °C. The heat block raised their VO₂max and time-trial power in a hot room by about 10% each. In a room at 18 °C, both were unchanged [@keiser2015].

By 2016 the disagreement was formal. The *Journal of Physiology* ran one of its set-piece debates, with Minson and James Cotter arguing that heat acclimation does improve performance in the cool, and Lars Nybo and Carsten Lundby arguing that it does not [@minsoncotter2016; @nybolundby2016]. Even the proposers hedged. Their summary describes the benefit as unresolved and possibly "modest or absent in some settings and individuals". They pointed out that nobody had yet tested truly elite athletes or run the heat for longer than a couple of weeks.

Length turned out to matter, because the blood adapts to heat in two stages.

## How it works

Within about a week of daily heat exposure, the liquid part of the blood expands. The studies here put the increase at 6% to 7% [@lorenzo2010; @keiser2015; @mikkelsen2019], well short of Swart's 25%, although he did say "up to". More plasma helps the heart fill on each beat and supply the skin.

Sweating starts sooner and becomes more copious and more dilute. In one five-week study, sweat rate rose by 0.44 litres an hour [@cubel2024]. Core temperature at a given workload comes down, helping the rider cope with heat.

For performance in the cool, the same change cuts both ways. Chapter 1 described VO₂max as mostly a matter of oxygen delivery, which is the amount of blood the heart can pump multiplied by the oxygen each litre carries. Extra plasma helps the first and hurts the second, because the same number of red cells is now spread through more liquid. The Copenhagen group suggested that the two roughly cancel [@mikkelsen2019]. That would explain why 10 days of heat does so much in a hot room and so little in a cool one.

The second stage is slow, and it is the reason for the current fashion. If heat exposure continues for several weeks, the total amount of haemoglobin in the body starts to rise. Haemoglobin is the protein in red cells that carries oxygen, and its total mass, measured in grams, sets how much oxygen the blood can carry in all. Raising it is the whole point of altitude training.

The proposed explanation is that the kidney, which makes the hormone driving red cell production, responds to blood concentration. Dilute the blood with extra plasma for long enough and the kidney reads a shortage of red cells and orders more [@cubel2024]. The evidence is indirect: Copenhagen researchers found no change in the relevant hormones during five and a half weeks of heat training, although haemoglobin rose [@oberholzer2019].

That suggests a longer heat block could raise haemoglobin mass and perhaps performance. Since 2019, trials lasting four or five weeks have tested it.

> **From the saddle.** It was crazy hot in the 2026 heatwave. My watts were way down when the pain cave was hitting 37 degrees. On a holiday in Greece soon afterwards I felt I was hitting some good power numbers in the Greek heat. When it cooled down, it was good to see my watts go back to normal.


## What the five-week trials show

Six have been published, and their designs are similar enough to compare. Riders add about five hour-long sessions a week of easy riding in the heat, for five weeks, and are compared with a control group doing the same training in normal temperatures. The heat comes either from a chamber at 38 to 40 °C or from a heat suit worn while riding in an ordinary room.

::: trial
Question: Does five weeks of easy riding in the heat raise haemoglobin mass, and does it make elite athletes faster in the cool?
| | Haemoglobin mass, against controls | Performance in the cool, against controls
| 23 elite male cyclists [@ronnestad2021] | Up 42 g, from 893 to 935 g; controls up 6 g | VO₂max no better; threshold and 15-minute power up by small to intermediate amounts, not significant
| Cyclists, heat suit or chamber [@ronnestad2022a] | Up 2.6% with the suit and 2.4% in the chamber; controls down 0.7% | Threshold and one-minute power better with the suit only; heat beat control, 4.9% against 1.7%, only with both heat groups and three measures combined
| 25 elite cross-country skiers [@ronnestad2022b] | 30 g higher than controls | No difference in VO₂max, economy or a 15-minute test
| 20 elite women and 27 elite men [@lundby2023] | Up 4.0% in the women and 3.5% in the men | Threshold, VO₂max and 15-minute power all improved more with heat
| 20 elite male cyclists, 2024 [@cubel2024] | Up 3% after three weeks and 4% after five | Peak power up 12 W in a ramp test; VO₂max change not significant
:::

On haemoglobin every trial points the same way, and the size of the effect, 30 to 40 g or 3% to 4%, is about what an altitude camp delivers. One study made that comparison directly in seven riders who did both: 5.4% from five weeks of heat, 3.5% from three weeks at 3,000 metres [@cubel2026]. {grade B}

On performance they do not agree: two say yes, one says yes after pooling and two say no. A meta-analysis that gathered 28 heat studies of all lengths put the effect on cool-weather VO₂max, compared with control training, at 0.30 standard deviations, which is conventionally called small, with a confidence interval running from 0.06 to 0.54 [@waldron2021]. My reading is that there is probably a real effect on performance in elite riders, that it is small, and that it is much less certain than the effect on haemoglobin. {grade B}

Carsten Lundby, who argued the "no" side of the 2016 debate, is an author on every one of these five-week trials. The evidence comes from one connected group of labs in Copenhagen and Lillehammer; nobody outside it has yet repeated the result.

The riders were nearly all elite, and the men among them had VO₂max values in the mid-70s [@ronnestad2021; @lundby2023]. An elite rider has little room left to improve by ordinary training, so a 3% rise in haemoglobin is worth chasing.

And the gain does not last. In the 2024 trial, haemoglobin mass was back to its starting value two weeks after the heat sessions stopped [@cubel2024]. Rønnestad's group found that three sessions a week were enough to hold it [@ronnestad2022a]. Heat training is therefore something you keep doing or time carefully, like a taper.

### One study, up close: the Copenhagen amateurs

In 2019 Nybo and Lundby's group published the first trial long enough to test the haemoglobin idea, and it remains the only one in riders who resemble the readers of this book [@mikkelsen2019; @oberholzer2019].

::: trial
Hypothesis: Training in the heat would raise VO₂max, peak power and performance in a 15 km time trial, all tested in the cool.
Design: Randomised controlled trial lasting five and a half weeks. 24 male cyclists recruited, with at least three years of riding behind them, an average age of 38 and an average VO₂max of about 58 mL/kg/min: good club riders. Everyone was tested before and after at 14 °C [@mikkelsen2019].
| | Heat | Control
| Riders who finished | 12 | 9
| Training | Part of normal training replaced by an hour at 60% of VO₂max in a chamber at 40 °C, five days a week | All riding below 15 °C
| Training time | About 8½ hours a week | About 9½ hours a week
-- After five and a half weeks
| 15 km time trial at 14 °C, average power | 298 W to 315 W | 294 W to 311 W
| VO₂max | 60.0 to 59.8 mL/kg/min | 57.9 to 59.4 mL/kg/min
| Peak power, ramp test | 409 W to 422 W | 408 W to 411 W
~ Neither changed significantly in either group.
| Haemoglobin mass [@oberholzer2019] | Up 34 g | Up 2 g
Authors' conclusion: Training in the heat was no better than normal training for aerobic power or time-trial performance in cool conditions. Replacing a large part of the riders' training with heat sessions did not reduce what they gained from training either.
:::

The heat sessions were harder than "an hour at 60%" sounds. Rectal temperature at the end of every one was above 39 °C, 39.9 °C in the first and 39.4 °C in the last. By every standard test the riders acclimated: their sweat became more dilute, their heart rate in the heat fell, and they lasted longer in a hot endurance test [@mikkelsen2019].

They were no faster in the cool for it. Both groups gained 17 W in the time trial. The difference in haemoglobin mass between the groups fell just short of statistical significance, at p = 0.061 [@oberholzer2019]. The elite trials later found a similar gain, and here it bought nothing.

Twenty-one riders is few, and a real performance difference of a few watts could be hidden in a group that size. The controls improved by 17 W over five weeks, which suggests the whole group was coming into form after winter. A rising tide of that kind can swamp a small effect [@mikkelsen2019]. Only men took part. But the central result is hard to argue with for an amateur weighing this up. Riders much like you gave five of their eight and a half weekly hours to sitting in a 40 °C chamber, and were no faster than the riders who trained normally. {grade C} for the claim that heat training improves cool-weather performance in amateurs.

### Reading the research: a marker is not a performance

Haemoglobin mass measures a proposed route to better performance. Time-trial power measures whether the rider actually got faster. The heat trials show why the distinction matters.

Haemoglobin rose in all six trials, but the skiers and the Copenhagen amateurs gained no performance advantage over controls [@ronnestad2022b; @mikkelsen2019]. A headline saying "heat training increases haemoglobin mass" leaves that out.

When a study reports a marker, look for the performance measure in the same paper. If it is missing, the study has shown that a mechanism was engaged. If it is present and didn't move, that is the finding, whatever the title says.

The table shows the same thing from the other side. Where only the heat-suit group improved, heat beat control only once both heat groups and three tests were combined [@ronnestad2022a]. A choice of that kind, made after the data are in, raises the odds of finding something, and a result that appears only after pooling deserves less weight than one that was there in the first comparison. What survives is the marker itself, which rose in every trial, and the gain in hot conditions, which is well established.

## The sensor

The device most riders mean is called CORE, made by a Swiss company, greenTEG. It doesn't measure core temperature, which can only be measured from inside the body. It measures the temperature of the skin on your chest and the rate at which heat is flowing out through it. It optionally adds heart rate, and uses an algorithm to estimate what the temperature deep in the body must be [@verdel2021].

I don't own one. I found three independent studies comparing it with a direct measurement.

One group tested it against a rectal probe during indoor cycling. It was repeatable: when 12 men did the same ride twice, the sensor's readings on the two days differed by an average of 0.02 °C. Against the probe it did less well. At 19 °C it read 0.23 °C high on average, and only 51% of its readings were within 0.3 °C of the true value. At 31 °C it read 0.10 °C low on average, with 45% of readings within 0.3 °C. Most individual readings fell somewhere between 0.6 °C below the probe and 0.7 °C above it [@verdel2021]. The authors concluded that their results did not support the maker's claim of a valid measure.

Two other independent tests, one in hockey players at a heat camp and one in a 5 km run at 30 °C, found it reading low as people got hotter, or lagging behind the real rise {shadow @goods2023; @jolicoeur2023}. I found no independent test of the newer, smaller CORE 2.

For heat training, an error of half a degree matters a good deal. The sessions in the Copenhagen trial ended with core temperatures between 39.4 and 39.9 °C [@mikkelsen2019]. Heat illness is usually associated with core temperatures above 40 °C [@dolson2022]. The useful range is about one degree wide. The sensor's error in the published tests is about half a degree in either direction, and in two of the three studies it tended to read low when the body is hottest. A reading of 38.8 °C could mean you are not yet in the range, or that you are at the top of it.

It is also worth knowing that none of the trials in this chapter used the sensor. The Copenhagen group used rectal probes. They have obvious drawbacks for home use. Rønnestad's group prescribed time, a suit or a room temperature, and an easy intensity. The haemoglobin results came from riders who were simply made hot for 50 minutes, five times a week [@ronnestad2022a].

A repeatable but inaccurate reading might help track your own sessions, though it cannot tell you your true temperature. The sensor is optional: the training benefits were demonstrated without it.

## Cooling on the day

The ice on those start lines has more trial evidence behind it than most of this chapter. A 2015 meta-analysis pooled 28 trials of cooling, all in men exercising at above 30 °C. Cooling before the effort improved performance by an average of 5.7%, and cooling during it by 9.9%; the difference between those two was not statistically significant. During exercise, ice vests were the most effective method. Beforehand, combining several methods worked best [@bongers2015]. {grade A}

The slushy has studies of its own. Ice absorbs a great deal of heat as it melts, so a drink that is partly ice takes more heat out of the body than the same drink served cold. In one study, 10 men drank either an ice slurry or cold water before running to exhaustion at 34 °C. The slurry lowered their rectal temperature by 0.66 °C before they started, against 0.25 °C for the water, and they ran for 50 minutes instead of 41 [@siegel2010]. In 10 cyclists, taking a sports drink as a slushy through 90 minutes of riding at 32 °C brought the time for a time trial straight afterwards down from 20:24 to 18:28 {shadow @burdon2013}.

That cycling study had a third arm, in which the riders swilled the slushy round their mouths and spat it out. They finished in 19:45, between the other two. Some of the benefit of cold is in the sensation, with nothing swallowed. The meta-analysis points the same way: across its 28 trials, how far a method lowered core temperature did not predict how much it improved performance [@bongers2015]. Feeling cooler lets you work harder. In the running study the men who had drunk the slurry stopped at a higher rectal temperature than they reached after cold water, 39.4 °C against 39.1 °C [@siegel2010]. The ice had bought them room to go further into the heat, and they used it.

Cooling and acclimation have been compared directly. Thirteen triathletes did a 20 km time trial at 35 °C before and after an eight-day training camp in the heat, each time with and without an ice vest beforehand {shadow @schmit2017}. Before the camp the vest was worth 10 W. The camp was worth 28 W. After the camp the vest added 4 W, an effect the authors called unclear, although the riders still felt more comfortable with it. So a rider who has adapted gets less from the ice, and a rider who hasn't had the chance to adapt gets the most. For an amateur flying to a hot sportive from a British spring, that second case is the usual one.

## What to do with it

If your target event will be hot, heat training is worth doing, and the case is as strong as any in this book. One to two weeks of regular riding in the heat before the event is what the 2015 studies used, with gains of 10% to 16% in hot conditions [@karlsen2015; @keiser2015]. The sweating and cardiovascular adaptations were largely complete within three weeks in the 2024 trial, and they were gone two weeks after stopping [@cubel2024], so the block needs to run up close to the event. On the day itself, cool down before the start and keep the coldest, iciest drink you can carry.

If the aim is to be faster in ordinary temperatures, my advice for most readers is not to bother. The dose that raises haemoglobin is about five hours a week for five weeks. For a professional training 25 hours a week, five easy hours in a heat suit are a small part of the programme. For a rider with eight hours, they are most of it, and they are hours at low power in conditions that make quality work impossible. The one trial in riders at that level found no advantage [@mikkelsen2019]. The time is better spent on the things the earlier chapters covered.

If you want to try it anyway, perhaps because you are short of ways to add load without adding hours on the road, the trials give a template. Ride indoors at a genuinely easy intensity for 50 to 60 minutes, with no fan and enough clothing to make you sweat heavily. Do it four or five times a week for five weeks, and then two or three times a week to hold what you have gained [@ronnestad2022a]. Expect your power at a given heart rate to be well down in these sessions. Keep your hard sessions cool, as the riders in the trials did.

Be careful. The sessions in the trials were supervised, the riders were young and screened, and their core temperatures were being measured properly. Riding to a core temperature above 39 °C alone in a spare room is a different undertaking. Drink, stop if you feel dizzy, confused or chilled, and don't do it at all without medical advice if you have a heart condition or take medication that affects sweating or blood pressure.

The longer trials established an increase in haemoglobin more clearly than an increase in speed. For an amateur weighing five hot hours against ordinary training, the one direct comparison found no advantage.
