## The question

Does training in the heat make you faster when it isn't hot, and do you need a core-temperature sensor to do it?

## The argument

1. Heat training has two purposes that get confused. Preparing for a hot race is settled science. Using heat as a substitute for altitude, to get fitter in normal conditions, is the fashion.
2. The fashion started with one 2010 study (Lorenzo) in which 10 days of heat raised VO₂max and time-trial power in the cool. Short replications failed (Karlsen 2015, Keiser 2015).
3. Mechanism: plasma volume expands within a week, which helps in the heat but dilutes the blood. Over about five weeks, total haemoglobin mass rises by 3 to 4%, possibly because the kidney responds to the dilution. This second step is the altitude-like effect.
4. Five-week trials (2019 to 2026), mostly from one network of Scandinavian labs and mostly in elite riders, agree that haemoglobin mass rises. They disagree about whether performance follows. The one trial in amateurs found no advantage over normal training.
5. The dose is five hours a week of extra heat for five weeks, and the gain is gone two weeks after stopping. For a rider on 6 to 10 hours a week that is a poor trade unless the target event is hot.
6. The CORE sensor is repeatable but, in three independent studies, did not agree with rectal or ingested-pill temperature closely enough for the narrow target range heat training uses. The trials did not use it. It is optional.

Expected grades: heat acclimation for hot races A; haemoglobin mass after five weeks in elite riders B; performance gain in cool conditions for elites B (small, inconsistent), for amateurs C.
