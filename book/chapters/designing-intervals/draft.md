# Chapter 11: Designing intervals

In 2013 Stephen Seiler's group in Kristiansand published a trial in 35 cyclists who were very like the readers of this book [@seiler2013]. They were recreational riders, about 52 mL/kg/min of VO₂peak, who had been training about six hours a week with one or two interval sessions in it. For seven weeks, three groups did two interval sessions a week of 4 × 4, 4 × 8 or 4 × 16 minutes, each ridden as hard as the rider could sustain for that session, and a fourth group rode only easy. The 4 × 8 group improved twice as much as any other. The 4 × 4 group, whose intervals ran at the highest heart rate and felt harder than the 4 × 8s, improved by 5.5%, little more than the 4.2% of the group that rode only easy.

There is no single best interval session, and the claims made for particular ones are mostly larger than the trials behind them. What the trials do support is a pattern. In trained cyclists the sessions that let a rider pile up the most work close to the top of what they can sustain, whether as intervals of four to eight minutes or as 30/15s, beat both shorter, harder intervals and longer, easier ones [@seiler2013; @ronnestad2015b]. Long intervals beat all-out sprints for time-trial performance, and sprints run all-out carry a risk of injury [@rosenblat2020; @helgerud2023]. The order in which the sessions come across the weeks made no difference in the largest trial [@sylta2016]. So the practical choices are fewer than the arguments suggest: pick one or two formats you will ride hard, ride every set at the highest effort you can hold for all of it, and progress by adding work at that effort. {grade B}

Underneath most arguments about interval sessions sits one idea: that the best session is the one that keeps you closest to your VO₂max for longest. My reading is that time near VO₂max is the best working explanation for what a hard session does, and the most useful single number for comparing two sessions on the day. It has not been shown to be the reason intervals work. The one study that followed it through a whole training programme found that the riders who spent more of their sessions close to VO₂max improved more, but those riders also trained two hours a week more. And the same session gives very different amounts of it depending on how hard it is ridden. Use it to design and judge the hard sessions, and don't treat it as the only thing a session does.

## Why intervals work: time near VO₂max

In 2000 Véronique Billat's group put eight long-distance runners on a 400 m track, carrying portable gas analysers, and had them run two hard sessions [@billat2000]. In one they alternated 30 seconds at the speed that just brought out their VO₂max with 30 seconds at half that speed. In the other they ran without stopping at a speed halfway between their threshold and that top speed. The two sessions left about the same lactate in their blood. During the stop-start session the runners spent 7 minutes 51 seconds at VO₂max, and during the continuous one 2 minutes 42 seconds.

Twenty-five years of interval research has been organised around that difference. Intervals themselves are much older than any measurement of what they do. Chapter 8 told how the leading runners of the 1930s to the 1950s trained on repeated hard efforts, Emil Zátopek running dozens of 400 m repeats in a session {shadow @casado2023}. A 2001 review by Billat traced the method to the German physicians Hans Reindell and Helmut Roskamm and to Zátopek's example, and noted that coaches had set interval speeds from race paces, not from any physiological marker {shadow @billat2001}. Measuring what a session does became practical only when gas analysers became light enough to carry, and by 2006 enough laboratory work had accumulated for a review to propose rules for the work and recovery periods that keep a runner longest at or near VO₂max [@midgley2006]. That review was explicit that its rules described what sessions do, and that experiments were needed before anyone could say what the sessions would produce over weeks. Most of the research since has been of the first kind.

At the start of hard work, oxygen uptake does not jump to its new level. It rises over a minute or two, so a short effort ends before the body's oxygen use has caught up with the demand. In the severe domain, above critical power (Chapters 1 and 5), it then keeps creeping upward, an effect known as the slow component, until it reaches VO₂max or the rider stops. That is how Billat's continuous runners reached VO₂max in more than half the attempts despite running below the speed that would bring it out straight away [@billat2000].

Close to VO₂max the heart is pumping as much blood as it can and nearly every muscle fibre in the legs is working. The usual argument is that this is the strongest signal the heart and the muscles receive, and a widely cited review describes the optimal stimulus as "at least several minutes per session" above 90% of VO₂max [@buchheit2013]. In a 2007 trial from Trondheim, the two interval programmes raised the amount of blood the heart pumped per beat by about 10% [@helgerud2007]. That is the mechanism as its proponents describe it. Nobody has shown that time near VO₂max is what produces the adaptation, rather than something that goes with it, such as the total amount of hard work.

The muscles are part of the argument too. Chapter 7 described how an easy ride is carried mostly by the slow, fatigue-resistant fibres, with the fast fibres joining in only as the effort rises or the ride goes on. Close to VO₂max nearly all of them are working, and a fibre that is not used receives no signal to adapt. In a 2017 Canadian study, ten active men trained one leg with intervals and the other with continuous cycling, doing the same total work with each, for six sessions over two weeks. Citrate synthase, an enzyme used as a marker of mitochondrial content, rose further in the interval leg, to 10.2 against 8.4 in the units the study used {shadow @macinnis2017}. Using each person as their own control removes most of the differences between people that cloud other comparisons, although ten men and two weeks is a small and short test.

The 90% line is a convention. An earlier review used 95% [@midgley2006], and the most careful recent study found that the average share of VO₂max across the work intervals said more than the minutes above any line [@odden2024].

## Short work periods, and the catch

Oxygen uptake also falls slowly when the effort stops. Fifteen seconds of easy pedalling is too short for it to drop far, so a session of 30 seconds hard and 15 easy holds oxygen uptake high through the recoveries as well as the efforts. The 2006 review recommended work and recovery periods of 15 to 30 seconds for exactly this reason [@midgley2006].

The cycling studies agree, at matched power. When 13 well-trained cyclists rode intervals to exhaustion at the power that brings out VO₂max, work periods of 30 seconds gave longer above 90% than longer work periods at the same power {shadow @ronnestad2016}. In 2024 the same Norwegian group had 11 women and 8 men ride three sessions of 6 × 8 minutes at the same average power, their best for 40 minutes: steady, alternating 60 seconds harder and easier, or 30/15. The 30/15 version gave 1,123 seconds above 90% of VO₂max, against 879 for the steady one and 917 for the 60/60, and the women responded as the men did [@urianstad2024]. Varying the power within long intervals helps too. Fourteen cyclists spent 410 seconds above 90% when each work interval mixed harder and easier sections, against 286 seconds when the power was steady, at the same average power and with no difference in how hard the session felt [@bossi2020].

Every one of those comparisons fixed the power. Riders on the road do not ride at a fixed power, and the difference turns out to matter more than the format.

In 2025 twelve well-trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 × 13 × 30/15 and 6 × 5 minutes twice each: once at prescribed powers, and once self-paced and as hard as they could manage [@appelhans2025]. At prescribed powers the 30/15 session won easily, with 420 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 178. Ridden all-out, both sessions produced far more, 1,130 seconds and 1,016, and the difference between them was no longer statistically significant.

In runners the short format can lose outright. Twelve highly trained middle-distance runners spent 201 seconds above 90% of VO₂max in a session of 24 × 30 seconds and 328 seconds in 4 × 3 minutes, even though the 30-second efforts were run faster [@fleckenstein2024]. Their heart rates said the opposite: 820 seconds above 90% of maximum heart rate in the short session against 545 in the long one. In short intervals heart rate and oxygen uptake do not move together, and heart rate can make a session look closer to the ceiling than it is.

Perceived effort is steadier. When twelve well-trained runners chose their own pace for sessions of 1-, 2-, 4- and 6-minute repeats, they rated every one at about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, very hard, and their lactate was similar across all of them [@seiler2004]. The 2- to 6-minute repeats all took them to a peak oxygen uptake of about 92% of VO₂max, against 82% for the 1-minute repeats, and six of the twelve reached their highest oxygen uptake in the 4-minute session. The pace adjusted to the length; the effort did not.

So the format of a session changes its time near VO₂max less than the effort a rider is willing to put into it. A 30/15 session makes a large dose easier to reach at a power that feels manageable, and that is a real advantage. A rider who paces long intervals well can get much the same. {grade B}

### Reading the research: a correlation inside a trial

Until 2024 nobody had measured oxygen uptake in every session of a training programme and then asked whether it predicted the result [@odden2024].

The study did something nobody had done, and did it carefully. The riders were tested for VO₂max twice during the programme as well as before and after, so each session's oxygen uptake was compared with a current VO₂max rather than one that the training had already left behind. The share of VO₂max proved more repeatable from session to session than the share of maximum heart rate, and it tracked the improvements better [@odden2024].

What the study cannot show is that reaching a higher share of VO₂max caused the bigger improvements, and the table shows why. Every rider rode the same session at the same share of their own 40-minute power. Whether a rider reached 86% of VO₂max or 80% was therefore a property of the rider, not of the session. Riders whose 40-minute power sits high relative to their VO₂max work closer to the ceiling at that power, and the paper reports that riders with a higher power at a lactate of 4 mmol/L and a higher 15-minute power before the programme reached a higher share during it [@odden2024]. The two halves were different groups of people before the training began.

They also trained differently while it ran. The higher half rode two hours a week more, most of the difference in the lowest zone, and they started with a lower VO₂max, which leaves more room to improve. The authors acknowledge that the extra volume "could have affected the results", and judge that the share of VO₂max was "most likely" responsible, because the lower half did slightly more training in the two hardest zones [@odden2024]. That is a reasonable judgement. It is a judgement, not a result.

The skill is to notice when a trial has stopped being a trial. Assigning riders to sessions at random lets a study compare the sessions. Splitting them afterwards by something they did, or something about them, turns the comparison into an observational one, with all the confounding of Chapter 10's cohorts. A correlation of this kind is a strong reason to run the trial that would test it, in which riders are assigned to sessions that differ only in how close to VO₂max they keep them. As far as I can find, nobody has run it. {grade C}

## Doses, and other routes

Chapter 2 described the HERITAGE study, in which 481 sedentary adults did the same 20 weeks of training, gained about 400 millilitres a minute of VO₂max on average, and varied from more than a litre to almost nothing. It also described the Zurich experiment in which giving people more sessions a week made the apparent non-responders disappear {shadow @monterolundby2017}.

In 2013 a group that included Michael Joyner, whose model of endurance opened Chapter 1, asked the same question of interval training [@bacon2013]. They gathered 37 studies in which sedentary or recreationally active people under 45 did at least ten minutes of hard work per session, three or more times a week, for six to thirteen weeks. The average gain was 0.51 litres a minute, more than HERITAGE's in a fraction of the time. The nine studies with the largest gains averaged 0.87 litres and the nine with the smallest 0.27, and the larger gains came from programmes that ran longer, 9.7 weeks against 6.9, and, though the difference was not significant, had more training each week, 209 minutes against 123. In the studies with the longest intervals, the authors reported marked gains in almost every participant. They also found signs that small studies with small effects had gone unpublished, and correcting for that lowered the average to 0.37 litres a minute.

The argument they drew from it is that much apparent non-response is under-dosing: programmes too short, too easy or too sparse to move VO₂max in everyone. That fits the Zurich result, and it fits the idea of time near VO₂max, because the programmes that worked best spent more of it. It does not separate time near VO₂max from the other things those programmes also had more of, which were hard minutes, weeks and sessions.

Comparisons of whole programmes support a narrower claim: that intervals raise VO₂max more than continuous training, by a modest margin. A 2015 analysis of 28 controlled studies in 723 healthy adults found that both raised it substantially against no exercise, by 4.9 mL/kg/min for continuous training and 5.5 for intervals, and that intervals beat continuous training by 1.2 mL/kg/min on average, with the gap larger in less fit people and in longer programmes [@milanovic2015]. A difference of that size, about 3% for a recreational rider, is real and modest. {grade B}

The same evidence shows other routes to the same place. In 2024 a Canadian group randomised 84 young adults to six weeks of training in one of four intensity domains, defined from each person's own thresholds, or to no training [@inglis2024]. Continuous riding in the upper part of the band between the thresholds raised VO₂max by 5.4 mL/kg/min, nearly as much as intervals in the severe domain at 6.2, and all-out sprints raised it by 4.7. Riding below the first threshold, matched for work, raised it by 1.8, which was not significantly different from doing nothing. Because each person's domains were set from their own tests, everyone in a group received a similar physiological stimulus, which is the authors' main point: prescribing intensity from a person's thresholds, not from a percentage of their maximum, made the training more uniform.

And closest is not automatically best. In the trial this chapter opened with, the 4 × 4 riders worked at a higher heart rate than the 4 × 8 riders and found the session harder, and gained half as much [@seiler2013]. The longer session gave twice the work at a slightly lower intensity, and the amount of work seems to have mattered at least as much as how close to VO₂max it was done.

In people who are not very fit, then, almost any hard enough training raises VO₂max, and time near it is one route among several. In trained riders the claim is harder to test, because they improve slowly and trials are short, and the reviews that argue that trained athletes need time near VO₂max say "may" [@midgley2006]. The trials in trained cyclists that improved VO₂max mostly used intervals close to it, which is consistent with the idea without proving it. {grade C}

So time near VO₂max is best used as a way to compare sessions. Between two sessions of the same length and effort, the one that keeps you closer to the ceiling for longer is probably the stronger stimulus for the heart and the oxygen system. The best sessions in the figure gave 15 to 19 minutes above 90% of VO₂max, and they were sizeable: 6 × 8 minutes, 6 × 5 minutes or three sets of 13 × 30/15, each with 20 to 50 minutes of hard work in it. The often-quoted "several minutes" near VO₂max is a floor, not a target [@buchheit2013]. For a rider without a gas analyser, the guide is effort and power, not heart rate.

## What a session is made of

An interval session is defined by more numbers than its name suggests. The same widely used review counts up to nine: the intensity and length of the work periods, the intensity and length of the recoveries, the type of exercise, the number of repetitions, the number of sets, and the length and intensity of the rest between sets [@buchheit2013]. Most arguments about intervals change one of these and leave the reader to assume the others were equal.

There is a tenth that matters as much as any of them: how hard the session was actually ridden. Trials prescribe intensity in one of two ways. Either every rider works at a fixed share of a tested power, which makes the sessions comparable on paper but makes some of them much harder than others in practice, or every rider goes as hard as they can sustain for the whole set, which matches the sessions for effort and lets the power fall where it will. Most of the trials in this chapter used the second method, which is also what riders do on the road. The pacing study above showed what the choice can do: two sessions that differed by more than two to one in time near VO₂max at fixed powers were almost level when both were ridden all-out [@appelhans2025].

The heart rates and lactates show what effort-matching did. Asked to ride as hard as they could sustain, the 4 × 4 riders worked near their maximum heart rate and built up 13.2 mmol/L of lactate, the 4 × 16 riders settled at a level between the thresholds, and the 4 × 8 riders landed in between, close enough to the top to be very hard and long enough to double the work of the 4 × 4 session [@seiler2013]. It was neither the hardest session nor the longest that won.

The trial has limits that a reader of only the abstract cannot see past. Each group had nine riders, the programme lasted seven weeks, and the abstract gives a single figure combining three measures rather than each measure separately, so I can't say whether the 4 × 8 advantage was in VO₂peak, in threshold power or in both. The 4.2% gain in the easy-only group is a reminder that seven weeks of riding more than before will improve a rider whatever the sessions [@seiler2013]. But its central finding has a clear reading for a rider with two hard sessions a week: doubling the work at a slightly lower intensity did more than squeezing half the work in at the very top.

## Long intervals

Four to eight minutes is the oldest and best-tested range. The Trondheim group's 4 × 4 minutes, at 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate with three minutes of easy recovery, has been used in heart patients as well as athletes [@hatle2014]. In their 2007 trial, 40 moderately trained men trained three times a week for eight weeks, with every group doing the same amount of work. The 4 × 4 programme raised VO₂max by 7.2% and a 15-second-on, 15-second-off programme by 5.5%, while continuous training at 70% or 85% of maximum heart rate did less [@helgerud2007].

The Trondheim group's intervals made their name in a very different population. In 2007 the group randomised 27 patients with heart failure after a heart attack, with an average age of 75.5 and a VO₂peak of 13 mL/kg/min, to three sessions a week for twelve weeks of either intervals at 95% of peak heart rate or continuous exercise at 70% {shadow @wisloff2007}. Peak oxygen uptake rose 46% after the intervals and 14% after the continuous training, and only the interval group's hearts became smaller and pumped a larger share of their contents with each beat. Intervals that some cyclists think of as brutal were being prescribed to people in their seventies with failing hearts. Hard is relative to the person.

In trained cyclists, adding long intervals to a base of easy riding improves performance quickly. In 1996 eight competitive South African cyclists, riding about 300 km a week, replaced 15% of it with six sessions over four weeks of six to eight 5-minute efforts at 80% of their peak power. Their 40 km time trial fell from 56.4 to 54.4 minutes, and the power they held in it rose from 301 to 326 W {shadow @lindsay1996}. Three years later the same group gave 20 cyclists six sessions over three weeks of one of five session types, from 12 × 30 seconds at 175% of peak power to 4 × 8 minutes at 80%. The time trial improved most after 8 × 4 minutes at 85%, by 2.8%, and after the 30-second efforts, by 2.4%, while 60-second efforts at 100% and 8-minute efforts at 80% did not produce significant gains {shadow @stepto1999}. The authors suggested that efforts close to race pace and efforts far above it may work through different mechanisms.

The largest trial of long intervals in cyclists, published in 2016, put 63 riders with a VO₂peak of about 61 through 24 sessions in 12 weeks: 4 × 16, 4 × 8 and 4 × 4 minutes, all as maximal efforts for the session, arranged in three different orders [@sylta2016]. Every group improved its 40-minute power, peak power and VO₂peak by 5% to 10%, and the order made no difference at all, either to the averages or to how many riders responded strongly. Effort-matching again set the intensities: the riders' lactate averaged 4.7, 9.2 and 12.7 mmol/L in the three sessions.

Across sports the middle of the range keeps doing well. A 2025 analysis of 51 studies in 1,261 athletes, many of them runners and team-sport players, found that the effect of interval training on VO₂max rose with the length of the work bouts up to about 140 seconds and then fell away [@yang2025]. That is a statistical fit across very different studies rather than a rule, and it sits comfortably beside Seiler's runners, who reached their highest oxygen uptake in 4-minute repeats when they set their own pace [@seiler2004].

## Short intervals: 30/15

The session called 30/15 is three sets of 13 repetitions of 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, with three minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. That is 19.5 minutes of hard work in about 35 minutes, and the short recoveries keep oxygen uptake high throughout, as described above. Fifteen seconds is long enough to decide that the next 30 seconds are impossible, which is about when they start.

Two trials from Bent Rønnestad's group in Norway put it against effort-matched sessions of 4 × 5 minutes with two and a half minutes of recovery. In 2015, 16 cyclists did two sessions a week for ten weeks among their easy training, with the same volumes of hard and easy riding in both groups. VO₂max rose 8.7% in the 30/15 group against 2.6% in the long-interval group, and every performance measure, from 30-second to 40-minute power, moved in favour of the short intervals by a moderate to large amount [@ronnestad2015b]. In 2020 the same comparison in 18 elite cyclists, with a VO₂max of 73, ran for three weeks at three sessions a week. VO₂max did not differ between the groups, but the 30/15 group's peak power rose 3.7% against a fall of 0.3%, and its 20-minute power rose 4.7% against a fall of 1.4% [@ronnestad2020].

Both trials are small, both come from the group that designed the session, and both compared it with long intervals ridden at an even effort chosen by the rider. Long intervals are hard to pace, and in the study above in which both sessions were ridden all-out, they came out almost level in time near VO₂max [@appelhans2025]. The fairest reading is that 30/15s make it easier to do a large dose of work near the top, which is a real advantage for anyone who struggles to hold a hard long interval, and that a well-paced long-interval session may do much the same. {grade B}

## Threshold intervals

Between the long intervals of this chapter and the easy riding of Part II lies the band that Chapter 5 called "between the thresholds", and many amateurs spend much of their training in it. Intervals there, typically 8 to 20 minutes just below the second threshold, are less intense than anything above and easier to recover from: a single session took 24 to 48 hours of the heart's autonomic recovery in the review Chapter 12 describes, against 48 hours or more after harder intervals {shadow @stanley2013}.

In the trials they did less for VO₂max than harder sessions and more for the share of it a rider can sustain. Seiler's 4 × 16-minute group, which settled at a lactate of 4.9 mmol/L, gained 5.6%, the same as the 4 × 4 group and half as much as the 4 × 8s [@seiler2013]. In Stöggl and Sperlich's four-programme trial in Chapter 8, the group that did most of its hard work at threshold showed no significant change in VO₂peak and gained only 1.4% in power at a lactate of 4 mmol/L {shadow @stoggl2014}. On the other side, the elite skiers in Chapter 12's two-against-four trial did nothing but threshold intervals, 136 minutes a week, and the group that did them in two long sessions raised the share of VO₂max they could hold at threshold from 80.7% to 85.4% and became more economical {shadow @tonnessen2020}. The Norwegian runners described in Chapter 8 build their week around three or four lactate-controlled threshold sessions {shadow @casado2023}.

The pattern is consistent with what Chapter 1's model would predict. Threshold work trains the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain, and work near VO₂max trains the ceiling itself. A rider whose VO₂max is already high relative to their threshold has more to gain from the first; one whose ceiling is low has more to gain from the second. For most amateurs on six to ten hours, who have one or two hard sessions to spend, the trials favour giving at least one of them to intervals near the top. {grade C}

## Sprints

Sprint interval training usually means repeated efforts of about 30 seconds, all-out, with three or four minutes of rest. Its appeal is time. In 2006 Martin Gibala's group in Canada found that sixteen active young men gained as much in a time trial and in their muscles' oxidative capacity from six sessions of sprints over two weeks, about two and a half hours of training in all, as from about ten and a half hours of continuous riding {shadow @gibala2006}. Across 16 trials in young people, sprint training raised VO₂max by about 8%, the same as continuous training, although sprints keep oxygen uptake near VO₂max only briefly [@gist2014].

For a rider who already trains, the comparison that matters is with long intervals. A 2020 analysis of six trials in moderately trained people that compared the two found no overall difference in time-trial performance, but long intervals of four minutes or more beat sprints by 2%, and they raised maximal aerobic power more [@rosenblat2020]. A 2023 trial in 81 well-trained women, running, was starker. After eight weeks at three sessions a week, only 4 × 4 minutes raised VO₂max, by 7.3%; two sprint protocols raised anaerobic capacity instead and improved a 300 m sprint more [@helgerud2023]. Every group got faster over 3,000 m. And the 10 × 30-second all-out sprints caused eight injuries, six of them hamstring strains, each bad enough to stop training for at least a week. The other two protocols caused none.

Sprints raise VO₂max in untrained people, improve sprinting, and save time. For a trained rider aiming at long efforts they are the weaker choice, and run all-out they are the riskier one. That trial was on a treadmill, and I found no comparable count of injuries from sprinting on a bike. {grade B}

## How hard

Every successful trial in this chapter asked riders for the hardest effort they could sustain for the session, and the self-paced runners above describe what that feels like: about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, very hard, whatever the length of the repeats [@seiler2004].

That gives a practical rule. Start each set at the highest power you believe you can hold for all of it, expect the last repetition to be the hardest, and if you finish with something left, raise the power next time. Long efforts can be made to give more for the same effort by building surges into them, which added time near VO₂max without making the session feel any harder [@bossi2020].

## Fixed power or all-out

Smart trainers can hold a set power whatever the rider's cadence, and many riders do their intervals that way, typing in a power and letting the trainer enforce it. That is a fixed-power session in the sense of the trials, and the pacing study above found that every measure it took, from oxygen uptake to power, was higher when the riders chose their own pace and went as hard as they could [@appelhans2025]. The 6 × 5-minute session at fixed powers gave three minutes above 90% of VO₂max; ridden all-out, it gave seventeen.

Part of that gap may come from the particular powers the researchers chose. But the same thing happens to a rider who sets a power from an old test, or a cautious one. A fixed power is a ceiling on the session as well as a floor. The remedy is not to abandon the trainer's fixed mode, which makes the efforts repeatable and the recoveries genuinely easy, but to set the power from recent efforts, raise it when a session ends with something left, and accept that the last repetition should be very hard. {grade C}

## What each session does

The sessions are not interchangeable even when they raise VO₂max by similar amounts. In the women's trial, 4 × 4 minutes raised VO₂max and the sprints raised anaerobic capacity, the energy that can be released without oxygen, and improved a 300 m sprint more [@helgerud2023]. In Stepto's cyclists, the two sessions that improved a 40 km time trial were the one closest to race pace and the one far above it, and the authors suggested they worked by different routes {shadow @stepto1999}. Threshold sessions moved the fraction of VO₂max that could be sustained and the oxygen cost of moving, more than VO₂max itself {shadow @tonnessen2020}.

For a rider whose events last an hour or more, which covers most of the readers of this book, that argues for intervals of four minutes and longer as the core, with 30/15s as an alternative way to the same place and threshold sessions as a cheaper complement [@rosenblat2020]. Sprints train something most of those riders rarely need. A rider who races criteriums, or who wants to win the sprint for the village sign, has a reason to include them that the VO₂max trials do not capture.

## Recoveries and warm-up

The recoveries between efforts get less attention than the efforts, and the evidence suggests they deserve less. When nine well-trained runners did 6 × 4 minutes as hard as they could with recoveries of one, two or four minutes, the length of the rest made little difference to how fast they ran, how much oxygen they used or how hard it felt. Left to choose, they rested for about two minutes [@seiler2005]. The long-interval sessions in the trials used two and a half to three minutes [@ronnestad2020; @hatle2014]. Within that range, a rider can take whatever lets the next effort be as hard as the last.

The research sessions began with a warm-up of about ten minutes [@hatle2014], and the 2006 review recommended ten to fifteen minutes at an easy pace with no long gap before the first effort [@midgley2006]. I found nothing to suggest that a longer one adds anything.

## Order and variety

The 63 riders who did the same three sessions in three different orders over twelve weeks all improved by the same amount [@sylta2016]. Nothing in the evidence says that a block of long intervals must come before a block of short ones, or the reverse. Variety has other uses. A session a rider looks forward to gets ridden harder, and a rider preparing for long climbs or time trials has reason to keep long intervals in the mix, since those were the sessions that beat sprints on time-trial performance [@rosenblat2020]. Those are reasons of tolerance and specificity, not evidence that variety itself adds anything.

## Women and men

Most interval trials are of men, and the few direct comparisons of the sessions suggest they work in much the same way in women. In the 30/15 study above, which measured 11 women and 8 men, 30/15s gave more time near VO₂max than steady intervals in both, with no difference between the sexes [@urianstad2024]. The 4 × 4 trial in well-trained women raised VO₂max by 7.3%, much as in men, although one sprint protocol that had raised it in men did not in women, and the all-out sprints injured more women than the same protocol had injured men [@helgerud2023]. Chapter 18 describes a meta-analysis of untrained people that found men's VO₂max rose more than women's on the same training, and returns to women's training generally.

## The sessions, in brief

These are the sessions the trials in this chapter used, as a rider might ride them. Each assumes a warm-up of about ten minutes of easy riding with a few short pick-ups, and an easy spin afterwards. Chapter 17 puts two of them into a week.

![The five sessions in the list below on one time scale, each between a 10-minute warm-up and cool-down, with the minutes of hard work in each. Drawn for this book: the heights show roughly how hard each part is, not measured power.](figures/session-shapes.svg)

1. **4 × 4 minutes,** with 3 minutes of easy riding between. Ridden as hard as can be sustained for all four, which in the Trondheim trials meant 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate [@hatle2014]. Sixteen minutes of hard work; the shortest of the long sessions.
2. **4 × 8 minutes,** with 2 to 3 minutes between, about what Seiler's runners chose for themselves [@seiler2005]. The session that beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16 in recreational cyclists, ridden at about 90% of peak heart rate [@seiler2013]. Thirty-two minutes of hard work. A rider who finds 4 × 8 comfortable can add a fifth repetition, as the 22 cyclists in the study above did [@odden2024].
3. **30/15:** three sets of 13 × 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, 3 minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. The 30-second efforts are harder than a long interval and the 15 seconds are easy pedalling, not rest. Two sets is a reasonable start.
4. **Threshold intervals:** for example 3 × 12 or 2 × 20 minutes just below the second threshold, with 3 minutes between. Less intense, cheaper to recover from, and better for the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain than for VO₂max itself {shadow @tonnessen2020}.
5. **Sprint intervals:** 4 to 6 × 30 seconds all-out with 4 minutes of easy riding between. Useful for sprinting and for a rider short of time; the weaker choice for long efforts [@rosenblat2020].
