# Chapter 11: Designing intervals

Harder intervals are not always better intervals. In 2013 Stephen Seiler's group in Kristiansand split recreational cyclists, much like the readers of this book, into four groups for seven weeks. Three added two interval sessions a week to their easy riding, of 4 × 4, 4 × 8 or 4 × 16 minutes, each ridden as hard as the rider could sustain. The fourth rode only easy. The 4 × 8 group improved twice as much as any other. The 4 × 4 group, which rode hardest, at 94% of peak heart rate, gained 5.5% against the easy riders' 4.2% [@seiler2013].

There is no single best interval session, and the claims made for particular ones are mostly larger than the trials behind them. In trained cyclists the sessions that let a rider pile up the most work close to the top of what they can sustain, whether as intervals of four to eight minutes or as 30/15s, sets of 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, beat both shorter, harder intervals and longer, easier ones [@seiler2013; @ronnestad2015b]. Long intervals beat all-out sprints for time-trial performance, and sprints run all-out carry a risk of injury [@rosenblat2020]. In the largest trial, riders who worked from long intervals to short ones over 12 weeks gained as much as those who went from short to long, or mixed them. So the choice comes down to three things: pick one or two formats you will ride hard, ride every set at the highest effort you can hold for all of it, and progress by adding work at that effort. {grade B}

## Twice the work, a little less hard

The Kristiansand trial deserves a close look, because its design is the one most riders copy without knowing it. Nobody was given a target power. Every interval was ridden as hard as the rider could sustain for the set, so the sessions were matched for effort and the power fell where it would.

### One study, up close: four ways to spend two sessions a week

The heart rates and lactates show where each group ended up when left to find its own limit. The 4 × 4 riders worked near their maximum heart rate and built up 13.2 mmol/L of lactate. The 4 × 16 riders settled at a level between the thresholds. The 4 × 8 riders landed in between, close enough to the top to be very hard and long enough to double the work of the 4 × 4 session [@seiler2013]. It was neither the hardest session nor the longest that won.

The trial has limits. Each group had nine riders and the programme lasted seven weeks. The headline gain combines three measures, so on its own it doesn't show whether the 4 × 8 advantage was in VO₂peak, in threshold power or in both. And the 4.2% gain in the easy-only group is a reminder that seven weeks of riding more than before will improve a rider whatever the sessions [@seiler2013]. But its central finding is clear for a rider with two hard sessions a week: doubling the work at a slightly lower intensity did more than squeezing half the work in at the very top.

## What a hard session does: time near VO₂max

Interval sessions are usually judged by how long they keep a rider near VO₂max, and the idea comes from one experiment. In 2000 Véronique Billat's group put eight long-distance runners on a 400 m track, carrying portable gas analysers, and had them run two hard sessions [@billat2000]. In one they alternated 30 seconds at the speed that just brought out their VO₂max with 30 seconds at half that speed. In the other they ran without stopping at a speed halfway between their threshold and that top speed. The two sessions left about the same lactate in their blood. During the stop-start session the runners spent 7 minutes 51 seconds at VO₂max, and during the continuous one 2 minutes 42 seconds.

Twenty-five years of interval research has been organised around that difference, on the idea that the best session is the one that keeps you closest to your VO₂max for longest. A 2006 review of the running studies turned it into a recipe: efforts at 90% to 105% of the speed that brings out VO₂max, recoveries somewhere between half that speed and threshold pace, each lasting 15 to 30 seconds, after a warm-up of 10 to 15 minutes [@midgley2006]. The same review said that more experiments were needed to find out what adaptations such sessions produce before anyone advised competitive runners to use them.

At the start of hard work, oxygen uptake does not jump to its new level. It rises over a minute or two, so a short effort ends before the body's oxygen use has caught up with the demand. Above critical power (Chapters 1 and 5) it then keeps creeping upward until it reaches VO₂max or the rider stops, which is how Billat's continuous runners got there without running at the speed that would bring it out straight away. Oxygen uptake also falls slowly when the effort stops. Fifteen seconds of easy pedalling is too short for it to drop far, so a session of 30 seconds hard and 15 easy holds it high through the recoveries as well as the efforts, which fits the review's short work and recovery periods [@midgley2006].

Close to VO₂max the heart is pumping as much blood as it can and nearly every muscle fibre in the legs is working, the fast fibres as well as the slow. The usual argument is that this is the strongest signal the heart and the muscles receive. In a 2007 trial from Trondheim, two interval programmes raised the amount of blood the heart pumped per beat by about 10% [@helgerud2007]. In a 2017 Canadian study, ten active men trained one leg with intervals and the other with continuous cycling, matched for work, and a marker of mitochondrial content rose further in the interval leg {shadow @macinnis2017}. Most studies count the minutes above 90% of VO₂max, though the line is a convention, and the 2006 review used 95% [@midgley2006].

### Reading the research: a correlation inside a trial

Nobody has shown that time near VO₂max is what produces the adaptation, rather than something that goes with it, such as the total amount of hard work. Until 2024 nobody had even measured oxygen uptake in every session of a training programme and then asked whether it predicted the result [@odden2024].

::: trial
Aim: To find out whether the share of VO₂max that cyclists reached during their interval sessions was related to how much they improved.
Design: One training programme, not a comparison of programmes. 22 well-trained cyclists (19 men, 3 women; VO₂max 67.1 mL/kg/min) rode 21 sessions of 5 × 8 minutes at their best 40-minute power over nine weeks, on smart trainers, with oxygen uptake measured through every work interval. The main analysis was a regression across all 22; the authors also split the riders into halves by the average share of VO₂max they reached [@odden2024].

| Average share of VO₂max in the work intervals | 86.2% | 79.9%
| VO₂max at the start | 65.1 mL/kg/min | 69.1 mL/kg/min
| Training a week during the study | 9 h 43 min | 7 h 40 min
| In the lowest zone, below 55% of 40-minute power | 3 h 12 min | 1 h 48 min
~ The difference in lowest-zone training was statistically significant.
| In the two hardest zones | 1 h 45 min | 1 h 59 min

| VO₂max | Up 5.2 mL/kg/min | Up 1.8 mL/kg/min

| Best 15-minute power | Up 0.30 W/kg | Up 0.15 W/kg
~ The 15-minute difference was a tendency, not statistically significant.
Authors' conclusion: Improvements in endurance were positively related to the share of VO₂max reached during interval training, and that share was the measure that best reflected how much each rider adapted.
:::

The study was careful. The riders were tested for VO₂max during the programme as well as before and after, so each session was compared with a current VO₂max, and the share of VO₂max proved more repeatable from session to session than the share of maximum heart rate [@odden2024].

What it cannot show is that reaching a higher share caused the bigger improvements, and the table shows why. Every rider rode the same session at the same share of their own 40-minute power, so whether a rider reached 86% of VO₂max or 80% was a property of the rider, not of the session. The two halves were different groups of people before the training began, and they trained differently while it ran. The higher half rode two hours a week more, most of it in the lowest zone, and started with a lower VO₂max, which leaves more room to improve. The authors acknowledge that the extra volume "could have affected the results", and judge that the share of VO₂max was "most likely" responsible [@odden2024]. That judgement is reasonable, but it is a judgement, not a result.

The thing to check is what is being compared. When riders are assigned to different sessions at random, chance decides who does what, so a difference in results can be put down to the sessions. This study gave everyone the same session and then compared the riders by how they responded to it. That is the comparison Chapter 10's cohorts make, and it has the same weakness: the riders who did better differed in other ways too. The trial that would test the idea, with riders assigned at random to sessions that differ only in how close to VO₂max they keep them, has not been run as far as I can find. {grade C} What survives doesn't depend on this study. The randomised trials in this chapter favour the sessions that pile up the most work close to the top of what a rider can sustain.

## Other routes to a bigger engine

Comparisons of whole programmes support a narrower claim: that intervals raise VO₂max more than continuous training, by a modest margin. A 2015 analysis of 28 controlled studies in 723 healthy adults found that intervals beat continuous training by 1.2 mL/kg/min on average, about 3% for a recreational rider, with a larger gap in less fit people and in longer programmes [@milanovic2015].

And there are other routes. In 2024 a Canadian group randomised 84 young adults to six weeks of training in one of four intensity domains, each set from the person's own thresholds [@inglis2024]. Continuous riding in the upper part of the band between the thresholds raised VO₂max by 5.4 mL/kg/min, nearly as much as intervals in the severe domain at 6.2, and all-out sprints, whose long rests leave little time near VO₂max, raised it by 4.7. Riding below the first threshold, matched for work, raised it by 1.8, which was not significantly different from doing nothing.

Closest is not automatically best either. Seiler's 4 × 4 riders worked nearer their ceiling than the 4 × 8 riders and gained half as much [@seiler2013]. In people who are not very fit, almost any hard enough training raises VO₂max, and time near it is one route among several. In trained riders the trials that improved VO₂max mostly used intervals close to it, which is consistent with the idea without proving it. {grade C}

As a yardstick, though, it works. Between two sessions of the same length and effort, the one that keeps you closer to the ceiling for longer is probably the stronger stimulus for the heart and the oxygen system.

## Long intervals

Four to eight minutes is the oldest and best-tested range. The best-known version is the Trondheim group's 4 × 4 minutes, at 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate with three minutes of easy recovery, which has been used in heart patients as well as athletes [@hatle2014].

The largest trial of long intervals in cyclists, published in 2016, put 63 riders through 24 sessions in 12 weeks of 4 × 16, 4 × 8 and 4 × 4 minutes, all as maximal efforts, on top of a large amount of easy riding [@sylta2016]. One group rode four weeks of each, longest first; a second rode them shortest first; a third mixed all three throughout. Every group improved its 40-minute power, peak power and VO₂peak by 5% to 10%, and the order made no difference. Nothing in the evidence says that a block of long intervals must come before a block of short ones, or the reverse. A session a rider looks forward to gets ridden harder, which is a reason for variety, though not evidence that variety itself adds anything.

## 30/15, and the catch

The session called 30/15 is three sets of 13 repetitions of 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, with three minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. That is 19.5 minutes of hard work in about 35 minutes. Fifteen seconds is long enough to decide that the next 30 seconds are impossible, which is about when they start.

In two trials from Bent Rønnestad's group in Norway, it beat effort-matched sessions of 4 × 5 minutes. In 2015, 16 cyclists did two sessions a week for ten weeks among their easy training. VO₂max rose 8.7% in the 30/15 group against 2.6% in the long-interval group, and every performance measure, from 30-second to 40-minute power, moved in favour of the short intervals [@ronnestad2015b]. In 2020 the same comparison in 18 elite cyclists ran for three weeks. VO₂max did not differ, but the 30/15 group's 20-minute power rose 4.7% against a fall of 1.4% [@ronnestad2020].

At matched power, the short format does give more time near VO₂max. In 2024 a Norwegian group had 11 women and 8 men ride 6 × 8 minutes at the same average power, either steady or as 30/15s. The 30/15 version gave 1,123 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 879, and the women responded as the men did [@urianstad2024].

The catch is that riders on the road do not ride at a fixed power. In 2025 twelve well-trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 × 13 × 30/15 and 6 × 5 minutes twice each: once at prescribed powers, and once as hard as they could manage [@appelhans2025]. At prescribed powers the 30/15 session won easily, with 420 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 178. Ridden all-out, both sessions produced far more, 1,130 seconds and 1,016, and the difference was no longer statistically significant. How hard a session is ridden changes its time near VO₂max more than its format does.

Heart rate can't settle the question for a rider without a gas analyser. Twelve highly trained runners spent less time above 90% of VO₂max in 24 × 30 seconds than in 4 × 3 minutes, 201 seconds against 328, but more time above 90% of maximum heart rate, 820 against 545 [@fleckenstein2024]. In short intervals heart rate and oxygen uptake do not move together, and heart rate can make a session look closer to the ceiling than it is. The guide is effort and power.

Both Rønnestad trials are small, both come from the group that designed the session, and both compared it with long intervals paced by the rider, which are hard to pace well. The fairest reading is that 30/15s make a large dose of work near the top easier to reach, a real advantage for anyone who struggles to hold a hard long interval, and that a well-paced long-interval session may do much the same [@ronnestad2015b; @ronnestad2020; @appelhans2025]. {grade B}

The best sessions in the figure gave 15 to 19 minutes above 90% of VO₂max, and they were sizeable: 6 × 8 minutes, 6 × 5 minutes or three sets of 13 × 30/15, each with 20 to 50 minutes of hard work in it [@urianstad2024; @appelhans2025].

## Threshold intervals

Between the long intervals of this chapter and the easy riding of Part II lies the band that Chapter 5 called "between the thresholds", and many amateurs spend much of their training in it. Intervals there, typically 8 to 20 minutes just below the second threshold, are less intense than anything above and easier to recover from. A single session took 24 to 48 hours of the heart's autonomic recovery in the review Chapter 12 describes, against 48 hours or more after harder intervals {shadow @stanley2013}.

In the trials they did less for VO₂max than harder sessions and more for the share of it a rider can sustain. Seiler's 4 × 16-minute group, which settled at a lactate of 4.9 mmol/L, gained half as much as the 4 × 8s [@seiler2013]. The elite skiers in Chapter 12's two-against-four trial did nothing but threshold intervals, 136 minutes a week, and the group that did them in two long sessions raised the share of VO₂max they could hold at threshold from 80.7% to 85.4% and became more economical {shadow @tonnessen2020}.

The pattern fits Chapter 1's model. Threshold work trains the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain, and work near VO₂max trains the ceiling itself. A rider whose VO₂max is already high relative to their threshold has more to gain from the first; one whose ceiling is low has more to gain from the second. For most amateurs on six to ten hours, who have one or two hard sessions to spend, the trials favour giving at least one of them to intervals near the top. {grade C}

## Sprints

Sprint interval training usually means repeated efforts of about 30 seconds, all-out, with three or four minutes of rest. Its appeal is time. In 2006 Martin Gibala's group in Canada gave sixteen active young men six sessions of sprints over two weeks, about two and a half hours of training in all. They gained as much in a time trial and in their muscles' oxidative capacity as men who did about ten and a half hours of continuous riding {shadow @gibala2006}.

For a rider who already trains, the comparison that matters is with long intervals. A 2020 analysis of six trials in moderately trained people found no overall difference in time-trial performance, but intervals of four minutes or more beat sprints by 2%, and raised maximal aerobic power more [@rosenblat2020]. A 2023 trial in 81 well-trained women, running, was starker. After eight weeks at three sessions a week, only 4 × 4 minutes raised VO₂max, by 7.3%; two sprint protocols raised anaerobic capacity instead and improved a 300 m sprint more [@helgerud2023]. And the 10 × 30-second all-out sprints caused eight injuries, six of them hamstring strains, each bad enough to stop training for at least a week. The other two protocols caused none. That trial was on a treadmill, and I found no comparable count of injuries from sprinting on a bike.

Sprints raise VO₂max in untrained people, improve sprinting, and save time. For a trained rider aiming at long efforts they are the weaker choice, and run all-out they are the riskier one. {grade B}

Most readers of this book ride events that last an hour or more, and sprints train something those riders rarely need. A rider who races criteriums, or who wants to win the sprint for the village sign, has a reason to include them that the VO₂max trials do not capture.

## Riding the sessions

Every successful trial in this chapter asked riders for the hardest effort they could sustain for the session. When twelve well-trained runners chose their own pace for repeats of one to six minutes, they rated every session at about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, very hard, whatever the length [@seiler2004]. Start each set at the highest power you believe you can hold for all of it, expect the last repetition to be the hardest, and if you finish with something left, raise the power next time. Long efforts can be made to give more for the same effort by building surges into them, which added time near VO₂max without making the session feel any harder [@bossi2020].

Smart trainers can hold a set power whatever the rider's cadence, and many riders do their intervals that way, which in the sense of the trials makes them fixed-power sessions. In the pacing study above, the 6 × 5-minute session gave three minutes above 90% of VO₂max at the researchers' fixed powers and seventeen when the riders went as hard as they could [@appelhans2025]. Part of that gap may come from the particular powers the researchers chose, but the same thing happens to a rider who sets a power from an old test, or a cautious one. A fixed power is a ceiling on the session as well as a floor.

The trainer's fixed mode makes the efforts repeatable and the recoveries genuinely easy, so the remedy is not to abandon it. It is to set the power from recent efforts, raise it when a session ends with something left, and accept that the last repetition should be very hard. {grade C}

The recoveries between efforts matter less than the efforts. When nine well-trained runners did 6 × 4 minutes as hard as they could with recoveries of one, two or four minutes, the length of the rest made little difference to how fast they ran, how much oxygen they used or how hard it felt. Left to choose, they rested for about two minutes [@seiler2005]. The trials used two and a half to three minutes between long intervals, and a warm-up of about ten minutes [@ronnestad2020; @hatle2014]. I found nothing to suggest that a longer warm-up adds anything.

Most interval trials are of men. The few that compared the sexes found the sessions worked in much the same way, with two exceptions in the women's sprint trial: one sprint protocol that had raised VO₂max in men did not in women, and the all-out sprints injured more women than the same protocol had injured men [@urianstad2024; @helgerud2023]. Chapter 18 returns to women's training.

> **From the saddle.** I ride two interval sessions a week. One is 5 × 4 minutes at 300 W, with 2 minutes' rest between them. The other is 10 × 1 minute at 350 W. I know I'm going to suffer in both. If I'm tired from work I might put a session off, or replace it with a longer Zone 2 ride. Writing this chapter showed me that those sessions hold 20 and 10 minutes of hard work, less than the trials here favour, so I'm changing them.


## The sessions, in brief

These are the sessions the trials in this chapter used, as a rider might ride them. Each assumes a warm-up of about ten minutes of easy riding with a few short pick-ups, and an easy spin afterwards. Chapter 17 puts two of them into a week.

![The five sessions in the list below on one time scale, each between a 10-minute warm-up and cool-down, with the minutes of hard work in each. Drawn for this book: the heights show roughly how hard each part is, not measured power.](figures/session-shapes.svg)

1. **4 × 4 minutes,** with 3 minutes of easy riding between. Ridden as hard as can be sustained for all four, which in the Trondheim trials meant 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate [@hatle2014]. Sixteen minutes of hard work; the shortest of the long sessions.
2. **4 × 8 minutes,** with 2 to 3 minutes between, about what Seiler's runners chose for themselves [@seiler2005]. The session that beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16 in recreational cyclists, ridden at about 90% of peak heart rate [@seiler2013]. Thirty-two minutes of hard work. A rider who finds 4 × 8 comfortable can add a fifth repetition, as the 22 cyclists in the correlation study did [@odden2024].
3. **30/15:** three sets of 13 × 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, 3 minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. The 30-second efforts are harder than a long interval and the 15 seconds are easy pedalling, not rest. Two sets is a reasonable start.
4. **Threshold intervals:** for example 3 × 12 or 2 × 20 minutes just below the second threshold, with 3 minutes between. Less intense, cheaper to recover from, and better for the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain than for VO₂max itself {shadow @tonnessen2020}.
5. **Sprint intervals:** 4 to 6 × 30 seconds all-out with 4 minutes of easy riding between. Useful for sprinting and for a rider short of time; the weaker choice for long efforts [@rosenblat2020].
