# Chapter 11: Designing intervals

Harder intervals are not always better intervals. In 2013 Stephen Seiler's group in Kristiansand tested this in recreational cyclists, much like the readers of this book. For seven weeks, three groups added two interval sessions a week to their easy riding: four efforts of 4, 8 or 16 minutes. A fourth group just rode easy. Nobody was given a target power. Each effort was ridden as hard as the rider could sustain for its length, so the longer the interval, the lower the intensity [@seiler2013].

### One study, up close: four ways to spend two sessions a week

Design: Randomised trial lasting seven weeks. 35 cyclists, 29 men and 6 women, with a VO₂peak of 52 mL/kg/min, who had trained about 6 hours a week, with about one and a half interval sessions, for the two months before. The 4 × 8 group improved twice as much as any other. Its riders worked less intensely than the 4 × 4 group but accumulated twice as many hard minutes [@seiler2013]. Going longer still, to 4 × 16, meant settling at about the second threshold and brought a smaller gain.

For a rider with two hard sessions a week, the finding is clear: doubling the work at a slightly lower intensity did more than squeezing half the work in at the very top. The trial was small, with eight or nine riders in each group, and it lasted seven weeks. But the 4 × 8 advantage didn't come from one measure. That group's gain was the largest in each of the three, and in a ride to exhaustion as well. The 4 × 4 riders rated their sessions much harder than the others did, and the authors could not rule out that they were still tired at the final tests. The easy-only group, who rode about a fifth more than before, gained 4.2%: seven weeks of riding more improves a rider whatever the sessions [@seiler2013].

What works is enough intensity to be near your limit, held for enough minutes to add up to a substantial session. In trained cyclists long intervals have worked well, and so have 30/15s, sets of 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy [@seiler2013; @ronnestad2015b]. Pick one or two formats you will ride hard, pace each set so you can finish it, and progress by adding work at that effort. {grade B}

## What a hard session does: time near VO₂max

Interval sessions are usually compared by how long they keep a rider near VO₂max. Close to it, the heart is pumping as much blood as it can, and nearly every muscle fibre in the legs is working, the fast fibres as well as the slow. The usual argument is that this is the strongest signal the heart and the muscles receive. In a 2007 trial from Trondheim, two interval programmes raised the amount of blood the heart pumped per beat by about 10% [@helgerud2007]. In a 2017 Canadian study, ten active men trained one leg with intervals and the other with continuous cycling, matched for work. A marker of mitochondrial content rose further in the interval leg {shadow @macinnis2017}. Most studies count the minutes above 90% of VO₂max, though the line is a convention.

The idea comes from one experiment. In 2000 Véronique Billat's group put eight long-distance runners on a 400 m track with portable gas analysers and had them run two hard sessions [@billat2000]. In one they alternated 30 seconds at the speed that just brought out their VO₂max with 30 seconds at half that speed. In the other they ran without stopping, at a speed halfway between their threshold and that top speed. Both sessions left about the same lactate in the blood. The stop-start session kept the runners at VO₂max for 7 minutes 51 seconds, the continuous one for 2 minutes 42.

The reason lies in how slowly oxygen uptake moves. It does not jump to its new level when hard work starts. It rises over a minute or two, so a short effort ends before the body's oxygen use has caught up with the demand. Above critical power (Chapters 1 and 5) it then keeps creeping upward until it reaches VO₂max or the rider stops. That is how Billat's continuous runners got there without running at their top speed. Oxygen uptake also falls slowly when the effort stops. Fifteen seconds of easy pedalling is too short for it to drop far, so a session of 30 seconds hard and 15 easy holds it high through the recoveries as well as the efforts {shadow @midgley2006}.

Twenty-five years of interval research has been organised around Billat's result. A 2006 review of the running studies turned it into a recipe of efforts and recoveries lasting 15 to 30 seconds each. It also asked for more experiments on what such sessions build before competitive runners were advised to use them {shadow @midgley2006}.

### Reading the research: a correlation inside a trial

Nobody has shown that time near VO₂max is what produces the adaptation, rather than something that goes with it, such as the total amount of hard work. Until 2024 nobody had even measured oxygen uptake in every session of a training programme and then asked whether it predicted the result [@odden2024].

::: trial
Aim: To find out whether the share of VO₂max that cyclists reached during their interval sessions was related to how much they improved.
Design: One training programme, not a comparison of programmes. 22 well-trained cyclists (19 men, 3 women; VO₂max 67.1 mL/kg/min) rode 21 sessions of 5 × 8 minutes at their best 40-minute power over nine weeks, on smart trainers, with oxygen uptake measured through every work interval. The main analysis was a regression across all 22; the authors also split the riders into halves by the average share of VO₂max they reached [@odden2024].
| | Higher half *(11 riders)* | Lower half *(11 riders)*
| Average share of VO₂max in the work intervals | 86.2% | 79.9%
| VO₂max at the start | 65.1 mL/kg/min | 69.1 mL/kg/min
| Training a week during the study | 9 h 43 min | 7 h 40 min
| In the lowest zone, below 55% of 40-minute power | 3 h 12 min | 1 h 48 min
~ The difference in lowest-zone training was statistically significant.
| In the two hardest zones | 1 h 45 min | 1 h 59 min
-- After nine weeks
| VO₂max | Up 5.2 mL/kg/min | Up 1.8 mL/kg/min
| Peak power in the VO₂max test | Up 0.51 W/kg | Up 0.23 W/kg
| Power at a lactate of 4 mmol/L | Up 0.31 W/kg | Up 0.12 W/kg
| Best 15-minute power | Up 0.30 W/kg | Up 0.15 W/kg
~ The 15-minute difference was a tendency, not statistically significant.
Authors' conclusion: Improvements in endurance were positively related to the share of VO₂max reached during interval training, and that share was the measure that best reflected how much each rider adapted.
:::

VO₂max was measured during the programme as well as before and after, so each session was compared with a current figure. The share of VO₂max proved more repeatable from session to session than the share of maximum heart rate [@odden2024].

It cannot show that reaching a higher share caused the bigger improvements, and the table shows why. Every rider rode the same session at the same share of their own 40-minute power. Whether a rider reached 86% of VO₂max or 80% was therefore a property of the rider, not of the session. The two halves were different groups of people before the training began, and they trained differently while it ran. The higher half rode two hours a week more, most of it in the lowest zone, and it started with a lower VO₂max, which leaves more room to improve. The authors acknowledge that the extra volume "could have affected the results", and judge that the share of VO₂max was "most likely" responsible [@odden2024]. That judgement is reasonable, but it is a judgement, not a result.

Despite taking place inside a training programme, this was an observational comparison: the riders who did better also differed in other ways. Testing the cause would require random assignment to sessions that differ only in how close to VO₂max they keep riders. I found no such trial. {grade C} The practical case rests on the randomised comparisons in this chapter, which favour accumulating work close to the top of what a rider can sustain.

## Other routes to a bigger engine

Comparisons of whole programmes support a narrower claim: that intervals raise VO₂max more than continuous training, by a modest margin. A 2015 analysis pooled 28 controlled studies in 723 healthy adults [@milanovic2015]. Intervals beat continuous training by 1.2 mL/kg/min on average, about 3% for a recreational rider, with a larger gap in less fit people and in longer programmes.

Intervals are not the only route. In 2024 a Canadian group randomised 84 young adults to six weeks of training in one of four intensity domains, each set from the person's own thresholds [@inglis2024]. Continuous riding in the upper part of the band between the thresholds raised VO₂max by 5.4 mL/kg/min. That was nearly as much as intervals in the severe domain, at 6.2. All-out sprints, whose long rests leave little time near VO₂max, raised it by 4.7. Riding below the first threshold, matched for work, raised it by 1.8, not significantly different from doing nothing.

Closest is not automatically best either. Seiler's 4 × 4 riders worked nearer their ceiling than the 4 × 8 riders and gained half as much [@seiler2013]. In people who are not very fit, almost any hard enough training raises VO₂max, and time near it is one route among several. In trained riders the trials that improved VO₂max mostly used intervals close to it, which is consistent with the idea without proving it. {grade C}

As a yardstick, though, it works. Between two sessions of the same length and effort, the one that keeps you closer to the ceiling for longer is probably the stronger stimulus for the heart and the oxygen system.

## Long intervals

Four to eight minutes is the oldest and best-tested range. The best-known version is the Trondheim group's 4 × 4 minutes, at 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate with three minutes of easy recovery. It has been used in heart patients as well as athletes [@hatle2014].

Ride the different lengths in whatever order suits you. The largest trial of long intervals in cyclists, published in 2016, gave 63 riders 24 sessions over 12 weeks [@sylta2016]. They rode 4 × 16, 4 × 8 and 4 × 4 minutes, all as maximal efforts, on top of a large amount of easy riding. One group rode four weeks of each, longest first; a second rode them shortest first; a third mixed all three throughout. Every group improved its 40-minute power, peak power and VO₂peak by 5% to 10%, and the order made no difference.

## 30/15, and the catch

The session called 30/15 is three sets of 13 repetitions of 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, with three minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. That is 19.5 minutes of hard work in about 35 minutes. Fifteen seconds is long enough to decide that the next 30 seconds are impossible, which is about when they start.

In trained cyclists it has done at least as well as long intervals. In 2015 Bent Rønnestad's group in Norway had 16 cyclists add two hard sessions a week to their easy training for ten weeks. One group rode 30/15s, the other 4 × 5 minutes, each as hard as they could sustain. VO₂max rose 8.7% in the 30/15 group against 2.6% in the long-interval group. Every performance measure, from 30-second to 40-minute power, moved in favour of the short intervals [@ronnestad2015b]. A three-week version in 18 elite cyclists found no difference in VO₂max, but 20-minute power rose 4.7% with the 30/15s and fell 1.4% with the long intervals [@ronnestad2020].

At the same average power, the short format keeps a rider near VO₂max for longer. In 2024 a Norwegian group had 11 women and 8 men ride 6 × 8 minutes at the same average power, either steady or as 30/15s. The 30/15 version gave 1,123 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 879, and the women responded as the men did [@urianstad2024].

In four of them the short or varied format gave more: 6.8 against 4.8 minutes, 18.7 against 14.7, 7.0 against 3.0, and 18.8 against 16.9 when both sessions were ridden all-out, a difference that was not statistically significant. The catch is that riders on the road do not ride at a fixed power. In 2025 twelve well-trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 × 13 × 30/15 and 6 × 5 minutes twice each: once at powers the researchers set, and once as hard as they could manage [@appelhans2025]. At the set powers the 30/15 session won easily, with 420 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 178. Ridden all-out, both sessions gave far more, 1,130 seconds and 1,016, and the difference was no longer statistically significant. How hard a session is ridden changes its time near VO₂max more than its format does.

So the Rønnestad trials support a narrower claim than their headline numbers. Both are small, both come from the group that designed the session, and both compared it with long intervals paced by the riders, which are hard to pace well. Thirty-fifteens make a large dose of work near the top easier to reach. That is a real advantage for anyone who struggles to hold a hard long interval, but a well-paced long-interval session may do much the same [@ronnestad2015b; @appelhans2025]. {grade B}

Whatever the format, the sessions that kept riders near VO₂max longest were big ones. The best in the figure gave 15 to 19 minutes above 90% of VO₂max. They were 6 × 8 minutes, 6 × 5 minutes and three sets of 13 × 30/15, each with 20 to 50 minutes of hard work in it [@urianstad2024; @appelhans2025].

A large 2026 review agrees with the figure, although at first sight it seems not to. Across 239 sessions in 86 studies, efforts of two minutes or more gave 555 seconds above 90% of VO₂max on average, against 336 for efforts of 30 seconds or less [@schoenmakers2026]. But the review counted a set of 30/15s as short intervals, and its authors point out that a set behaves more like one long effort. Its long-interval sessions also held about twice as much hard work. And where studies compared steady and varied power within the same session, the 30/15 comparisons above among them, varied power gave 165 seconds more. Short efforts strung together with short recoveries behave like a long interval, and varying the power inside an effort adds time near the ceiling rather than costing it. {grade C}

## Threshold intervals

Threshold intervals sit in the band Chapter 5 called "between the thresholds", where many amateurs spend much of their training. They typically last 8 to 20 minutes, just below the second threshold, and are easier to recover from than anything harder. In the review Chapter 12 describes, the heart's nervous control took 24 to 48 hours to recover from a single session, against 48 hours or more after harder intervals {shadow @stanley2013}.

Their strength is the share of VO₂max a rider can sustain. In 2025 a Norwegian group gave 22 young, well-trained cyclists a week of six threshold-interval sessions, and two months later a week of five sessions of 30/15s. Both weeks raised 15-minute power by similar amounts. But the threshold week raised power at a lactate of 4 mmol/L about twice as much, 4.5% against 2.1%. Only the threshold week raised the share of VO₂max the riders could hold there. VO₂max rose a little after both weeks, with no significant difference between them. Every rider did the threshold week first, so the comparison was not randomised [@ronnestad2025].

Seiler's cyclists showed no such advantage. His 4 × 16-minute group settled at a lactate of 4.9 mmol/L. It gained about as much as the 4 × 4s, in VO₂peak and in threshold power alike, and half as much as the 4 × 8s [@seiler2013].

The pattern fits Chapter 1's model. Threshold work trains the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain, and work near VO₂max trains the ceiling itself. A rider whose VO₂max is already high relative to their threshold has more to gain from the first; one whose ceiling is low has more to gain from the second. For most amateurs on six to ten hours, who have one or two hard sessions to spend, the trials favour giving at least one of them to intervals near the top. {grade C}

## Sprints

Sprint interval training usually means repeated efforts of about 30 seconds, all-out, with three or four minutes of rest. Its appeal is time. In 2006 Martin Gibala's group in Canada gave sixteen active young men six sessions of sprints over two weeks, about two and a half hours of training in all. They gained as much in a time trial and in their muscles' oxidative capacity as men who did about ten and a half hours of continuous riding {shadow @gibala2006}.

For a rider who already trains, sprints come off worse than long intervals. A 2020 analysis of six trials in moderately trained people found no overall difference in time-trial performance. But intervals of four minutes or more beat sprints by 2%, and raised maximal aerobic power more [@rosenblat2020]. A 2023 trial in 81 well-trained women, running, was starker. After eight weeks of three sessions a week, only 4 × 4 minutes raised VO₂max, by 7.3%. Two sprint protocols raised anaerobic capacity instead, and improved a 300 m sprint more [@helgerud2023]. The 10 × 30-second all-out sprints also caused eight injuries, six of them hamstring strains, each bad enough to stop training for at least a week. The other two protocols caused none. That trial was on a treadmill, and I found no comparable count of injuries from sprinting on a bike.

Sprints raise VO₂max in untrained people, improve sprinting, and save time. For a trained rider aiming at long efforts they are the weaker choice. {grade B}

Most readers of this book ride events that last an hour or more, and sprints train something those riders rarely need. Criterium racers have a reason to include them that the VO₂max trials do not capture, and so does anyone who wants to win the sprint for the village sign.

## Riding the sessions

For self-paced sessions, aim for the hardest effort you can sustain across the whole set. When twelve well-trained runners chose their own pace for repeats of one to six minutes, they rated every session at about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, very hard, whatever the length [@seiler2004]. Expect the last repetition to be the hardest, and if you finish with something left, raise the power next time. Building surges into long efforts added time near VO₂max without making the session feel any harder [@bossi2020].

On a smart trainer, set the power from recent efforts and raise it when you finish with something left. Smart trainers can hold a set power whatever the rider's cadence, and many riders do their intervals that way. In the terms of the trials, those are fixed-power sessions. In the pacing study above, the 6 × 5-minute session gave three minutes above 90% of VO₂max at the researchers' powers. Ridden as hard as the riders could manage, it gave seventeen [@appelhans2025]. Part of that gap may come from the particular powers the researchers chose, but the same thing happens to a rider who sets a power from an old test, or a cautious one. A fixed power is a ceiling on the session as well as a floor. {grade C}

Judge short intervals by power and effort, not heart rate. Twelve highly trained runners spent less time above 90% of VO₂max in 24 × 30 seconds than in 4 × 3 minutes, 201 seconds against 328 [@fleckenstein2024]. Yet they spent more time above 90% of maximum heart rate, 820 seconds against 545. In short intervals heart rate and oxygen uptake do not move together, and heart rate can make a session look closer to the ceiling than it is.

The recoveries between efforts matter less than the efforts. Nine well-trained runners did 6 × 4 minutes as hard as they could, with recoveries of one, two or four minutes. The length of the rest made little difference to how fast they ran, how much oxygen they used or how hard it felt. Left to choose, they rested for about two minutes [@seiler2005]. The trials used two and a half to three minutes between long intervals, and a warm-up of about ten minutes [@ronnestad2020; @hatle2014]. I found nothing to suggest that a longer warm-up adds anything.

Most interval trials are of men. The few that compared the sexes found the sessions worked in much the same way. The exceptions came in the women's sprint trial. One sprint protocol that had raised VO₂max in men did not in women, and the all-out sprints injured more women than the same protocol had injured men [@urianstad2024; @helgerud2023]. Chapter 18 returns to women's training.

> **From the saddle.** I ride two interval sessions a week. One is 5 × 4 minutes at 300 W, with 2 minutes' rest between them. The other is 10 × 1 minute at 350 W. I know I'm going to suffer in both. If I'm tired from work I might put a session off, or replace it with a longer Zone 2 ride. Writing this chapter showed me that those sessions hold 20 and 10 minutes of hard work, less than the trials here favour, so I'm changing them.


## The sessions, in brief

These are the sessions the trials in this chapter used, as a rider might ride them. Each assumes a warm-up of about ten minutes of easy riding with a few short pick-ups, and an easy spin afterwards. Chapter 17 puts two of them into a week.

![The five sessions in the list below on one time scale, each between a 10-minute warm-up and cool-down, with the minutes of hard work in each. Drawn for this book: the heights show roughly how hard each part is, not measured power.](figures/session-shapes.svg)

1. **4 × 4 minutes,** with 3 minutes of easy riding between. Ridden as hard as can be sustained for all four, which in the Trondheim trials meant 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate [@hatle2014]. Sixteen minutes of hard work; the shortest of the long sessions.
2. **4 × 8 minutes,** with 2 to 3 minutes between, about what Seiler's runners chose for themselves [@seiler2005]. The session that beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16 in recreational cyclists, ridden at about 90% of peak heart rate [@seiler2013]. Thirty-two minutes of hard work. A rider who finds 4 × 8 comfortable can add a fifth repetition, as the 22 cyclists in the correlation study did [@odden2024].
3. **30/15:** three sets of 13 × 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, 3 minutes between sets [@ronnestad2020]. The 30-second efforts are harder than a long interval and the 15 seconds are easy pedalling, not rest. Two sets is a reasonable start.
4. **Threshold intervals:** for example 3 × 12 or 2 × 20 minutes just below the second threshold, with 3 minutes between, ridden at about 14 to 15 on the 6-to-20 scale: hard, not very hard. In the 2025 trial that effort put the riders at about 85% of maximum heart rate and a lactate of about 3 mmol/L [@ronnestad2025]. Less intense, cheaper to recover from, and better for the fraction of VO₂max a rider can sustain than for VO₂max itself.
5. **Sprint intervals:** 4 to 6 × 30 seconds all-out with 4 minutes of easy riding between. Useful for sprinting and for a rider short of time; the weaker choice for long efforts [@rosenblat2020].
