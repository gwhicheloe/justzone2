## Part III's argument, in one place (the same paragraph opens all three briefs)

- **Chapter 11:** time near VO₂max is the best working explanation for what a hard session does and the best yardstick for comparing sessions, but it is not shown to be the cause of the gains; pacing changes it as much as format does.
- **Chapter 12:** no single session is best; in trained cyclists, sessions that pile up the most work near the top (4 to 8 minutes, or 30/15s) beat shorter-and-harder and longer-and-easier ones; long intervals beat sprints for time trials; order does not matter.
- **Chapter 13:** two hard sessions a week, at least 48 hours apart, is the best-supported default; one maintains; three only as a short block; the dose that works in trials (about 40 to 65 minutes of hard work a week) is close to the champions' 1.3 hours, which suggests recovery sets the ceiling.
- Together they answer Chapter 8's open question (is the champions' hour a ceiling?) and fit Chapter 10's conclusion (for a rider on six to ten hours, the easy/hard split is a performance question).

## The question

Why does time spent close to VO₂max keep coming up in interval research, and does it explain why intervals work?

## The argument (to approve)

1. **The position, stated on the first page:** time near VO₂max is the best working explanation and the best yardstick for comparing sessions on the day; it has not been shown to cause the gains; use it to design and judge hard sessions, not as the only thing a session does. **My reading.**
2. History: intervals came first (Zátopek; Reindell and Roskamm); portable gas analysers made it measurable (Billat 2000); the 2006 review's rules described sessions, not outcomes.
3. Mechanism: heart and fibre recruitment near VO₂max (stroke volume +10% after intervals, Helgerud 2007; one-leg study, MacInnis 2017). Not shown to be the cause.
4. Short intervals keep you there at fixed power (Rønnestad & Hansen 2016; Urianstad 2024; Bossi 2020), shown in the figure.
5. The catch is pacing: all-out, 30/15 and 6 × 5 are level (Appelhans 2025); in runners the short format loses and heart rate misleads (Fleckenstein 2024); RPE about 17 whatever the length (Seiler & Sjursen 2004). Grade B.
6. **Close-up: Odden 2024**, laid out as a table of the paper's two halves, which shows that the half with the higher share of VO₂max also trained two hours a week more and started lower.
7. **Reading the research: a correlation inside a trial.** Splitting riders afterwards by something they did turns a trial into an observational study. Grade C for the causal claim.
8. Responders and doses: Bacon 2013 (Joyner an author): under-dosing explains much non-response; ties to Chapter 2's HERITAGE and Montero & Lundby.
9. Training trials: intervals beat continuous training modestly (Milanović 2015: +1.2 mL/kg/min; Bacon 2013; Helgerud 2007), grade B; but other routes work in the less trained (Inglis 2024: continuous riding between the thresholds +5.4 vs HIIT +6.2; sprints, Gist 2014), and closest is not best (Seiler 2013). Grade C on the trained-rider claim.
10. Practical: compare sessions by it; judge by power and effort, not heart rate; a hard session should hold about half an hour of hard work.
