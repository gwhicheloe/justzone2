## 1. What a session is made of
- Buchheit & Laursen 2013 [Abs]: up to nine variables: work intensity and duration, relief intensity and duration, exercise mode, repetitions, series, and the duration and intensity of recovery between series.
- Billat 2001 [Abs]: interval training described by Reindell and Roskamm, popularised by Zátopek in the 1950s.
- Billat 2001 [Abs]: coaches set interval speeds from race paces, not from physiological markers. ## 2. Long intervals: 4 × 4, 4 × 8 and longer

### Seiler, Jøranson, Olesen & Hetlelid 2013, Scand J Med Sci Sports [FT since 25 October 2026; abstract before] — the close-up
- Aim (abstract): "To compare the effects of three 7-week interval training programs varying in work period duration but matched for effort in trained recreational cyclists." **No hypothesis stated in the abstract**; the table says "Aim".
- 35 cyclists (29 men, 6 women), VO₂peak 52 ± 6 mL/kg/min; the previous two months about **6 h a week with about 1.5 interval sessions a week**.
- Randomised to four groups. Low only (n = 8): 4 to 6 sessions a week at low intensity. Three interval groups (n = 9 each): two sessions a week for 7 weeks of 4 × 4, 4 × 8 or 4 × 16 min, plus 2 to 3 low-intensity sessions a week.
- Intervals "prescribed at the maximal tolerable intensity" (effort-matched). Performed at **94 ± 2, 90 ± 2 and 88 ± 2% of HRpeak** and **13.2, 9.6 and 4.9 mmol/L** lactate in 4 × 4, 4 × 8, 4 × 16 respectively.
- Gains, "overall" in VO₂peak, power at VO₂peak and power at 4 mmol/L (mean, 95% CI): **4 × 8: 11.4% (8.0 to 14.9)**; Low 4.2% (0.4 to 8.0); 4 × 16 5.6% (2.1 to 9.1); 4 × 4 5.5% (2.0 to 9.0). P < 0.02 for 4 × 8 against every other group.
- Authors: "Accumulating 32 min of work at 90% HR max induces greater adaptive gains than accumulating 16 min of work at ~95% HR max despite lower RPE."
- [Own] Work per session: 16, 32 and 64 min. Recovery durations are not in the abstract and are not stated in the draft. The "overall" figure appears to combine three measures; the abstract does not give each one.

### Sylta et al. 2016, Med Sci Sports Exerc [Abs]
- 63 cyclists (VO₂peak 61.3), 12 weeks, 24 interval sessions, high volume of low-intensity training. Increasing group (n = 23): 4 × 16 (weeks 1 to 4), 4 × 8 (5 to 8), 4 × 4 (9 to 12); decreasing (n = 20) the reverse; mixed (n = 20). Sessions as maximal session efforts: lactate **4.7, 9.2 and 12.7 mmol/L** in 4 × 16, 4 × 8, 4 × 4.
- All groups improved **5% to 10%** in 40-min power, peak power and VO₂peak; **no differences between groups**; similar likelihood of large, moderate or non-response in each.

### Helgerud et al. 2007 [Abs]
- 40 moderately trained men; 3 days a week for 8 weeks, four groups matched for work: long slow 70% HRmax, lactate threshold 85%, 15/15, 4 × 4 min. VO₂max +5.5% (15/15) and +7.2% (4 × 4), more than the continuous groups; stroke volume about +10% after intervals. Paywalled; abstract only.

### Helgerud et al. 2023, Scand J Med Sci Sports [FT] (running, women)
- 81 well-trained women (22 years, VO₂max 51.8), three times a week for 8 weeks: 4 × 4 min at ~95% of maximal aerobic speed with 3 min active recovery; 8 × 20 s at ~150% with 10 s passive recovery; 10 × 30 s at ~175% with 3.5 min active recovery. 49 analysed (15, 20, 14); ≤80% compliance excluded.
- Only 4 × 4 raised VO₂max: **+7.3%**; anaerobic capacity rose after both sprint protocols (+6.5% and +14.4%); all groups improved 3,000 m and 300 m; the sprint protocols improved 300 m more.
- **10 × 30 s all-out caused eight injuries: six hamstring strains, one calf strain and one fall at high speed**; none in the other groups. All eight could not train for at least 7 days.
- Two authors affiliated with a commercial training clinic (sources.yaml notes).

### Stepto et al. 1999 [Abs]
- 20 male endurance cyclists; 6 sessions over 3 weeks on top of base training; five session types: 12 × 30 s at 175% of peak power, 12 × 60 s at 100%, 12 × 2 min at 90%, 8 × 4 min at 85%, 4 × 8 min at 80%.
- Predicted 40-km time-trial gains greatest for 8 × 4 min at 85% (**2.8%**, 1.3 to 4.3) and 12 × 30 s at 175% (**2.4%**, 0.7 to 4.0); 100% and 80% not significant.
- Authors: work bouts close to race pace enhance 1-h performance; much higher intensities "also appear to improve performance, possibly by a different mechanism".

### Lindsay et al. 1996 [Abs]
- 8 competitive cyclists, about 300 km a week; replaced 15% of it with 6 sessions over 4 weeks of 6 to 8 × 5 min at 80% of peak power, 60 s recovery.
- 40-km time trial 56.4 → 54.4 min; peak power 416 → 434 W; time-trial power 301 → 326 W.

### Driller et al. 2009 [Abs]
- 10 well-trained rowers (5 women), crossover, 4 weeks, 7 sessions of 8 × 2.5 min at 90% of the speed at VO₂peak vs continuous training at 2 to 3 mmol/L: HIT improved 2,000 m time 1.9%, power 5.8%, relative VO₂peak 7.0% more than continuous training.

## 3. Short intervals: 30/15

### Rønnestad et al. 2015, Scand J Med Sci Sports [Abs]
- 16 cyclists (SI 9, LI 7), 10 weeks, two interval sessions a week between low-intensity training; groups matched for volume of both. Effort-matched short vs long intervals.
- VO₂max **+8.7% ± 5.0% vs +2.6% ± 5.2%** (P ≤ 0.05). Effect sizes for all measures including 30-s, 5-min and 40-min power: moderate to large (0.86 to 1.54) in favour of SI.
- The sessions (from the 2020 abstract, which calls them "the present SI protocol"; George's article gives the same for 2015 [Rep]): 3 series of 13 × 30 s work / 15 s recovery, 3 min between series; long: 4 × 5 min with 2.5 min recovery.

### Rønnestad, Hansen, Nygaard & Lundby 2020 [Abs]
- 18 elite cyclists (VO₂max 73), 3 weeks, 3 sessions a week (9 in all); same SI and LI sessions, effort-matched by perceived effort.
- Peak aerobic power **+3.7% vs −0.3%**; fraction of VO₂max at 4 mmol/L +3.0 vs −3.5 points; power at 4 mmol/L +2.0% vs −2.8%; **20-min power +4.7% vs −1.4%** (P < 0.01); no group difference in VO₂max.

### Appelhans 2025, Urianstad 2024, Bossi 2020 — see Chapter 11's notes.
- [Own] The 30/15 trials compared it with long intervals ridden at a self-chosen even effort; Appelhans 2025 found that when both are ridden all-out, time near VO₂max no longer differs. So part of the 30/15 advantage may be that long intervals are hard to pace well.

## 4. Sprint intervals
- **Gist et al. 2014** [Abs]: 16 RCTs, 318 young people: VO₂max ~+3.6 mL/kg/min (~8%), not different from continuous endurance training.
- **Rosenblat, Perrotta & Thomas 2020** [Abs]: 6 trials comparing HIIT (between VT2 and VO₂max) with SIT (above VO₂max) in moderately trained people: time trial no overall difference (0.9%; 90% CI −1.2 to 1.9); **long HIIT (≥4 min) 2% better than SIT**; no difference in VO₂max; moderate effect (0.70) for HIIT in maximal aerobic power.
- **Yang, Wang & Guan 2025** [FT, parts]: 51 studies, 1,261 athletes (many team-sport and running studies). Against conventional training: repeated sprint training g = 1.04, HIIT 1.01, SIT 0.69, continuous 0.29 (not significant); no significant differences among RST, HIIT and SIT. Meta-regression: inverted U for HIIT, peak at **140 s of work** and work:recovery 0.85; SIT not significant with recovery over 97 s. Mostly running-based.
- **Gibala et al. 2006** [Abs, in sources]: 16 active young men, 6 sessions over 14 days: ~2.5 h sprint intervals vs ~10.5 h endurance; similar time-trial and muscle oxidative gains.
- **MacInnis et al. 2017** [Abs, in sources]: 10 active men, one leg intervals and the other continuous, work matched: citrate synthase higher in the interval leg (10.2 vs 8.4).
- Helgerud 2023 above: sprints raised anaerobic capacity, not VO₂max, in well-trained women, and the 30-s running sprints caused injuries.

## 5. Pacing and intensity
- **Seiler & Sjursen 2004** [Abs]: self-paced runners: RPE ~17 whatever the interval length; 4-min bouts gave the highest VO₂ in six of twelve; "3-5 min" optimal for a high physiological load.
- **Bossi et al. 2020** [Abs]: varying power within each work interval gave more time above 90% VO₂max (410 vs 286 s) with no higher perceived effort.
- **Inglis 2024** [Abs]: continuous riding in the upper heavy domain (between the thresholds) raised VO₂max +5.4 against +6.2 for HIIT in untrained young adults (Chapter 11).
- [Own] Practical rule for a rider: the highest power that can be held for the whole set, which in the trials meant an RPE of about 17; for 30/15, the website's rule from Rønnestad's interview (the 30 s at about 5-min power, the 15 s at about half) is from a video and is not used as a source.

## 6. Order and variety
- Sylta 2016 above: order of long, medium and short intervals over 12 weeks made no difference.
- [Own] Variety is defensible on grounds of tolerance and specificity, not on evidence that it adds adaptation.

## Newer papers on interval length and effort matching (searched 25 October 2026, George: "check if there are any more, more recent papers on this topic")

Searched: OpenAlex for everything citing Seiler 2013 since 2018 (119 works), Europe PMC (effort-matched / isoeffort intervals; 4 × 8 in cyclists; long against short intervals in cyclists; interval-training meta-analyses in trained athletes), and two web searches. **No repeat of Seiler's design (effort-matched 4 × 4 / 4 × 8 / 4 × 16) was found.** All below are abstracts only unless marked; none is in the draft yet.

- **Rosenblat, Arnold, Nelson, Watt & Seiler 2024**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01924-y [Abs; paywalled]: 14 studies, 2–12 weeks, 171 recreational and 128 competitive endurance athletes. Adding training above the maximal metabolic steady state to training below it gave **2.5 mL/kg/min (95% CI 1.4 to 3.6) more VO₂peak**, I² = 0%; no clear effect on peak power or time trial, "in part due to a smaller sample size"; 81 per group needed for adequate power. The best current support for the Threshold intervals paragraph's "did less for VO₂max than harder sessions" (which now rests on Helgerud 2007).
- **Rosenblat, Lin, da Costa & Thomas 2021**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01457-2 [Abs; paywalled]: 29 studies, 67 groups; trained people gained about 2% in time trials, less-trained up to 6%; with HIIT the time-trial change "depended on the duration but not the intensity of the interval work-bout"; dose-response with sessions, weeks and total work. The abstract doesn't say which direction duration works in: full text needed before using. (Norte 2024's full text reads it as longer work bouts giving bigger time-trial gains in trained people; secondhand.)
- **Norte, Steele & Wright 2024**, Int J Strength Cond, doi:10.47206/ijsc.v4i1.271 (the PDF's own header says 2023 and v3i1; the v3i1 DOI does not resolve, v4i1 does) [**FT 25 Oct 2026**, LJMU repository copy, CC BY; text in papers/norte2024.txt]: trained cyclists (VO₂max ≥ 45), 2–3 interval sessions a week; intervals against low/moderate continuous training SMD 0.33 (0.06 to 0.60) overall, most outcomes very imprecise; the network meta-analysis "did not reveal a clear superior effect of any HIIT/SIT types when directly comparing interval training differing in interval work-bout duration". A small journal; exploratory by its own title.
  - Full text: "conducted as part of an undergraduate dissertation", not pre-registered. 14 studies, 302 cyclists; 13 in the meta-analysis. GRADE low or very low for every outcome except one rated moderate.
  - Against easy/moderate continuous training: relative VO₂max **trivial**, 0.10 (−0.34 to 0.54); absolute VO₂max 0.28 (0.15 to 0.40); time trial/time to exhaustion 0.96 (−0.81 to 2.73), I² 71.7%, GRADE very low.
  - Categories: short HIIT < 4 min, long HIIT ≥ 4 min, SIT 20–30 s all-out. But its Table 1 puts Seiler 2013's 4 × 4 group under "Short HIIT", against its own rule; 4 × 8 and 4 × 16 are both "Long HIIT". The network model pools every outcome in each study, which the authors say may explain the lack of differences. So it is weak evidence on 4 × 8 against 4 × 4, and not a basis for tempering the Seiler claim.
  - Useful asides: only two included studies (Seiler 2013, Stepto 1999) used work bouts of 8 minutes or more near the heavy/severe boundary; "the vast majority" were in the severe or extreme domains. Seiler's easy group's VO₂max rose 3.4% (matches our Table 3 figure).
  - Secondhand on Rosenblat 2021: Norte reads it as finding that "intervals of longer duration" gave "greater increases in TT performance among trained individuals". Direction now stated by a citing paper; still not verified in Rosenblat's full text.
  - **Verdict: nothing for the draft.** Withdraws my 25 Oct suggestion to cite it for "no clear winner by interval length".
- **Wiesinger … Hopkins 2025 (online 2024)**, Front Physiol, doi:10.3389/fphys.2024.1486526, PMC11739151 [**FT 25 Oct 2026**, Europe PMC; text in papers/wiesinger2025.txt]: 34 studies in highly trained and elite athletes; "more aerobic types of HIIT" gave +2.6% more VO₂max; largest single effect 12.6% for female endurance athletes after 6 weeks of "aerobic traditional long intervals".
  - Full text corrections to the abstract reading: the **2.6%** is the predicted VO₂max difference between type 1 (long aerobic intervals, 2–10 min) and type 4 (speed-endurance production, 10–75 s near all-out with long rests), with type of HIIT coded as a straight line from 1 to 6. It is aerobic against anaerobic intervals, **not** long against short aerobic intervals (30/15 is type 2). The **12.6%** is aerobic/anaerobic **threshold** in female endurance athletes, not VO₂max.
  - Literature search **18 February 2021**, so the evidence is older than the 2025 date suggests. Includes team-sport and racket athletes as well as endurance athletes. HIIT had to be ≥ 90% HRmax or VO₂max, or all-out.
  - VO₂max, male endurance athletes, 5 weeks of long-interval HIIT against control: **+7.7%** (4.9 to 10.6), control +0.1%. Better with a lower starting VO₂max (−1.8% per +20%). Type of HIIT made no clear difference to time trials (0.4%, −1.8 to 2.6).
  - **Time trials, male endurance athletes (Table 9):** at 5 weeks HIIT beat control by 2.8% (25-min test) and 5.3% (45-min test). At 9 weeks the model predicts control groups had caught up: HIIT 3.7%/5.1%, control 4.0%/2.9%, differences −0.3% (−6.4 to 6.2) and 2.1% (−4.8 to 9.6), both unclear. Authors: "a tendency (albeit mostly unclear) … toward control groups 'catching up' with the HIIT groups over longer durations." Model predictions with very wide ranges; interesting for the book's question, too weak to state.
  - Authors on individual settings: "control training could be equally effective, if not better, in other individual settings. We, therefore, cannot recommend HIIT unprovisionally".
  - HIIT in-season gave more VO₂max than off-season (+4.3% against control); HIIT added on top of training gave a little more VO₂max than HIIT replacing training (1.1%).
  - Statistics: Hopkins' probabilistic magnitude method ("likely", "very likely"), which some statisticians have criticised. Two authors employed by the Red Bull Athlete Performance Center. Their publication-bias simulation used "the mean sample size (Seiler et al., 2013)".

- **Mølmen … Rønnestad 2025**, Med Sci Sports Exerc, doi:10.1249/mss.0000000000003706 [Abs; paywalled]: 30 well-trained male cyclists (VO₂max 70.5), randomised crossover; six moderate-intensity interval sessions in 7 days (5–7 × 10–14 min at RPE 14–15, 85% HRmax, 2.8 mmol/L) then 6 days' recovery, against regular training: power at 4 mmol/L +4.0% vs −1.3%, power at VO₂max +2.5% vs −0.7%, VO₂max +2.0% vs 0.0% (P = 0.05); 15-min power +3.9% vs +0.2% (n.s.).
- **Rønnestad … Mølmen 2025**, Eur J Sport Sci, doi:10.1002/ejsc.70067 [**FT 25 Oct 2026**, PMC12575440 via Europe PMC; text in papers/ronnestad2025.txt]: 22 well-trained cyclists, crossover, a one-week block of moderate-intensity intervals (as above) against one of high-intensity intervals (5 × 8.75 min of short intervals, RPE 17.1): 15-min power +4.9% and +2.8% (no difference); power at 4 mmol/L +4.5% vs +2.1% (P = 0.03); % of VO₂max at 4 mmol/L up with MIT only; no difference in VO₂max.
  - **Design limits (full text):** "this study is not a randomized cross-over study". Every rider did the moderate block first (October/November) and the hard block about two months later (January). 43 started, 21 withdrew (8 respiratory infections; 7 during the moderate block, 12 between blocks). A secondary aim of a larger preregistered study, no sample-size calculation for this comparison. No comparison with normal training.
  - Riders: 21 men, 1 woman, age **19.2**, VO₂max 69.5; 11 of 22 at "professional" performance level.
  - **Moderate block:** 6 sessions in 7 days, 7 × 10, 6 × 12 or 5 × 14 min (2.5–3.5 min recoveries), RPE 14–15. Measured: RPE 14.5, **85.5% HRmax**, lactate **2.9 mmol/L**, 78% of VO₂max, 66% of peak power in the VO₂max test. 7 h 4 min of work intervals in the block.
  - **Hard block:** 5 sessions in 6 days, 5 × 8.75 min of 30 s at ~118% / 15 s at ~60% of 40-min power, RPE 16–18. Measured: RPE 17.1, 90% HRmax, lactate 7.5, 83% of VO₂max. 3 h 42 min of work intervals.
  - Time ≥ 90% of VO₂max per session: hard 10:39, moderate 4:44. In the hard block each extra minute per session went with **+0.41 mL/kg/min** more VO₂max gain (R² adj 0.65); no relationship in the moderate block. A correlation within one group, like Odden 2024 (same Lillehammer group).
  - VO₂max: +1.7 mL/kg/min after the hard block (significant), +1.1 after the moderate (P = 0.06); **no difference between blocks** (P = 0.43). Peak power in the VO₂max test +3.7% hard, +2.4% moderate (no difference). 10-s sprint: hard +1.5%, moderate −1.5% (P = 0.08). Knee-extension torque at 60°/s fell after the moderate block only.
  - Authors' conclusion: "rather than focusing on which approach is superior … both MIT and HIT should be integrated along with low-intensity exercise". They cite Seiler 2013 for intensity and duration interacting.

The VO₂max half is only a non-significant difference here. Also a tested anchor for list item 4: RPE 14–15 put these riders at about 85% HRmax and a lactate of about 3.

- **Schoenmakers, Murray, White, Matta & Bossi 2026**, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 18:358, doi:10.1186/s13102-026-01766-x, PMC13480085 [**FT 27 Oct 2026**, Europe PMC, CC BY]. Systematic review and meta-analysis of time near VO₂max in **single sessions**; search to 23 Sept 2025; 86 studies, 239 protocols, all modalities.
  - Time ≥ 90% VO₂max (Table 3, 183 protocols): long (≥ 2 min) **555 ± 234 s**, moderate (> 30 s to < 2 min) 322 ± 174, short (≤ 30 s) **336 ± 273**. Total work interval duration **1,296 vs 694 vs 659 s**; cumulative session 2,058 vs 1,431 vs 1,390 s. ANCOVA adjusted for cumulative duration (work + rest), not for work duration. Long vs short g = 0.87.
  - Mixed models: cumulative duration and interval length explain **6.4%** of variance in time ≥ 90% (marginal R²; 7.2% for ≥ 95%). Adding interval length alone did not improve fit for ≥ 90% (p = 0.070); the interaction did (p = 0.034).
  - Within-study meta-analysis by interval length: SMD 0.45, small, I² 76%.
  - **Classification (Limitations section):** series such as 30/15 were kept as short intervals following Wen et al., though "11 repetitions of 30 s 'on'/15 s 'off' is considered one 7 min 45 s work interval" elsewhere; the authors say this obscures "the true comparisons". Odden, Urianstad, Appelhans and Almquist are among those classed as short.
  - **Variable vs even-intensity work intervals:** SMD 0.80 (0.40 to 1.18), I² 68%; **+165 s (97 to 223)**. Includes decreasing-intensity (fast starts, Rønnestad 2020 skiers), increasing and variable (Bossi 2020, Odden, Urianstad 2024, Appelhans 2025, Rønnestad 2022 skiers); subgroup differences not significant (p = 0.64). So fast starts are not worse than surges, contrary to the Substack post.
  - Active vs passive recovery: no difference (SMD −0.15); in sessions to exhaustion passive recovery allowed more work.
