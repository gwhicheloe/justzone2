# Chapter 18: Special cases

Twenty-one older track athletes, with an average age of about 50, had their maximum oxygen uptake measured, and then measured again ten years later and twenty years later, when their average age was 70. Nine of them had remained at an elite level. Every group had lost fitness at each test, and the nine who had kept training hardest had lost the least [@pollock1997].

The rules of the earlier chapters carry across to riders who are older, to women, and to riders stuck indoors. What changes is a matter of degree, and in each case the research is thinner than for the young men who make up most of it.

## Older riders

![VO₂max over twenty years in older track athletes, by how they trained, as a percentage of each group's value at the start, when their average age was about 50. Every group declined; the group that kept training at a high level declined least. The low group is two people. The cumulative percentages are my arithmetic from the changes the paper reports for each decade.](figures/pollock-decline.svg)

Pollock's athletes were tested in three groups by what they had done over the 20 years: nine who remained elite, ten who carried on with frequent moderate-to-hard endurance training, and two who greatly reduced their training [@pollock1997]. The elite group's VO₂max fell 8% in the first decade and 15% in the second. The moderate group's fell 13% and 14%. The two who had largely stopped lost 18% and then 34%. The authors' summary was that older athletes' capacities fall despite continued vigorous training, by about 8% to 15% a decade.

Their maximum heart rate fell by about 5 to 7 beats a minute per decade, and the fall was the same whether they trained or not [@pollock1997]. That has a direct consequence for anyone who sets zones from a maximum. A maximum measured ten years ago is now too high by several beats, and every zone hung from it is too high with it. Chapter 6's argument for setting the top of Zone 2 from a threshold, not from a percentage of a maximum, is stronger for an older rider.

The review that set out how endurance performance changes with age, by Hirofumi Tanaka and Douglas Seals in 2008, describes the usual pattern in masters athletes: peak performance holds to about 35, falls modestly to between 50 and 60, and falls more steeply after that [@tanaka2008]. Of the three factors in Joyner and Coyle's model, VO₂max falls most and does most of the damage. The lactate threshold falls too, perhaps as a consequence. Efficiency, in trained athletes, does not change with age. The heart's maximum output falls because both its maximum rate and its maximum stroke volume fall, and the muscle extracts less oxygen from the blood.

A large part of the decline, the authors write, appears to come from a fall in the intensity and volume of training that older athletes can do. Some of that fall is ageing itself. Some of it is that older athletes train less hard, because they have less time or less appetite for it or because they expect to recover slowly. Pollock's elite group, who kept training hardest, lost the least, though with nine people and no randomisation it is impossible to say how much of that was the training and how much was the kind of person who keeps racing at seventy. {grade B for the decline; grade C for how much training slows it}

### Recovery

Older riders often say they recover more slowly, and it is plausible. Older muscle may be damaged more by a given session and repair more slowly. A 2008 review of the evidence found that the studies rarely compare like with like [@fell2008]. Older participants were usually less active than younger ones, so the effects of age and inactivity were tangled together, and the exercise used to cause damage was rarely anything like normal training. A later review of masters athletes reached a similar view: age may slow recovery less than is often assumed, and a sedentary life explains more of the decline in performance [@borges2016]. {grade C} A rider reading a book about training is unlikely to be leading one.

That leaves an older rider with an experiment to run on themselves. Chapter 12's default of two hard sessions a week, at least 48 hours apart, is where to start. If the second session keeps going badly, stretch the gap to 72 hours or more, or drop to one hard session a week for a while, which keeps most of what you have (Chapter 12). Chapter 19 covers how to tell which is happening.

### Lifting

Strength training matters more with age, for reasons that have little to do with speed. Pollock's athletes lost lean mass at each test, and those who had begun lifting weights tended to keep more of it. Leg strength and bone density held up reasonably well across the group, and the ones who lifted had denser bones in their arms [@pollock1997]. Chapter 14 described the consistent finding that road cyclists have low bone density in the spine, which the bike does nothing to correct. For a rider over 40 whose only exercise is cycling, heavy lifting twice a week is probably the most useful addition to the week that has nothing to do with going faster. {grade C}

### Is hard training safe?

For a healthy older rider, the trials are reassuring. In Generation 100, the five-year trial in Chapter 10, 1,567 Norwegians aged 70 to 77 were assigned to interval training, moderate training or the national guidelines, and the interval group had the fewest deaths {shadow @stensvold2020}. In 2007 a Trondheim group gave patients with heart failure after a heart attack, average age 75.5, the same 4 × 4-minute intervals that Chapter 11 describes {shadow @wisloff2007}. Neither study is evidence that hard training is safe for everyone. Both are evidence that age alone is not a reason to avoid it. Anyone with a heart condition, or chest pain, palpitations or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort, should see a doctor before changing their training, and this chapter is no substitute for one.

## Women

### Reading the research: who was in the study?

![Of 1,441 studies published in three leading sports medicine journals between 2021 and 2023, about one in five studied only men and one in fourteen only women. Fewer than one in seventeen of the studies that included women took account of the menstrual cycle in their design.](figures/who-was-studied.svg)

The research in this book is mostly about men, and it is worth knowing by how much. In 2014 a group counted the participants in three leading sports medicine journals and found that 39% were women. In 2025 another group repeated the count for 2021 to 2023 [@ose2025]. They found 1,441 studies, of which 268 studied only men and 103 only women. The average study's participants were 40% women. Of the studies that included women, only 66, or 5.6%, took account of the menstrual cycle in their design.

Those are general sports medicine journals, and training studies are worse. Almost every training trial in Parts II and III of this book is of men or mostly men. Chapter 11 noted that most interval trials are of men, and the few that compared the sexes directly found similar responses.

The question to ask of any study is who it was done on, and then whether the answer is likely to carry across. For most of the physiology in this book there is no particular reason to expect a different direction in women. There are reasons to expect differences in degree, and the honest position is that most of them have not been measured.

### What has been measured

**How much training raises VO₂max.** The one meta-analysis I found that set out to answer this pooled eight studies of untrained people, 90 men and 85 women, who were given the same training [@diazcanestro2019]. The men's VO₂max rose more: by 191 mL/min more in absolute terms, and by 1.95 mL/kg/min more relative to body weight. There was no sign of disagreement between the studies. It is one small analysis of untrained people, and it measured VO₂max alone, not threshold, efficiency or performance. In trained women, the Trondheim 4 × 4 trial found a rise in VO₂max of 7.3%, much as in men, and the 30/15 study in Chapter 11 found the same advantage in time near VO₂max in both sexes {shadow @helgerud2023; @urianstad2024}. {grade C}

**Fatigue.** Women are usually less fatigable than men in sustained, static muscle contractions at the same relative intensity, according to a 2014 review by Sandra Hunter, though the difference depends on the task: how fast and how hard the muscle works, which muscle it is, and the conditions [@hunter2014]. In 82,303 marathon runners, the women's heart rate drifted less relative to their pace, and later in the race, than the men's {shadow @smyth2022}. Chapter 9 called that quality durability. Whether it means women should train differently has not been tested.

**The menstrual cycle.** A 2020 meta-analysis of 78 studies found that performance might be trivially lower in the early follicular phase, the days around the start of a period, than at other times [@mcnulty2020]. The pooled effect was small enough that its credible interval included no effect at all, the largest contrast between two phases was also small, and the authors rated the quality of the evidence as low. Their conclusion was that general guidelines can't be drawn, and that women should take a personal approach, based on how they themselves respond.

That conclusion matters because a fashion has grown up around the opposite idea: that training should be scheduled around the phases of the cycle, with hard sessions in one phase and easy riding in another. The evidence doesn't support a general rule of that kind. It does support paying attention. A rider who notices that the hard sessions in particular days of her cycle go badly can move them, as she would move them for a bad night's sleep. {grade B that the average effect is trivial; grade C for anything more specific}

**Hormonal contraception.** A meta-analysis of 42 studies found that women on the oral contraceptive pill might perform slightly worse on average than women not taking it, an effect most likely to be trivial, and that performance was consistent across the pill cycle [@elliottsale2020]. Most of the studies were of moderate or low quality. The authors did not think the evidence justified general advice either way.

**Eating enough.** Chapter 13 described relative energy deficiency in sport, the syndrome of persistently eating less than training costs, with effects on hormones, bones and performance. It affects men as well as women {shadow @mountjoy2023}. A rider who is losing weight on a hard training load, or whose periods stop, should treat it as a medical matter.

**Pregnancy and menopause.** Neither is covered by the research in this book, and I would not give advice on either from it. Both are matters for a doctor or a specialist in women's sports medicine.

So the training in this book applies to women as it is. The hard sessions, the spacing, the easy riding and the fuel are the same, with the caveat that most of the trials behind them were of men, and the one meta-analysis of trainability found men gained more VO₂max from the same training.

## Indoors

Riding indoors changes the numbers more than it changes the training.

> **From the saddle.** I was fine with the boredom. In fact I was never bored, whether I was watching Netflix or chess videos on YouTube, or listening to music. Zone 2 was my sacred decompression time, great after a busy day at work. I could think things through and solve life's problems. I'd also enjoy the kids roaming into the pain cave to chat. They might go on the rowing machine, or we'd just catch up. Luckily, in Zone 2 it's still easy to talk.


### One study, up close: same effort, indoors and out

In 2014 a group of American researchers had cyclists ride a 40 km time trial twice, once in the laboratory and once outdoors, in random order, at the same perceived effort [@mieras2014].

::: trial
Aim: To compare the physiological and psychological responses to cycling in the laboratory and outdoors.
Design: 12 recreationally trained male cyclists. Two 40 km trials, one in each setting, in random order, with the same instruction to give the same perceived effort in both. Wind 2.5 metres a second outdoors and none in the laboratory; other conditions similar [@mieras2014].
| | Outdoors | Laboratory
| Average power | 208 W | 163 W
| Average heart rate | 152 bpm | 143 bpm
| Core temperature | Similar | Similar
| Skin temperature | 31.4 °C | 33.0 °C
| Rated effort | No difference | No difference
Authors' conclusion: Outdoor cycling lets cyclists exercise at a higher intensity at the same perceived effort, so cyclists may want to ride at a higher perceived effort indoors to get the same benefit.
:::

At the same sense of effort, the riders produced about a fifth less power in the laboratory, and their heart rate was 9 beats lower. Their core temperature was the same in both places; their skin was warmer indoors, where nothing moved the air.

The abstract doesn't say what the riders rode indoors, and a laboratory ergometer may feel different from a rider's own bike on a smart trainer. It is twelve men and one ride each. A rider who sets a session by feel indoors will probably ride it easier than intended, and a rider who sets it by power or heart rate will find the same numbers feel harder than outdoors. Neither is wrong. They are measuring different things in a different place. {grade C}

### Heat and still air

The reason indoor riding feels hard is mostly heat. A rider outdoors makes their own wind, and the air moving over the skin carries heat away. Indoors, without a fan, it doesn't. In a 2005 study, nine riders cycled for two hours at 33 °C with fans set to air speeds from almost nothing to 50 km/h [@saunders2005]. With little or no moving air, their heat storage, body temperature and rated effort were all higher. At the speeds a rider actually rides at, the differences disappeared, and drinking more made no difference to heat storage once the air was moving. The authors' point was that laboratory studies done in still air overstate the heat stress of riding outdoors.

Heat drives heart rate up over a long ride, the drift that Chapter 9 measured. The heart pumps less blood with each beat as the body sends more to the skin and as fluid is lost, so it beats faster to keep up {shadow @coyle2001drift}. Indoors, with poor airflow, drift comes sooner and goes further. Good fans, a cool room and a drink on every long session are the remedies, and they matter more for the long easy rides than for short intervals.

### Holding power, or holding heart rate

Smart trainers can hold a set power whatever the cadence, which riders call ERG mode. It makes intervals precise and recoveries genuinely easy, and Chapter 11 discussed what that does for hard sessions. For a long Zone 2 ride it means that heart rate does all the drifting. An hour in, a power that started a few beats under the ceiling may have taken heart rate over it, especially in a warm room.

Chapter 9 explained why no way of handling it is perfect late in a long ride. One is to hold power and accept the drift, setting the power 10 to 15 W under the top of the zone for long rides, the rule of thumb in Chapter 9. The other is to hold heart rate and let the power fall, which keeps the body under its threshold but may lower the work more than it needs to, because the threshold heart rate itself rises as you tire. I found no study of either approach indoors. Pick one, keep the room cool, and judge the ride by Chapter 19's measures over weeks, not minutes. {grade C}

## Short of time

Most riders are short of time, and the earlier chapters were written for them. The four-hour week in Chapter 17 shows the shape: two hard sessions and two short easy rides. One well-ridden hard session a week holds most of what a rider has for months (Chapter 12), and sprint intervals give a little more for very little time, at the cost of being the weaker choice for long efforts and the riskier one if run all-out (Chapter 11). The thing that matters most, for a rider short of time, is the same thing that mattered in my own winter: riding regularly. Four short rides every week will usually do more than one long ride when there is time for it. {grade C}

## What to do with it

For an older rider, keep the intensity, expect the numbers to fall slowly anyway, reset heart-rate zones from a recent threshold, lift for your bones, and stretch the gap between hard sessions if two leave you flat. For a woman, train as the book describes, knowing that most of the evidence comes from men and that the average effect of the cycle on performance is trivial, and track your own response if you are curious. Indoors, expect less power at the same effort, use fans, and decide whether you are holding power or heart rate on long rides. {grade C overall: most of this chapter rests on small studies, reviews and reasoning}

None of these changes the order of the week. Each of them is a reason to judge your training by what happens to you, which is the subject of the next chapter.
