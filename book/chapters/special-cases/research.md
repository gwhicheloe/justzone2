# Special cases (Chapter 19): research notes

Written 15 October 2026 in the Part V batch. Tags: [FT] full text, [Abs] abstract, [Rep] second hand, [GK] general knowledge, [Own] my arithmetic or reasoning.

## Masters riders

- **Tanaka & Seals 2008** [Abs], J Physiol review. Peak endurance performance is maintained until about 35, modest decreases to 50–60, "progressively steeper declines thereafter". Of the three determinants (VO₂max, lactate threshold, economy), the fall in VO₂max is the main mechanism; the lactate threshold falls too, perhaps secondary to VO₂max; economy does not change with age in endurance-trained adults. Maximal stroke volume, maximal heart rate and arterio-venous O₂ difference all fall. The declines "appear to be mediated in large part by a reduction in the intensity (velocity) and volume of the exercise that can be performed during training sessions".
- **Pollock 1997** [Abs], 20-year follow-up of 21 older track athletes; mean ages 50.5 (T1), 60.2 (T2), 70.4 (T3). Groups at 20 years: high (remained elite, n = 9), moderate (continued frequent moderate-to-rigorous endurance training, n = 10), low (greatly reduced training, n = 2). VO₂max fell at each test: high 8% (T1→T2) and 15% (T2→T3); moderate 13% and 14%; low 18% and 34%. Maximal heart rate fell about 5–7 beats a minute per decade, independent of training. Weight stable in high and moderate; fat up about 2–2.5% per decade; fat-free mass fell, with a trend to better maintenance in those who began weight training. Leg strength and bone density generally maintained from 60 to 89 (cross-sectional at T3); weight trainers had greater arm bone density. Summary: capacities reduced despite continued vigorous training, "approximately 8–15%/decade".
  - [Own] Cumulative, from the abstract's two steps (figure `pollock1997.csv`): high 100 → 92 → 78.2; moderate 100 → 87 → 74.8; low 100 → 82 → 54.1 (n = 2). Multiplying successive percentage falls is my arithmetic; the paper's own cumulative figures are not in the abstract.
- **Fell & Williams 2008** [Abs], review: older athletes perceive slower recovery; plausible reasons (more damage, slower repair), but studies rarely account for physical activity level, and the exercise used is rarely like normal training, so the roles of ageing and inactivity are hard to separate.
- **Borges 2016** (already in sources; How many hard sessions) review of masters athletes: age may slow recovery less than assumed; a sedentary life explains more of the decline.
- Strength and bone: Strength training chapter (Olmedillas 2012, Nichols 2003); Pollock's weight trainers kept more fat-free mass (trend) and arm bone density.
- Heart rate zones: maximum falls 5–7 bpm a decade (Pollock), so a zone set from a maximum measured ten years ago is too high [Own]; Finding your zones covers estimating the maximum (Nes 2013) and why a threshold is better.
- Generation 100 (Beyond performance): 70- to 77-year-olds did intervals safely for five years; Wisløff 2007 (Designing intervals side road): heart failure patients, average age 75.5, did 4 × 4.

## Women

- **Ose 2025** [Abs], AJSM, a decade after Costello 2014. Original research in MSSE, BJSM and AJSM, January 2021 to August 2023: 1,441 studies, 40,152,860 participants, 43.95% female; mean proportion female per study 40.22%; 103 studies (7.15%) female-only, 268 (18.6%) male-only; 66 (5.6%) considered menstrual status in their design. [Own] 66/1,441 = 4.6%, so the 5.6% must be of a subset; 1,441 − 268 male-only = 1,173, and 66/1,173 = 5.6%: of the studies that included women. The draft says so. Female share up from 39% (Costello 2014) to 43.95%.
  - [Own] 100 − 7.15 − 18.6 = 74.25% mixed. The 40 million participants are dominated by a few very large epidemiological datasets [Own inference from the size; the abstract doesn't say], so the per-study figures are the better guide to training research. Three general sports medicine journals, not training studies specifically.
- **Diaz-Canestro & Montero 2019** [Abs], meta-analysis: eight studies, 175 healthy untrained people (90 men, 85 women), sex-matched training dose. Men gained more absolute VO₂max (mean difference +191 mL/min, 95% CI 99 to 283) and more relative VO₂max (+1.95 mL/kg/min, 0.76 to 3.15); no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). Conclusion: greater VO₂max improvements in men for a given dose. [Own] Untrained only; VO₂max only, not performance or threshold; eight small studies.
- Helgerud 2023 (Designing intervals): 4 × 4 raised VO₂max 7.3% in well-trained women, much as in men; sprints injured more women. Urianstad 2024: 30/15 gave more time near VO₂max in both sexes, no sex difference.
- **McNulty 2020** [Abs], meta-analysis, 78 studies; 51 in the pairwise analysis. Performance "might be trivially reduced" in the early follicular phase compared with all others (ES0.5 = −0.06, 95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04); largest network contrast early vs late follicular (−0.14, −0.26 to −0.03); quality of evidence "low" (42%). Recommends "a personalised approach ... based on each individual's response".
- **Elliott-Sale 2020** [Abs], meta-analysis, 42 studies, 590 participants; 83% of studies moderate, low or very low quality. Oral contraceptive users "might" have "slightly inferior exercise performance on average" than naturally menstruating women, "most likely to be trivial"; "the current evidence does not warrant general guidance on OCP use compared with non-use"; performance consistent across the pill cycle.
- **Hunter 2014** [Abs], review: women are usually less fatigable than men for similar-intensity isometric fatiguing contractions; the difference is task-specific (type, intensity and speed of contraction, muscle group, environment). Also notes the bias of studying more males.
- Smyth 2022 (82,303 marathon runners, Knowing it's working): women's decoupling smaller (1.12 vs 1.17) and later (26.3 vs 25.0 km).
- Energy availability: Mountjoy 2023 (Fuelling): relative energy deficiency in sport.
- [GK] Pregnancy and menopause: not covered by research read for this book; the draft says so and points to medical advice. No claims.

## Generation 100 and Wisløff (reused)

- Stensvold 2020 [FT] (Beyond performance): 1,567 of 6,966 invited Trondheim residents aged 70 to 77, five years, interval vs moderate vs guidelines; interval group fewest deaths, not significantly.
- Wisløff 2007 [Abs] (Designing intervals): 27 heart-failure patients after a heart attack, average age 75.5, 4 × 4.
- Helgerud 2023 [FT]: 4 × 4 raised VO₂max 7.3% in well-trained women.
- Chapter 9's rule of thumb (set power 10–15 W under the top of the zone for rides over two hours) is advice in the author's voice there, not a reported practice of George's; this chapter cites it as Chapter 9's rule.

## Indoors

- **Mieras 2014** [Abs], 12 recreationally trained male cyclists, randomised order, one laboratory and one outdoor 40 km trial, told to ride at the same perceived effort. Wind 2.5 m/s outdoors vs 0 indoors; other conditions similar. Power 208.1 ± 10.2 W outdoors vs 163.4 ± 11.8 W in the lab (p < 0.001); heart rate 152 ± 4 vs 143 ± 6 (p = 0.04). Core temperature similar; skin cooler outdoors (31.4 vs 33.0 °C). RPE no different. Authors: cyclists "may want to ride at a higher perceived exertion in indoor settings to acquire the same benefit".
  - [Own] 163.4 / 208.1 = 0.785: about 21% less power indoors at the same perceived effort, and 9 beats lower. Which bike indoors (an ergometer? their own bike on a trainer?) is not in the abstract.
- **Saunders 2005** [Abs], nine subjects, 2 h at 33 °C, 59% humidity, air velocity 0.2, 9.9, 33.3 and 50.1 km/h. Heat storage, body temperature and RPE higher at 0 and 10 km/h than at the higher speeds; no difference between 33 and 50 km/h; drinking more did not change heat storage when air speed was high. Conclusion: laboratory findings in still air cannot be extrapolated outdoors, where facing air speed is seldom lower than the rider's speed.
- **Coyle & González-Alonso 2001** (in sources as coyle2001drift; Finding your zones, shadow) [Abs]: cardiovascular drift — heart rate rises and stroke volume falls over prolonged exercise, worse with heat and dehydration.
- ERG mode [GK]: a smart trainer in ERG mode holds the power set, adjusting resistance to cadence; heart rate does all the drifting. No study of ERG mode itself found [Own: none searched successfully; say "I found none"].
