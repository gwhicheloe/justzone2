## Saris 1989 **[FT: PDF supplied by George 11 October 2026, `papers/saris1989.pdf`; a scan, read page by page]**

- Authors at the Nutrition Research Centre, University of Limburg, Maastricht.
- "Eight male cyclists, all from one of the leading professional teams, were recruited." Five kept a diary. "After 4 days of racing, one of the subjects had an accident and was forced to stop", and another cyclist took over for weeks 2 and 3. Four completed the diary over the whole race (Tables 1 and 2 are of four; Tables 4 to 6 say five, with the fifth based on days 8 to 22). Each had ridden the Tour at least three times.
- **The year and the team are not given.**
- A trained nutritionist instructed the riders, "made weekly checks, and cross-checked with the information about the food supply during the race including caloric beverages". Energy drinks came in standard bottles.
- The four: mean weight 69.2 kg before, 68.9 after; VO₂max 79.4 mL/kg/min; body fat 11.6%. "Body weight and percentage body fat did not change significantly."
- Energy expenditure (calculated from speed, time and altitude, not measured) 25.4 ± 1.4 MJ a day; highest 32.7. Intake 24.7 ± 2.4; highest 32.4, "during one of the mountain days". Rest day 12.9 and 16.1.
- 49% of energy taken during the race (Table 4). 61% of simple carbohydrate and 55% of complex carbohydrate taken during the race.
- **"Based on the results in Table 4 and the total cycling time over 22 days (mean 115 h), it is possible to calculate food intake during the race. Energy intake was about 2.3 MJ/h and CHO intake appeared to be 94 g CHO/h or 1.37 g CHO/kg/h representing 69 En%."** This confirms that 94 g/h is the rate during racing.
- Daily carbohydrate 12.3 g/kg/day at 60 En%.
- Food sources of energy (Table 6): sweet cakes 13.7%, maltodextrin formula drink 9.8%, bread 9.0%, pasta 6.4%, sugar 5.9%. Of carbohydrate: maltodextrin drink 15.8%, sweet cakes 14.9%, bread 11.3%. Sweet cakes "were eaten during the race". About a third of carbohydrate from liquids.
- Water 6.7 L a day, range 4.2 to 11.8.
- Their own laboratory simulation (Brouns et al., same supplement) reached 125 g/h during exercise. Not used.
- Vitamins and iron were also taken by tablet and injection (Table 5). Not used in the book.
- 24.7 MJ = 5,900 kcal; 25.4 MJ = 6,070 kcal. 12.3 × 69.2 = 851 g. [Own]

## What a ride costs

- About a quarter of energy burned becomes work; 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ; so kJ of work is roughly kcal burned. [GK. The same rule is used in the app's calorie calculation.]
- 200 W × 3 h = 2,160 kJ. 60 g/h × 3 h × 4 kcal = 720 kcal (a third). 90 g/h: 1,080 kcal (half). 150 W = 540 kJ an hour; 120 g = 480 kcal. [Own]
- Carbohydrate about 4 kcal per gram. [GK]
- Glycogen stores can be emptied by "a few hours of hard riding". [GK. No amount is quoted. Bergström 1967 has no abstract in the database.]
- Podlogar 2022: endogenous carbohydrate oxidation 2.15 and 2.20 g/min (about 130 g/h: my arithmetic) at 95% of gas exchange threshold, after an overnight fast. [Abs]
- Muros 2019: nine professional riders, three-week Vuelta, food weighed daily by two investigators; carbohydrate 12.5 ± 1.8 g/kg/day. [Abs] The comparison with Saris's 12.3 g/kg/day, and the caution about it, are mine.

## The gut-wall limit

- Tracer method: ingested sugars labelled with carbon isotopes. [Abs, Jentjens 2004; description simplified]
- Exogenous oxidation of a single carbohydrate peaks around 60 g/h at ingestion rates above 60 to 70 g/h; 144 and 180 g/h did not raise it much; not limited by gastric emptying, muscle uptake or liver storage, so absorption deduced to be limiting; SGLT1 saturated. [FT, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Fructose uses GLUT5. [FT, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Jentjens 2004: eight trained cyclists, 120 min at 50% of maximum power; 1.2 g/min glucose, 1.8 g/min glucose, or 0.6 fructose + 1.2 glucose. Peak exogenous oxidation 1.26 g/min with the mixture against 0.80 and 0.83. [Abs]
- Currell & Jeukendrup 2008 (already in sources for another chapter): eight cyclists, 120 min at 55% Wmax then a time trial of about an hour; glucose + fructose 8% quicker than glucose and 19% quicker than water. [Abs] **The times printed in the database abstract look transposed (the "quicker" trial has the larger number); I used only the percentages.**
- 90 g/h as the upper limit of established guidelines; above it "ultra-high". [FT, Plews 2026]

## Dose

- Smith 2013: 51 cyclists and triathletes, four sites, four trials each; 2 h at 95% of the workload at 4 mmol/L lactate, then a 20 km time trial; 12 beverages from 10 to 120 g/h (1:1:1 glucose, fructose, maltodextrin) plus placebo; double-blind. Modelled improvements 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0 and 4.7% at 9, 19, 31, 48 and 78 g/h; diminishing above 78. [Abs] Plews 2026 adds that the relationship was flat between about 30 and 90 g/h. [Rep]
- Norte 2026: Durability notes.
- Podlogar 2022: 11 male cyclists (VO₂peak 62.6), 3 h at 95% GET, fasted; 120 g/h (0.8:1 fructose to maltodextrin) against 90 g/h (1:2). Exogenous oxidation in the last hour 1.51 ± 0.22 against 1.29 ± 0.16 g/min (p = 0.026); endogenous not different. [Abs]
- Oxidation efficiency about 86% at 90 g/h and 76% at 120, leaving about 30 g/h unoxidised; "a large proportion may remain unabsorbed in the gastrointestinal tract". [Rep, Plews 2026 on Podlogar 2022]
- Viribay 2020: 20 male elite runners "who had previously undertaken nutritional and gut training", randomised to 120, 90 or 60 g/h in a mountain marathon; lower CK, LDH and GOT at 24 h and lower RPE-based internal load at 120. [Abs] No significant improvement in performance times. [Rep, Plews 2026] Group sizes not in the abstract.
- King et al.: 0, 60, 75, 90, 112.5 g/h; no significant differences between carbohydrate doses in a 30-minute time trial; supported in a follow-up. [Rep, Plews 2026. Papers not looked up. The draft says "two trials from one laboratory".]
- Hearris et al.: exogenous oxidation ranged from about 1.3 to 1.9 g/min at 120 g/h. [Rep, Plews 2026]
- Recovery hypothesis; no studies across full Ironman or ultramarathon duration. [FT, Plews 2026]
- Plews 2026 is a "current opinion", not a systematic review. First author declares an affiliation with a micronutrition company "with no presently commercial interest in carbohydrate fuelling products". Based in Auckland. [GK for the location; one of the open copies is in the Auckland University of Technology repository]

## Morton et al. 2026 **[FT: PDF supplied by George 11 October 2026, `papers/morton2026.pdf`]**

- Read: abstract, introduction, GI considerations, the cycling section, Toward contemporary guidelines, summary, declarations. Not read: the metabolism sections, marathon and ultra-endurance sections.
- Seven authors, all at UK institutions (Liverpool John Moores, Science in Sport, Bath, Manchester Metropolitan, Exeter, Birmingham).
- "Although field observations report a trend for endurance athletes to consume (and experiment with) higher rates of CHO ingestion during training and racing (i.e., 120–200 g/h), the efficacy of such doses is not yet substantiated by current scientific research."
- "The upper limit of CHO intake could increase from 90 to 120 g/h (at least for trained participants), considering that both exogenous and whole-body rates of CHO oxidation can be increased with these higher ingestion rates." Later: "a CHO intake of 90–120 g/h currently represents the most evidence-based recommendation for the upper limit".
- "We acknowledge that a definitive CHO dose–response study and its effect on performance in training or competition-related scenarios is yet to be completed under standardized conditions in the same laboratory."
- Professional cyclists' habitual intakes "have often been reported as 60–90 g/h", in Grand Tours (García-Rovés 1998, Muros 2019, Saris 1989) and shorter stage races.
- 2018 Giro d'Italia winner, stages 11 to 19: 52 to 96 g/h, the highest on stage 19 (on-bike expenditure 6,180 kJ). Source: "Morton, personal communication". The paper names the rider, Chris Froome. **George approved naming him in this chapter (11 October 2026).** The approval covers this fuelling fact only; the Drugs chapter's rule on living riders is unchanged.
- "Recent reports indicate that a stage winner on a 2025 Tour de France stage consumed ~116 g/h CHO, and some riders are now reportedly training their GI system to tolerate 200–220 g/h." The reference is a press article (Whitehead 2025), not a study. The draft calls it a press report and hearsay.
- Higher intakes "may help riders better match intake to expenditure and support daily energy balance, even if this does not necessarily confer an immediate performance benefit".
- The paper says recent Tours are "among the fastest in history". **Not used**: the book makes no speed comparisons between eras.
- Gut training: gut-training protocols "consistently reduced gut discomfort" (citing Martinez 2023); "there has so far been no evidence for increases in exogenous CHO oxidation" from them; the Cox 2010 diet study is the exception. Consistent with the draft.
- Fewer than 5% of studies on carbohydrate during exercise used elite or world-class athletes; more than 80% used recreational or trained participants; fewer than 3% used women only. [Not yet used; good for Special cases.]
- Opens with the 1924 and 1925 Boston Marathon studies (Levine 1924; Gordon 1925): hypoglycaemia at the finish in 1924, prevented by carbohydrate in 1925. [Not used.]
- Conflicts: JPM consulting and grants from Science in Sport; JTG consulting; JMF employment; MAH grants; JNP consulting (all Science in Sport); TP consulting for Nduranz; others none. Five of seven with a tie to Science in Sport: my count. The draft does not name the company.

## Pfeiffer 2012 [Abs]

- 221 athletes: two Ironman races, a half-Ironman, a marathon, a 100/150 km cycle race; professional cyclists during stage racing. Post-race questionnaire.
- Intakes 6 to 136 g/h. Means: Ironman 62 to 71 g/h, cycle race 53, marathon 35. The professionals' rate is not in the abstract.
- Serious gut symptoms: Ironman about 31%, half-Ironman 14%, cycle race 4%, marathon 4%, professionals 7%.
- In Ironman, carbohydrate intake correlated with nausea and flatulence and negatively with finishing time (r = −0.55 and −0.48).
- "The faster athletes are fitter, working at a lower fraction of capacity, with more blood flow for the gut": my reasoning. [GK]

## Can the gut be trained?

- Competitive eaters; record 69 hot dogs with buns in 10 minutes at the time of writing. [FT, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Lambert et al.: runners tolerated fluid at about sweat rate during 90 minutes at 65% VO₂max; stomach comfort improved with practice without measurable change in gastric emptying. [Rep, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Cunningham et al.: glucose 400 g a day for 3 days; half-emptying time for glucose 20.7 against 29.1 minutes; protein drink unchanged. [Rep, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Mice: SGLT1 protein 1.9-fold after 2 weeks of a high-carbohydrate diet; piglets after 3 days; horses after 1 week. "Although no direct human studies exist". [FT, Jeukendrup 2017]
- Cox 2010: 16 endurance-trained cyclists or triathletes, pair-matched and randomly allocated, 28 days; test of 100 minutes steady state then a 7 kJ/kg time trial, with water or 10% glucose; exogenous glucose oxidation during the 100 minutes 54.6 to 63.6 g in the High group, no change in Low; performance improved about 6% after training; the metabolic changes "do not alter the training-induced magnitude of increase in exercise performance". [Abs]
- Cox 2010, detail: 16 h/week of training; base diet 5 g/kg/day; High group given an extra 1.5 g/kg per hour of exercise, mainly as glucose drink, before, during and after; control group given fat- and protein-rich foods after exercise; daily intake 8.5 against 5.3 g/kg. [Rep, Jeukendrup 2017]
- 63.6 / 54.6 = 1.165, "about a sixth". [Own]
- "Has not been consistently replicated"; "limited support for enhancement in exogenous carbohydrate oxidation capacity". [FT, Plews 2026]
- Australian Institute of Sport; Gregory Cox as lead. [GK. Check the affiliation and first name.]
- Costa 2017: 25 runners; 2 h at 60% VO₂max with 30 g carbohydrate (2:1 glucose to fructose) every 20 minutes (90 g/h: my arithmetic), then a 1 h distance test; randomised to gel, food or placebo gut training for 2 weeks. Symptoms −60% (gel) and −63% (food), greater than placebo; distance +5.2%, +4.3%, −2.1%; oxidation rates not different. [Abs]
- Miall 2018: 18 runners; "blinded carbohydrate (90 g/h) or placebo"; daily during 1 h running for 2 weeks. Gut discomfort, total, upper and lower symptoms and nausea reduced on carbohydrate, not placebo; breath hydrogen peak 13 ± 6 to 6 ± 3 ppm; effort distance 11.7 to 12.3 km on carbohydrate. [Abs]
- Monash University, Melbourne. [GK]
- Martinez 2023: 304 identified, eight included; carbohydrate only in seven; 4 to 28 days; gut discomfort −47% on average with 2-week feeding (two studies); malabsorption −45 to −54% (two), no change (one); no change in gastric emptying (two); mostly low risk of bias, three with "some concerns"; 79% male; "a standard gut-training protocol does not yet exist"; exercise modes running (5), cycling (1), others. [FT]
- **Martinez 2023, full PDF supplied by George 11 October 2026 (`papers/martinez2023.pdf`); Table 2, sections 3.4 to 3.7, the discussion of performance and the declarations now read.**
  - Total gut symptoms: Costa gel −60%, food −63%, placebo −25%; Miall carbohydrate −61%, placebo −2%. Gut discomfort: Costa −44% and −49% against placebo −18%; Miall −48% against −20%.
  - King et al. 2022 (Nutrients 14:1929; reference 28 of the review; paper itself not read): study 1, 19 elite male race walkers, 14 days, 30 g/h against 60 to 90 g/h; total and upper gut symptoms significantly greater after the intervention in the high group; no change in breath hydrogen. Study 2, 18 elite male and female runners, 14 days, 30 g/h against 60 to 90 g/h hydrogel; total symptoms "similarly increased across exercise in both trials and groups"; 7 km time trial unchanged. Title: "Short-term very high carbohydrate diet and gut-training have minor effects on gastrointestinal status and performance in highly trained endurance athletes." Australian Institute of Sport: [GK, from the author list].
  - The reviewers attribute King's null result to the athletes being elite and the exercise load being lower.
  - Svendsen 2016: 13 highly trained male cyclists, 8 days, 60 g/h against 20 g/h; outcomes were immune markers, not symptoms. This is the one cycling-only study.
  - The review gives Cox 2010's time-trial improvement as about 9%; the paper's abstract says about 6%. The draft uses the abstract's figure. **Unresolved; needs the Cox paper.**
  - Authors: Martinez, Mika, Biesiekierski, Costa, all Monash. Costa is senior author of Costa 2017 and Miall 2018. No conflicts declared.
- Not used: the claim that runners get more gut trouble than cyclists. Pfeiffer's own figures (4% in both the marathon and the cycle race) do not support it cleanly.

## Mouth rinse

- Carter 2004: seven men and two women; set work of about 914 kJ; 6.4% maltodextrin rinsed for 5 s at every 12.5% of the trial, spat out; 59.57 against 61.37 minutes; 259 against 252 W; no difference in heart rate or RPE. Authors: "an increase in central drive or motivation rather than having any metabolic cause". [Abs]
- Rinse effects most consistent when fasted or depleted; no dose-response established. [FT, Plews 2026]

## Train low and under-eating

- Impey 2018: signalling up in 73% of 11 studies, gene expression in 75% of 12, oxidative enzymes in 78% of 9; performance improved in 37% and unchanged in 63% of 11. [Abs]
- Gejl & Nybo 2021: nine studies in athletes with VO₂max of at least 55 (women) or 60 (men); no overall effect, standardised mean difference 0.17 (−0.15 to 0.49), P = 0.29. [Abs]
- "Riders short of carbohydrate cannot train as hard": the abstract's "compromised training quality"; stated as the likely reason. [Abs, Gejl & Nybo 2021]
- Mountjoy 2023: REDs, low energy availability, female and male athletes. [Abs] "A cyclist trying to lose weight while training hard is an obvious candidate": mine.

## Water (added 11 October 2026, at George's request) [all Abs]

- The −2% basis of guidelines, still-air laboratories (wind under 12.9 km/h) and unblinded participants: Wall 2015's own background statement.
- Goulet 2011: five articles, all cycling time trials, 13 effect estimates, 39 subjects; mean 26 °C; dehydration of 2.20 ± 1.0% body weight against 0.44%: +0.06 ± 2.72% (p = 0.94); drinking to thirst +5.2% against below thirst (p = 0.01) and +2.4% against above thirst (p = 0.40, not used).
- Wall 2015: 10 cyclists dehydrated to −3%, then reinfused with saline to 0, −2 or −3%, participant-blinded, randomised; 25 km time trial at 33 °C, 40% humidity, wind 32 km/h; no differences except rectal temperature (38.9 against 38.6 °C beyond 17 km). Australian group: [GK].
- James 2017: seven active men (VO₂peak 48), single-blind, water by gastric tube, 34 °C; 8 × 15 min preload then a 15-minute performance test; body mass loss 2.4% against 0.1%; work 152 ± 24 against 165 ± 22 kJ. British group: [GK].
- The comparison of the two studies and the remark that still, hot air "describes an indoor trainer": mine.
- Almond 2005: 766 enrolled, 488 gave blood at the finish of the 2002 Boston Marathon; 13% hyponatraemia (135 mmol/L or less), 0.6% critical (120 or less); associated on multivariate analysis with weight gain (OR 4.2), racing time over 4 h, BMI extremes; not with fluid composition. "An important cause of race-related death".
- The Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia consensus (Hew-Butler 2015) has no abstract in the database; not used.

## Salt [all Abs]

- Hoffman & Stuempfle 2015: 161 km Western States Endurance Run; sodium supplements used by 93.9%; hyponatraemia in 6.6% of finishers sampled; no difference in sodium intake rate between hyponatraemic and other finishers; "overhydration is the primary characteristic".
- Schwellnus 2011: 210 Ironman triathletes; 43 cramped, 166 did not; no differences in electrolytes or body weight change; independent risk factors were faster race time and a history of cramping. South African group: [GK].
- Del Coso 2016: 26 triathletes matched and randomly placed into salt or cellulose groups; real half-Ironman; total race time lower with salt (P = 0.04). The size of the difference is not in the abstract.
- "A salty drink may be easier to keep drinking": [GK].

## Caffeine [all Abs]

- Southward 2018: 46 randomised placebo-controlled studies; moderate doses 3 to 6 mg/kg; mean power +3.03 ± 3.07%; time-trial time 2.22 ± 2.59% better; two studies slower, five lower power.
- Gonçalves 2017: 40 male endurance-trained cyclists in tertiles of habitual intake (58, 143, 351 mg/day); 6 mg/kg caffeine, placebo, or nothing; 29.92, 30.81, 31.14 min; no influence of habit. Brazilian group: [GK]. (31.14 − 30.81) / (31.14 − 29.92) = 0.27, "about a quarter": [Own].
- 70 kg × 3 to 6 mg/kg = 210 to 420 mg [Own]; "two to four strong coffees", "taken about an hour before", and the effect on sleep: [GK].

## Ketones

- What ketones are: [GK].
- Cox PJ 2016: five studies, 39 athletes; ketone ester; "improves physical endurance by altering fuel competition". No performance figure in the abstract; none quoted. Oxford: [GK]. [Abs]
- Leckey 2017: 10 elite male cyclists, randomised crossover, about 31 km simulated time trial; diester 2 × 250 mg/kg; 2 ± 1% impairment with gut discomfort and higher perceived effort. [Abs] A commentary (Stubbs et al. 2018, Front Physiol) contests it; "the product's defenders have said so" rests on that title and my memory of its argument. Australian group: [GK].
- Valenzuela 2020: 13 randomised trials; g = −0.05 (−0.30 to 0.20); no effect for esters or salts; events up to about 50 minutes. [Abs]
- Poffé 2019: fit men, 3 weeks, 6 days a week, two sessions a day; ketone ester (n = 9) or control (n = 9) after each session; sustainable training load in week 3 and power in the final 30 min of a 2 h session both 15% higher. [Abs] Leuven: [GK].
- UCI 2025: Drugs chapter notes. No teams or riders named.

## Beetroot juice and tart cherry

- Nitrate mechanism (conversion to nitric oxide, vessel widening, lower oxygen cost): [GK, with Lansley's abstract for the oxygen cost].
- Lansley 2011: nine club-level male cyclists, randomised crossover; 0.5 L beetroot juice (about 6.2 mmol nitrate) or nitrate-depleted juice about 2.5 h before; 4 km 2.8% faster, 16.1 km 2.7% faster (233 to 247 W). Exeter: [GK]. [Abs]
- Senefeld 2020: 80 studies; d = 0.174 (0.120 to 0.229); not observed in women-only studies (n = 6) or in well-trained endurance athletes (VO₂max ≥ 65; n = 26; d = 0.021). [Abs]
- The finish-line drink is tart cherry juice: a professional team's website, found by web search; the page was not opened and is promotional. Corroborated by several cycling news sites in the same search. [Rep]
- Bell 2014: 16 trained cyclists in two equal groups; 30 mL Montmorency concentrate or placebo twice a day for 7 days; 109-minute simulated race on days 5, 6, 7; lipid hydroperoxides, IL-6 and hsCRP lower with cherry; no effect on other markers including CK. [Abs]
- Daab 2026: 19 trials in trained athletes; strength (MVC) recovery improved at all time points; CRP reduced to 48 h; no significant pooled effect on soreness, CK, IL-6, TNF-α; I² 69 to 93%; certainty very low to moderate. [Abs]
- Gomez-Cabrera 2008: double-blind, 14 men trained for 8 weeks, 5 given 1 g vitamin C daily; 24 rats; vitamin C "significantly hampered endurance capacity" and reduced PGC-1, NRF-1 and Tfam expression. **The abstract does not say which results are human.** [Abs]
- That cherry juice suits stage races better than training: my inference from the above. Not tested.

## High-fat diets (20 October 2026)

One paragraph added to "Eating less on purpose", pointing to Chapter 7's account of Burke 2017 [Abs]: elite race walkers, 3 weeks of hard training; ketogenic LCHF raised fat oxidation to 1.57 g/min but 10 km times did not improve (-1.6%, 90% CI -8.5 to 5.3) while high and periodised carbohydrate groups improved 6.6% and 5.3%. Full notes in `easy-riding/research.md`, "Fat burning for performance". Grade B: one controlled trial in elite athletes, supported by Havemann 2006 (cyclists, sprint power lower after fat adaptation).
