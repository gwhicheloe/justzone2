## The question

How much does eating on the bike help, does eating very large amounts help more, can the gut be trained to take them, and is fuelling the reason racing has got faster?

## The argument

1. Eating a lot on the bike is not new. Five Tour riders were recorded at 94 g of carbohydrate an hour in a study published in 1989.
2. Carbohydrate during rides of two hours or more improves performance (grade A). The dose-response curve is steep at first and flat by 60 to 80 g/h.
3. Glucose absorption is capped at about 60 g/h by one gut transporter. Adding fructose, which uses another, lifts what can be burned to about 90 g/h and improved time-trial performance in one small trial (B).
4. Above 90 g/h the extra is burned in place of fat, spares no glycogen, and has not been shown to improve performance on the day (C). It may help recovery between stages; untested.
5. Nobody has tested whether fuelling explains faster racing, and it is hard to see how anyone could. Too much else changed at the same time.
6. The gut can probably be trained in the sense of comfort: two weeks of practice roughly halved symptoms and malabsorption in recreational runners (B, one laboratory); a trial in elite race walkers and runners found no benefit; cyclists barely tested. That practice raises how much carbohydrate can be absorbed and burned rests on one study of 16 cyclists (C), and that study found no performance benefit.
7. Training with low carbohydrate boosts molecular signals and does not improve performance in trained athletes (B). Chronic under-eating is harmful.
8. For an amateur: scale intake to the work done; 60 g/h gets most of the benefit; 90 for long hard days with mixed sugars; practise for two weeks before an event.

9. (Added 11 October at George's request.) Water: moderate dehydration costs less than the old 2% rule implied, the two blinded studies disagree, and over-drinking is the dangerous error (C). Salt does not prevent cramp or hyponatraemia (C for any performance benefit). Caffeine works at 3 to 6 mg/kg whatever your coffee habit (A). Ketones before exercise do nothing (B); the recovery use rests on one study (C). Beetroot juice has a small effect in recreational men and none in well-trained athletes (B). The red finish-line drink is tart cherry juice: it lowers some recovery markers, with low-certainty evidence, and may be the wrong thing after ordinary training (C).
