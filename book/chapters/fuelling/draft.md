# Chapter 14: Fuelling

In a study published in 1989, researchers from Maastricht followed riders from one of the leading professional teams through the Tour de France, 22 days and about 4,000 kilometres, and had them write down everything they ate [@saris1989]. Eight riders were asked and five agreed to keep a food diary. One crashed out after four days and a team-mate took over, so four riders have a complete record.

They took in an average of 24.7 megajoules a day, which is about 5,900 kilocalories, and 32.4 megajoules on the hardest day in the mountains. Almost half of it, 49%, was eaten on the bike. Across 115 hours of racing that came to 94 grams of carbohydrate an hour. The largest single source of their energy was sweet cakes, eaten during the stages. A maltodextrin drink came second and bread third [@saris1989].

That figure is worth holding against the way fuelling is talked about now. The usual account is that professionals have recently learned to eat far more during races than riders once did, 100 or 120 grams of carbohydrate an hour from drinks and gels designed for the purpose, and that this is one of the main reasons racing has become faster. A 2026 review of the subject says the change "has been widely described as a 'carbohydrate revolution'" [@plews2026].

The number came from diaries, and a diary is not accurate to the gram, although these were checked each week by a nutritionist against what the team had supplied, and the drinks came in standard bottles [@saris1989]. It should make anyone cautious about the word revolution. Riders in the Tour were eating a great deal on the bike in the 1980s, and much of it was cake.

The Durability chapter covered what eating does to the fade late in a long ride. This chapter takes the rest: how much carbohydrate helps, whether very large amounts help more, whether the gut can be trained to take them, and what the evidence says about water, salt, caffeine, ketones, beetroot juice and the red drink riders are handed at the finish.

## What a ride costs

A power meter makes the cost of a ride easy to estimate. The body turns roughly a quarter of the energy it burns into work at the pedals, and a kilocalorie is a little over four kilojoules, so the two factors nearly cancel. A ride that records 2,000 kilojoules of work has cost about 2,000 kilocalories.

Three hours at 200 watts is 2,160 kilojoules. A gram of carbohydrate supplies about 4 kilocalories, so 60 grams an hour for those three hours puts back 720 kilocalories, a third of what was spent. At 90 grams an hour it is half.

Nobody replaces everything on the bike, and nobody needs to. The body carries far more energy as fat than any ride could use. What it runs short of is carbohydrate, which is stored as glycogen in the muscles and the liver in amounts that a few hours of hard riding can empty. The harder the riding, the larger the share of the fuel that is carbohydrate. In one of the trials described below, highly trained cyclists riding at the top of Zone 2 after an overnight fast burned a little over 2 grams a minute of their own stored carbohydrate, about 130 grams an hour, on top of what they were drinking [@podlogar2022].

Professionals in a grand tour are at the far end of this. The researchers on the 1980s Tour estimated that the riders spent 25.4 megajoules a day, about 6,100 kilocalories, and their body weight did not change over the three weeks. They ate 12.3 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight a day [@saris1989]. Three decades later, investigators weighed everything nine riders on a WorldTour team ate through the three weeks of the Vuelta a España and found an average of 12.5 [@muros2019]. For a 69-kilogram rider, the average in the Tour study, that is about 850 grams of carbohydrate a day, every day for three weeks.

## How it works: the limit is in the gut wall

Carbohydrate eaten on the bike has to leave the stomach, cross the wall of the small intestine into the blood, and be taken up by the muscle. Researchers can follow it by labelling the carbohydrate in a drink with a traceable form of carbon and measuring how much of that carbon comes out in the rider's breath, which shows how much of the drink has actually been burned.

Such studies kept finding a ceiling. However much glucose a rider drank, the body burned no more than about 60 grams of it an hour. Intakes of 144 and 180 grams an hour did not raise the figure much [@jeukendrup2017]. The stomach was emptying fast enough and the muscle could have used more, so by elimination the limit was absorption. Glucose crosses the gut wall through a transport protein called SGLT1, and at about a gram a minute those transporters are working at capacity [@jeukendrup2017].

Fructose crosses by a different transporter, GLUT5. In 2004 Asker Jeukendrup's group gave eight trained cyclists drinks containing glucose alone, or glucose with fructose, during two hours of riding. With glucose alone, at either of two doses, the peak rate at which the drink was burned was about 0.8 grams a minute. With the mixture it was 1.26 [@jentjens2004]. A second route through the gut wall let more sugar through.

Four years later the same group tested whether that mattered for performance. Eight cyclists rode for two hours and then did a time trial lasting about an hour. On a glucose and fructose drink they finished it 8% faster than on glucose alone, and 19% faster than on water [@currell2008]. {grade B}

This is the basis of the standard advice for long events, which is up to 90 grams an hour from a mixture of sugars. The 2026 review treats 90 grams as the upper limit of established guidelines and anything above it as "ultra-high" [@plews2026].

## Does more help?

The largest trial of dose was run across four research sites and published in 2013. Fifty-one cyclists and triathletes each rode for two hours at a steady, fairly hard pace and then did a 20-kilometre time trial, on four occasions. Twelve drinks were tested, from 10 to 120 grams of carbohydrate an hour in steps of 10, along with a placebo, and neither the riders nor the testers knew which drink was which [@smith2013].

Performance rose with dose, steeply at first. Riders were 1% faster than on placebo at 9 grams an hour, 2% at 19, 3% at 31 and 4% at 48. The curve peaked at 78 grams an hour with a gain of 4.7%, and above that the benefit shrank [@smith2013].

Drawn from the modelled values in the abstract of Smith and colleagues (2013); the abstract does not give values above 78 grams an hour.](figures/carb-curve.svg)

The 2026 trial described in the Durability chapter has the same shape. Going from nothing to 60 grams an hour saved 20 watts of critical power after a three-hour ride, and doubling the dose to 120 saved 9 more [@norte2026]. That carbohydrate during a ride of two hours or more improves performance is as well established as anything in sports nutrition. {grade A}

Above 90 grams an hour the evidence thins quickly. In 2022 a trial compared 120 grams an hour with 90 in eleven highly trained cyclists during three-hour rides. At 120 the riders burned more of what they drank, 1.51 grams a minute against 1.29. But they burned just as much of their own stored carbohydrate as before [@podlogar2022]. The extra sugar displaced fat as a fuel and spared no glycogen. And less of it was used: by the 2026 reviewers' calculation about 86% of the carbohydrate drunk at 90 grams an hour was burned and about 76% at 120, which leaves some 30 grams an hour unaccounted for. They suggest much of that is still sitting in the gut [@plews2026].

The study most often cited in favour of 120 grams was run during a mountain marathon. Twenty elite runners, who had trained their guts beforehand, were randomised to 60, 90 or 120 grams an hour. A day after the race the 120 group had lower blood markers of muscle damage and reported a lower overall load during the race [@viribay2020]. The study did not show that they ran faster [@plews2026], and with 20 runners split three ways it had little chance of detecting it if they had.

The 2026 review, by a group based in Auckland, went through all of this and concluded that intakes above 90 grams an hour remain "poorly evidenced", with support "largely derived from anecdotal reports and elite athlete practice rather than controlled research" [@plews2026]. Two trials from one laboratory, comparing doses from 60 to about 110 grams an hour, found no significant difference in time-trial performance between them. {grade C}

The authors do not dismiss the practice. They point out that people differ: in one study, riders all given 120 grams an hour burned anything from about 1.3 to 1.9 grams a minute, so a few could use nearly all of it and most could not. And they suggest that the real benefit of eating a great deal during a stage may arrive the next day, as better recovery. That would matter in a three-week race, and no laboratory trial of a single ride would detect it [@plews2026].

A second review, published the same year by seven British researchers, reads the same studies and reaches a different recommendation. It argues that the upper limit could now rise from 90 to 120 grams an hour, at least for trained athletes, because more of the carbohydrate is burned at the higher intake [@morton2026]. The two reviews agree on the facts more than their conclusions suggest. This one states that the intakes of 120 to 200 grams an hour now being tried by athletes are "not yet substantiated by current scientific research", and concedes that a definitive study of dose and performance "is yet to be completed". Its case for 120 grams rests on how much is burned, and the Auckland review's case against rests on the absence of a performance gain. A reader can hold both: more is burned, and nobody has shown it makes you faster.

### Reading the research: what the fast riders eat

In 2012 a group surveyed 221 athletes after Ironman triathlons, a marathon, a long cycle race and professional stage races, asking what they had eaten and what stomach trouble they had had. Intakes ranged from 6 to 136 grams of carbohydrate an hour. In the Ironman races, the athletes who ate more finished faster, and the correlation was a fairly strong one [@pfeiffer2012].

It is tempting to read that as proof that eating more makes you faster. The survey can't show it. The faster athletes in an Ironman are the fitter and more experienced ones. By my reasoning, not the survey's, they are working at a lower fraction of their capacity at any given speed, which leaves more blood flow for the gut, and they have had years to practise eating in races. Speed may be what allows the eating. The same survey found that higher intakes went with more nausea and flatulence [@pfeiffer2012], so the athletes eating the most were not having an easy time of it.

The argument that professionals eat 120 grams an hour and professionals are fast has the same weakness, and so does the argument that winners eat more than the riders they beat. Chapter 4 calls this kind of evidence observation. It can suggest a cause and it can't establish one.

It is also worth knowing who is involved. The review on training the gut that this chapter draws on appeared in a journal supplement supported by the research institute of a sports drink company, and its author received a fee for attending the meeting behind it [@jeukendrup2017]. The carbohydrate in the 2026 dose trial was supplied by a supplement company [@norte2026]. That does not make the findings wrong, and the big dose trial was double-blind. Of the seven authors of the review that argues for raising the limit to 120 grams, five declare a relationship with one sports nutrition company, and one of them is employed by it [@morton2026]. The lead author of the more sceptical review declares a post with a company that sells other supplements [@plews2026]. None of this settles who is right. It does mean that the questions which get studied are often the ones a manufacturer would like answered, and that a reader should know where a recommendation to eat more of a product comes from.

## Is this why racing is faster?

I could not find a study that tests the claim, and it is hard to see how one could be done. Fuelling did not change in isolation. Over the same years bikes, tyres, clothing and riding positions changed, and so did training, altitude camps, the heat work described in the Heat training chapter and the age at which riders turn professional. A race has no control group, and there is no way to hold everything else still.

What can be said is narrower. Carbohydrate during long rides improves performance, and that has been known for decades. Riders in the Tour were recorded eating 94 grams an hour in the 1980s, and their total for the day, 12.3 grams per kilogram, is almost the same as the 12.5 measured on a WorldTour team three decades later [@saris1989; @muros2019]. Those are two teams in two different races, one recorded by diary and one by weighing, so the match should not be pressed.

The hourly figures that have been published tell a similar story with a recent upturn. The British review, some of whose authors work with professional teams, says that professional cyclists' intakes have usually been reported at 60 to 90 grams an hour. It gives Chris Froome's figures across nine stages of the 2018 Giro d'Italia, which he won: between 52 and 96 grams an hour, with the highest on the hardest day. It also passes on a press report that a stage winner at the 2025 Tour took about 116, and reports that some riders are said to be training their guts to tolerate 200 or more [@morton2026]. So the leading edge has moved up in the last few years, from the nineties to something over a hundred, if the press figures are right. That is a smaller change than the word revolution implies, and the 200 figure is hearsay. The controlled trials do not show that going from 90 to 120 makes anyone faster on the day [@plews2026]. So if modern fuelling has made racing faster, the laboratory evidence suggests it did not do so through a higher hourly number in the final of a single stage.

It might still be true by the recovery route. A rider who eats more on Tuesday starts Wednesday with more glycogen, and over three weeks that could add up. Both reviews say as much. The British one suggests that higher intakes help riders match what they eat to what they spend each day, "even if this does not necessarily confer an immediate performance benefit" [@morton2026].

It might also be true through consistency. An average from a handful of riders on one leading team says nothing about how many riders in the race ate that much, on how many days, or how well it stayed down. If every rider now eats what only the best-organised did then, the race is faster without the ceiling having moved. Both are reasonable guesses and neither has been tested. {grade C}

## Can the gut be trained?

The stomach can, and the best demonstration comes from outside sport. Competitive eaters train for weeks by drinking large volumes of fluid and eating progressively larger amounts of the food they will compete on. The stomach learns to stretch further, and a full stomach comes to feel less full. When Jeukendrup reviewed the subject in 2017 the record stood at 69 hot dogs, with buns, in ten minutes [@jeukendrup2017].

The athletic version is less dramatic and works the same way. In one study he describes, runners practised drinking at a rate that matched their sweat losses during 90-minute runs, and their stomach comfort improved over repeated sessions, although the rate at which the stomach emptied did not measurably change. What changed was how the fullness was tolerated [@jeukendrup2017].

Diet changes how fast the stomach empties, and the change is specific to the nutrient. Volunteers whose diet was supplemented with 400 grams of glucose a day for three days emptied a glucose drink from the stomach faster afterwards, with a half-emptying time of about 21 minutes against 29, while a protein drink emptied no faster than before [@jeukendrup2017]. Three days was enough.

The intestine is where the evidence gets thinner. In animals the answer is clear: feed more carbohydrate and the gut wall makes more of the SGLT1 transporter. Mice had nearly twice as much after two weeks on a high-carbohydrate diet, and changes have been seen in other species within days [@jeukendrup2017]. Nobody has measured this directly in a human athlete, because it would mean taking samples of the intestinal wall. "No direct human studies exist," the review says. The human evidence is indirect, and the most important piece of it is one study in cyclists.

### One study, up close: 28 days of eating on the bike

In 2010 a group at the Australian Institute of Sport led by Gregory Cox pair-matched 16 trained cyclists and triathletes and randomly allocated one of each pair to a high-carbohydrate group and the other to a low-carbohydrate group, for 28 days of their normal training, about 16 hours a week [@cox2010; @jeukendrup2017].

Both groups ate the same moderate-carbohydrate base diet. The high group was given extra carbohydrate, mostly as a glucose drink, before, during and after every training session. The other group was given a supplement of fat and protein with the same energy content, eaten after training. The difference between the groups was whether the gut met a stream of sugar during exercise every day for four weeks. Daily carbohydrate intake averaged 8.5 grams per kilogram of body weight in one group and 5.3 in the other [@jeukendrup2017].

Before and after, every rider did a test ride of 100 minutes at a steady pace followed by a time trial, drinking a glucose solution labelled with a tracer so that the amount burned could be measured. In the high group, the amount of the drink burned over the 100 minutes rose from 54.6 grams to 63.6. In the low group it did not change [@cox2010].

That is a rise of about a sixth in how much swallowed glucose reached the muscles and was used, after four weeks of practice. The likeliest explanation is that the gut wall had made more transporters, as it does in animals. The study did not measure that.

The performance result is less convenient. Time-trial performance improved by about 6% after the four weeks of training, and it improved by the same amount in both groups [@cox2010]. The riders who had trained their guts absorbed more and went no faster than the riders who hadn't.

There are limits on both findings. Eight riders a group is a small study by the standards of Chapter 4. The test drink was glucose alone, so the study cannot say whether training raises the higher ceiling that glucose and fructose mixtures reach. I have read this paper's abstract and Jeukendrup's detailed account of it, not the paper itself. And sixteen years later the 2026 reviewers note that its central finding "has not been consistently replicated" [@plews2026]. {grade C}

### Symptoms are another matter

Whether or not practice lets you absorb more, there is better evidence that it makes eating on the move more comfortable.

A group at Monash University in Melbourne ran two trials in recreational runners. In the first, 25 runners did a two-hour run while eating 90 grams of carbohydrate an hour, followed by a one-hour distance test. They were then randomly assigned to two weeks of repeated practice with carbohydrate gels, with carbohydrate foods, or with a placebo, and tested again. Total gut symptoms during the second test were 60% lower in the gel group and 63% lower in the food group. They were also 25% lower in the placebo group, so some of the improvement came simply from doing the test a second time. The distance covered in the hour rose by 5.2% and 4.3% in the two carbohydrate groups and fell by 2.1% on placebo [@costa2017; @martinez2023].

The second trial was blinded. Eighteen runners practised daily for two weeks, during an hour's running, with either 90 grams of carbohydrate an hour or a placebo, without being told which. Total symptoms fell by 61% in the carbohydrate group and 2% in the placebo group. Breath hydrogen, a sign of sugar reaching the large intestine unabsorbed, fell from a peak of 13 parts per million to 6 with carbohydrate and did not change with placebo [@miall2018; @martinez2023].

A third trial, from the Australian Institute of Sport, did not find this. Nineteen elite race walkers and 18 elite runners spent two weeks training on either 30 grams of carbohydrate an hour or 60 to 90. In the race walkers on the higher intake, total gut symptoms were greater after the two weeks than before. In the runners, symptoms rose during exercise by the same amount in both groups. Breath hydrogen did not change, and neither did the runners' time-trial performance. The authors' own title describes the effects as "minor" [@martinez2023].

All three are in a 2023 systematic review, which found eight studies of gut training in total. It concludes that two weeks of repeated feeding reduces gut discomfort and malabsorption, and that stomach emptying does not change. It also notes that no standard protocol exists, that 79% of participants were men, and that most of the studies were in runners [@martinez2023]. Only one used cycling alone. The review was written by the Monash group, and its senior author is also the senior author of the two trials that found a benefit. That is normal in a small field, and the review reports the contrary trial fairly, but it means the positive case comes from one laboratory.

So the gut can probably be trained, in the sense that matters most to most riders. In recreational runners, two weeks of regularly eating during exercise made it more comfortable and reduced how much sugar passed through unabsorbed. {grade B} In elite athletes one trial found no such benefit, and in cyclists it has barely been tested. That practice raises the ceiling on how much carbohydrate can be burned rests on one small study. {grade C} And nobody has shown that a trained gut makes a cyclist faster.

## Not only fuel

One finding does not fit the picture of carbohydrate as petrol. In 2004 nine endurance cyclists did a time trial lasting about an hour, twice. In one they rinsed a carbohydrate solution around the mouth for five seconds at intervals and spat it out. In the other they did the same with water. Nothing was swallowed either time. With the carbohydrate rinse they finished in 59.57 minutes against 61.37, at an average of 259 watts against 252 [@carter2004].

An hour is too short for a rider to run out of glycogen, and nothing reached the gut. The researchers concluded that receptors in the mouth detect carbohydrate and that the signal raises what the brain is prepared to ask of the muscles. That is the subject of Chapter 3: effort is regulated centrally, and it responds to information. The 2026 reviewers raise this as one possible explanation for why riders report feeling better on very high intakes, while noting that rinsing works most reliably in riders who have fasted, and that nobody has shown the effect grows with dose [@plews2026]. {grade B}

## Eating less on purpose

For some years the fashionable idea in sports nutrition ran the other way. Training with low glycogen, by riding before breakfast or withholding carbohydrate after a hard session, amplifies the molecular signals described in Chapter 2. A 2018 review counted the studies: training low increased cell signalling in 73% of them and raised oxidative enzymes in 78%. The same review counted performance, which improved in 37% of studies and did not change in 63% [@impey2018].

A meta-analysis in 2021 pooled the nine training studies done in well-trained endurance athletes. Periodically restricting carbohydrate had no overall effect on performance compared with doing the same training well fed [@gejl2021]. {grade B}

This is Chapter 2's warning in a real case. A larger signal after a session did not become a larger adaptation over weeks, and the likely reason is that riders short of carbohydrate cannot train as hard. The Cox study points the same way from the other side. Its two groups ate very differently around training for a month and improved by the same amount [@cox2010]. Within the range those studies cover, how much carbohydrate you eat around training seems to matter less to how fit you get than either camp has claimed.

The real hazard is at the far end. Athletes who persistently eat less than their training costs develop a recognised syndrome, relative energy deficiency in sport, which affects men as well as women and damages both health and performance [@mountjoy2023]. A cyclist trying to lose weight while training hard is an obvious candidate.

## Water

For decades the standard advice was to drink enough to keep weight loss during exercise under 2% of body weight, because laboratory studies showed performance falling beyond that. In many of those studies the riders knew whether they had been allowed to drink, and they were tested in still air, which no cyclist outdoors experiences [@wall2015].

A 2011 meta-analysis took only studies that used cycling time trials. It found five, with 39 riders between them. Riders who finished an average of 2.2% lighter performed no differently from riders who had been kept topped up: the difference was 0.06%. Drinking according to thirst gave better results than drinking less than thirst asked for [@goulet2011].

In 2015 an Australian group removed the rider's knowledge from the experiment. Ten cyclists were dehydrated by 3% of body weight and then given back all, a third or none of the fluid through a drip, without knowing which. They then rode a 25-kilometre time trial at 33 °C with a 32 km/h headwind. Performance did not differ between the three conditions [@wall2015].

Two years later a British group did the blinding another way, by putting water into the stomach through a tube, and got the opposite result. Seven active men, about 2.4% dehydrated and at 34 °C, did 152 kilojoules of work in a 15-minute test against 165 when hydrated [@james2017].

So two blinded studies disagree, and they had ten and seven people in them. They differed in ways that could matter: trained cyclists against active men, a strong headwind against less airflow, a time trial against a short all-out test. I would not claim more than this: moderate dehydration costs a fit rider less than the old advice implied, it probably costs more in heat and still air, which describes an indoor trainer, and thirst is a reasonable guide. {grade C}

Drinking too much is the more dangerous error. At the 2002 Boston Marathon, researchers took blood from 488 finishers. Thirteen per cent had hyponatraemia, a blood sodium concentration low enough to be abnormal, and 0.6% had it to a critical degree. The runners at risk were those who had gained weight during the race by drinking more than they lost, and those who took more than four hours. Whether they drank water or sports drink made no difference [@almond2005]. Hyponatraemia can kill. A slow rider on a long cool day who drinks at every opportunity is more exposed to it than to dehydration.

## Salt

Electrolyte tablets and salt capsules are sold on two promises: that they prevent hyponatraemia and that they prevent cramp. The evidence for both is poor.

At a 161-kilometre mountain run in California, 94% of the runners surveyed were taking sodium supplements, and 6.6% of the finishers tested still had hyponatraemia. Those who developed it had taken sodium at the same rate as those who did not. What marked them out was over-drinking [@hoffman2015]. Low blood sodium in endurance events comes from too much water, and salt tablets do not correct that.

On cramp, a South African group followed 210 triathletes through an Ironman. Forty-three cramped. Their blood electrolytes and weight changes before and after the race were no different from those of the athletes who did not cramp. What predicted cramp was a faster race pace and a history of cramping [@schwellnus2011]. That points to a muscle pushed beyond what it is trained for, and away from salt.

One randomised trial found a benefit. Twenty-six triathletes took salt capsules or a placebo during a real half-Ironman, and the salt group finished faster [@delcoso2016]. With 26 athletes split between two groups and a p-value of 0.04, it is the kind of result Chapter 4 advises waiting to see repeated. {grade C}

Sodium in a drink does no harm, and a salty drink may be easier to keep drinking. It is not protection against either of the things it is sold for.

## Caffeine

Caffeine is the best-supported performance aid a cyclist can legally buy. A 2018 meta-analysis pooled 46 randomised, placebo-controlled trials of endurance time trials. At moderate doses of 3 to 6 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, caffeine raised average power by about 3% and shortened finishing time by about 2%. The response varied, and two of the studies found riders slower [@southward2018]. {grade A}

A common belief is that regular coffee drinkers get nothing from it. A Brazilian trial tested that in 40 trained cyclists, divided by their habitual intake into low, moderate and high consumers. Each did a time trial of about half an hour three times: after caffeine, after a placebo and after nothing. On caffeine they averaged 29.92 minutes, against 30.81 on placebo and 31.14 with nothing, and the benefit was the same in all three groups [@goncalves2017]. The gap between the placebo and nothing is worth a second look after Chapter 4: a capsule with nothing in it produced about a quarter of the gain that caffeine did.

For a 70-kilogram rider, 3 to 6 milligrams per kilogram is 210 to 420 milligrams, which is roughly two to four strong coffees. It is usually taken about an hour before the effort. The costs are the familiar ones, and the one that matters most to training is sleep.

## Ketones

Ketones are fuel molecules the liver makes from fat when carbohydrate is scarce, as in fasting. A ketone ester drink raises their level in the blood without the fasting. The idea became famous through a 2016 paper from Oxford, reporting five studies in 39 athletes, which concluded that the drink changed which fuel the muscles used and improved endurance performance [@coxp2016].

The following year an Australian group gave a ketone drink or a placebo to ten professional cyclists before a simulated 31-kilometre time trial. On the ketone drink they were 2% slower, with stomach discomfort and a higher sense of effort [@leckey2017]. It was a different ketone compound from the Oxford one, and the product's defenders have said so. But when a 2020 meta-analysis pooled all 13 randomised trials of ketone supplements taken before exercise, of either type, it found no effect on performance [@valenzuela2020]. {grade B}

The use that has survived is after exercise. A group at Leuven in Belgium put 18 fit men through three weeks of deliberately excessive training, two sessions a day, with a ketone drink or a control drink after each session. By the third week the ketone group was sustaining a higher training load, and their power in the last half hour of a standard two-hour ride was 15% higher than the control group's [@poffe2019]. That is a striking result from nine people a group in one laboratory, and it is waiting for independent repetition. {grade C}

The sport's governing body reviewed the evidence in 2025, said it found none that was compelling, and recommended that riders not take ketones, without banning them. The Drugs chapter has the details.

## Beetroot juice and the red drink at the finish

These are two different things, taken for different reasons.

Beetroot juice is taken before a ride, for its nitrate, which the body converts into a compound that widens blood vessels and appears to lower the oxygen cost of exercise. The trial that started the interest came from Exeter in 2011. Nine club-level cyclists drank half a litre of beetroot juice, or the same juice with the nitrate removed, two and a half hours before time trials of 4 and 16.1 kilometres. With nitrate they were 2.8% and 2.7% faster, and their power over the longer distance rose from 233 to 247 watts [@lansley2011].

Nine years and many trials later, a meta-analysis of 80 studies found that the effect is real and small, and that it depends on who drinks it. In recreationally active men it was clear. In well-trained endurance athletes, with a VO₂max of 65 or more, it was absent. In the six studies done only in women it was not detectable [@senefeld2020]. A club rider might gain a little from beetroot juice before a race. {grade B} A professional almost certainly gains nothing, which makes it an odd thing to copy from one.

The red drink handed to riders as they cross the line is something else: tart cherry juice, as at least one professional team has explained on its own website [@efcherry]. It is taken for recovery. Tart cherries are rich in plant compounds that reduce inflammation, and the juice also contains sugar, which a rider needs at that moment anyway.

The trial closest to cycling gave 16 trained cyclists cherry concentrate or a placebo twice a day for a week, with a hard 109-minute simulated race on each of the last three days. Blood markers of inflammation and oxidative stress rose less in the cherry group [@bell2014]. The abstract reports no measure of how well they rode. A 2026 meta-analysis of 19 trials in athletes found that cherry juice improved the recovery of muscle strength and lowered one marker of inflammation, and had no significant effect on soreness or on the usual blood marker of muscle damage. The authors rate the certainty of the evidence as very low to moderate [@daab2026]. {grade C}

There is a reason to be careful with it in training. The inflammation and oxidative stress that follow a hard session are part of the signal described in Chapter 2. In a small study in men, backed by experiments in rats, a gram of vitamin C a day, another antioxidant, hampered the gains from endurance training and suppressed the molecular signals that build mitochondria [@gomezcabrera2008]. Nobody has shown that cherry juice does the same. But the logic of taking it is strongest in a stage race, where the aim is to be recovered by tomorrow, and weakest after an ordinary training ride, where the aim is to adapt.

## What to do with it

**Scale it to the ride.** Under about 90 minutes at an easy pace there is no need to eat on the bike; that is roughly where critical power began to slide in the trial described in the Durability chapter [@clark2019a]. For rides of two hours or more, eat from early on.

**Sixty grams an hour gets most of what is available.** In the largest trial the first 50 grams an hour bought four of the 4.7 percentage points on offer [@smith2013]. Sixty grams is roughly two gels and a banana, or a large bottle of sports drink and a bar. Check the labels, because products vary.

**Go to 90 only for long, hard days, and only with a mixture of sugars.** Above 60 grams an hour, glucose alone is absorbed no faster, and the excess is the likeliest cause of stomach trouble. Products that combine glucose or maltodextrin with fructose are made for this.

**Above 90 is unproven.** It may help a professional recover between stages. For an amateur doing one ride it has not been shown to add speed, and at lower power outputs less of it will be used. A rider averaging 150 watts spends about 540 kilocalories an hour, and 120 grams of carbohydrate would replace nearly all of it.

**Practise.** If you plan to eat a lot in an event, eat that way on your long training rides for at least the two weeks before. That is the dose in the trials that reduced symptoms. Use the same products you will use on the day.

**Don't under-eat to get fitter.** Training short of carbohydrate has not improved performance in trained athletes, and chronic under-eating causes harm.

**Drink to thirst.** That is enough on most rides. Drink more deliberately indoors and in heat, and never so much that you finish heavier than you started.

**Don't rely on salt** to prevent cramp or low blood sodium. Pace and training are the better explanation for cramp.

**Caffeine works.** Three milligrams per kilogram about an hour before a hard effort is the low end of the range that has been tested, and regular coffee drinkers benefit as much as anyone.

**Leave the rest to the professionals.** Ketones have not been shown to improve performance. Beetroot juice may be worth a small gain to a club rider before a race. Cherry juice is a recovery drink for multi-day events.

**Remember what sugar is.** The 2026 reviewers warn that a habit of high carbohydrate intake around every session is a different thing from fuelling a race. They cite a study in which about 30% of athletes on a moderately raised carbohydrate intake showed fasting blood glucose in the pre-diabetic range within a month, reversibly [@plews2026]. Fuel the long and hard rides. An easy hour does not need a gel.
