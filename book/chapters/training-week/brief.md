## Part V's argument, in one place (the same summary opens all four briefs)

- **Chapter 18, The training week:** build the week in order: two hard sessions, at least 48 hours apart; a long ride if your events need it and the week holds it; easy riding at the top of Zone 2 for everything else; rest where life needs it. The hard dose stays at about 50 to 60 minutes of work a week from 4 hours to 12, and the easy hours carry the growth. Across a season, no rule for building volume has been shown to work (the 10% rule failed its trial), easier weeks are convention, blocks have weak support, the order of sessions doesn't matter, and a two-week taper that cuts volume by about half and keeps the intensity is the best-supported way to arrive fresh. Load ratios that promise to predict injury do not stand up.
- **Chapter 19, Special cases:** the rules hold for everyone; what changes is degree. Masters riders lose fitness with age even when they keep training hard, but less if they keep the intensity, and their heart-rate zones move down. Women are under-studied; the cycle's effect on performance is trivial on average; the one meta-analysis of trainability found men gained more VO₂max. Indoors, riders produce about a fifth less power at the same effort, and heat and still air push heart rate up.
- **Chapter 20, Knowing it's working:** the best signals are cheap and repeated: power at a fixed heart rate on a standard ride, best efforts from ordinary riding, drift on long rides, and how you feel. A single reading can't show a real change; a month of them can. HRV-guided training helps by a small margin at most; watch estimates of VO₂max are right on average and wrong for individuals; fitness-and-fatigue charts are model outputs with uncertain settings. Fitness built recently goes quickly when you stop; one hard session a week keeps most of it.
- **Chapter 21, The verdict:** not just Zone 2. For a rider on six to ten hours: two hard sessions and the rest easy, mostly Zone 2, ridden regularly. Graded claim by claim.

## The question

What does a sensible week look like at 4, 8 and 12 hours, and how should it change across a season?

## The argument (to approve)

1. The order to build a week in, each rule traced to its chapter (How many hard sessions; Designing intervals; What easy riding does; Durability; Why elite athletes ride easy).
2. Three worked weeks (figure), my construction. The hard dose barely changes (51.5, 51.5 and 59.5 minutes of work); the share of time that is hard falls from 21% to 8%.
3. The website planner's rules, briefly, and where the book's evidence differs (4 × 8 rather than 4 × 4; threshold as the second session is grade C).
4. Building volume: the 10% rule failed its one trial (Buist 2008, close-up, trial table). Runners, novices, injury; the bike is kinder.
5. Reading the research: a ratio that correlates with itself (the acute:chronic workload ratio: Gabbett 2016's claim, Impellizzeri 2020's critique, Lolli 2019's title). Explained with arithmetic.
6. Monotony (Foster 1998), as a reason the hard–easy week is a good shape. Grade C.
7. Across a season: easier weeks (no trial found; convention); blocks (Mølmen 2019: small effects, low-quality studies; grade C); order doesn't matter (Sylta 2016; grade B); taper (Bosquet 2007; grade B). Season figure, an illustration.
8. When the week goes wrong: drop easy riding first, keep one hard session, don't make up missed sessions (Hickson; Spiering).
