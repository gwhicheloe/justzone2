# Chapter 17: The training week

Build the week in this order. Put in two hard sessions first, at least 48 hours apart and ideally 72 [@stanley2013]. Add a long ride if your events are long and the week has room for it. Fill the rest with easy riding near the top of Zone 2. Leave the remaining days empty.

The hard sessions produced most of the improvement in Part III's trials on a few hours a week [@seiler2013]. Planning them first protects their spacing and leaves the easy riding to fit around them [@stanley2013].

## The order to build it in

**The hard sessions.** Two a week is the best-supported default for a rider on six to ten hours. The reasons are in Chapter 12. Two sessions build about as much as three over a few weeks, and the heart's nervous control takes 48 hours or more to recover from a hard session [@stanley2013]. One of the two should be long intervals of four to eight minutes per effort, adding up to about half an hour of hard work. In recreational cyclists on about six hours a week, 4 × 8 minutes doubled the gain from 4 × 4 [@seiler2013]. The other can be 30/15s, more long intervals, or a controlled threshold session. A threshold session costs less to recover from, and builds the fraction of VO₂max you can sustain more than VO₂max itself (Chapter 11). A third hard session belongs only in a short block of a week or two, followed by an easier week, and only when the rest of the week can be genuinely easy. Beyond three the trials found no further gain, and the warning signs of overreaching came first (Chapter 12). {grade B for two sessions and their spacing; grade C for the choice of the second}

**Riding them.** For long intervals and 30/15s, ride at the hardest effort you can sustain to the end. Keep threshold sessions controlled below the second threshold. Judge by power and effort; heart rate can make short intervals look closer to VO₂max than they are (Chapter 11). When a session becomes comfortable, add a repetition before adding power. {grade B for the sessions; grade C for the advice on progression}

**The long ride.** If your events last four or five hours, a weekly ride of three or four hours is a reasonable bet for durability. Chapter 9 explains why it is a bet and not a finding. If your events are an hour, it is optional. Where the week is too short to hold it after the hard sessions, the hard sessions win.

**The easy riding.** Everything else is easy, and Chapter 7's rule sets how easy: as much work as the ride can carry without taking anything from the hard sessions. For a rider on these hours that means the top of Zone 2, a few beats under the ceiling. It is the part of the week that grows with the hours. A ride that drifts between the thresholds is not a better easy ride. It is an extra hard session, and it should be counted as one, because it costs about as much to recover from as an interval session does {shadow @seiler2007}.

**The rest.** No trial I found tested how many days off a rider needs each week. A rest day or a short easy ride before each hard session helps you arrive fresh. Regular riding also matters: the Norwegian analysis found larger gains at four or five sessions a week [@molmen2025], and regularity is my likeliest explanation for the winter's improvement in Chapter 7.

## Three weeks

![Three example weeks for a rider on 4, 8 and 12 hours, day by day. The hard sessions stay at two a week and about 50 to 60 minutes of work; the easy riding and the long ride carry the extra hours. My construction from the earlier chapters, not a tested programme.](figures/worked-weeks.svg)

The figure shows one way to lay out a week at 4, 8 and 12 hours. Each total is the time on the bike, including warm-ups and cool-downs.

At **4 hours**, the week is two hard sessions and two short easy rides: 4 × 8 minutes on Tuesday, an hour easy on Thursday, three sets of 30/15s on Saturday and 45 minutes easy on Sunday. The hard sessions are 96 and 72 hours apart. Their efforts total about 52 minutes, or 21% of the week. The short easy rides keep the riding regular. If three riding days suit your life better than four, you could combine them into one longer ride, which Chapter 7's account of the mechanism slightly favours.

At **8 hours**, the hard sessions move to Tuesday and Friday, 72 hours apart. An easy ride of an hour and a half goes on Wednesday and an hour on Thursday, and a three-hour long ride on Saturday. Sunday and Monday are rest. The hard work is the same 52 minutes, now 11% of the week.

At **12 hours**, the long intervals grow to 5 × 8 minutes, the long ride to four hours, and the easy days to two hours and an hour and a half. Another easy hour and a half goes on Sunday. The hard work comes to about an hour, 8% of the week.

From 4 hours to 12, the hard dose stays within Chapter 12's trial range of about 40 minutes to an hour a week. Most extra hours are easy. The falling percentage of hard training follows from that arrangement; it is not a target to aim at. {grade C}

The days are movable. Put the hard sessions where you are fresh and have time to ride them properly, and keep at least 48 hours between them [@stanley2013]. Put the long ride where you have the hours, which for most people is the weekend. If a hard session follows the long ride, eat well on the long ride. If the long ride follows a hard session, it should be genuinely easy.

> **From the saddle.** My training week has massively changed since I started on this book. My mates used to tease me about how much Zone 2 I was doing. I've introduced structured intervals and seen a massive benefit within a few weeks, and it's still building. My Zone 2 output has notched up, and I'm feeling stronger. I now do two interval sessions a week, two Zone 2 sessions and a longer ride at the weekend. Now that I know what I'm doing, I'm confident I'm on the right track.

That is one rider over a few weeks, and it can't separate the intervals from anything else that changed at the same time.


## Building up

Most advice about adding training comes with a number. The best known is to add no more than 10% a week. It has been tested once, properly, and it made no difference [@buist2008].

### One study, up close: the 10% rule

::: trial
Hypothesis: A graded programme for novice runners would reduce the number of running-related injuries compared with a standard programme.
Design: Randomised trial in 532 novice runners preparing for a recreational 4-mile (6.7 km) event. Injuries were recorded in an online running log. An injury was any complaint of the lower limbs or back that stopped running for at least a week [@buist2008].
| | Graded programme | Standard programme
| Length | 13 weeks | 8 weeks
| How the training rose | By no more than 10% a week | As in a standard beginners' plan
-- Result
| Runners injured | 20.8% | 20.3%
~ No difference (P = .90).
Authors' conclusion: A graded 13-week programme applying the 10% rule did not reduce injuries compared with a standard 8-week programme.
:::

Five extra weeks of slower build-up bought no measurable reduction in injuries.

The runners were beginners, the outcome was injury and not fitness, and running hurts the body in a way cycling mostly doesn't. Each footstrike loads bones and tendons, and the bike, apart from crashes, spares them. So the trial doesn't say that a cyclist can add hours at any rate. It says that the most quoted number for safe progression has no evidence behind it where it was tested, and nothing better has replaced it. {grade B for the trial; grade C for what it means on a bike}

What does seem to matter, in the research that followed, is the size of sudden jumps rather than any steady rate. A widely read review in 2016 argued that athletes used to high loads get injured less often, and that "excessive and rapid increases" in training are behind many injuries [@gabbett2016]. The idea is plausible and fits what coaches see. The tool that came with it is another matter.

### Reading the research: a ratio that correlates with itself

The review proposed a number for spotting a dangerous jump: the acute:chronic workload ratio, this week's training load divided by the average weekly load over the last four weeks. Above a certain ratio, the risk of injury was said to rise. It became the most popular measure of its kind [@impellizzeri2020], and the review called it "a best practice predictor of training-related injuries" [@gabbett2016].

Look at how the conventional version is calculated. The four-week average includes this week. So the top and the bottom of the ratio share a quarter of their content. A week with a high load raises the top of the ratio fully and the bottom by a quarter as much, and the ratio rises whatever was happening to the rider. A week of 400 units after three weeks of 200 gives a ratio of 400 divided by 250, which is 1.6. The same 400 after three weeks of 400 gives exactly 1.0. The ratio is partly measuring this week twice.

That matters when the ratio is correlated with something else that depends on this week's load, such as injury. In 2019 a group of sport scientists published a short paper with the plain title "Mathematical coupling causes spurious correlation within the conventional acute-to-chronic workload ratio calculations" {shadow @lolli2019}. A ratio whose top and bottom share a term will correlate with anything that correlates with that term, even when the ratio itself adds nothing.

In 2020 a fuller critique concluded that "There is no evidence supporting the use of ACWR in training-load-management systems or for training recommendations aimed at reducing injury risk" [@impellizzeri2020]. No study had tried to estimate a causal effect properly. The ratio failed to normalise what it claimed to normalise, behaved differently depending on how it was calculated, and was not consistently related to injury. In the authors' words, it "adds noise and creates statistical artifacts".

When a training tool claims to predict an outcome with one number, check whether its calculation already contains something related to that outcome. Overlapping terms can manufacture a correlation.

For a cyclist, the useful part survives without the ratio. Don't double your training in one week after a quiet month. Rebuild gradually after a break, which Chapter 19 covers. Neither needs a number to two decimal places.

## Monotony

In 1998 Carl Foster followed 25 experienced athletes who logged each session as its rated effort multiplied by duration [@foster1998]. He defined weekly monotony as the average daily load divided by its standard deviation: similar days score high, varied days low. Illnesses clustered when athletes went over individual thresholds, mostly of strain: the week's load multiplied by its monotony.

This was an observational study with self-reported illness. It does not establish a cause, but it supports varying the days rather than riding seven identical sessions between the thresholds. {grade C}

## Where the rest goes

The other things Part IV weighed fit around the riding, not into it.

**Strength.** Chapter 14's advice is heavy lifting for the legs twice a week for about three months, starting after the season ends, and once a week after that to keep it. In a week, that is one or two short sessions in the gym, and they compete with the hard rides for fresh legs. One arrangement, which no trial has tested, is to lift on the same day as a hard ride, after it or several hours later. The easy days then stay easy, and the legs are not heavy from squats at the start of an interval session. For the first two or three weeks of lifting, expect soreness and ride less. {grade C}

**Fuel.** Arrive at the hard sessions fuelled, because a hard session ridden on empty is a hard session wasted. Eat on anything longer than about 90 minutes. Chapter 13 has the amounts. The long ride is also the place to practise eating what you will eat in your events.

**Heat.** Chapter 15 found little for an amateur in deliberate heat training, apart from a week or two of riding in warm conditions before a hot event. A rider training indoors in summer is getting some of it anyway, and Chapter 18 covers what that does to heart rate.

**Indoors.** A trainer makes the hard sessions easier to ride well and the long ride harder to bear. The same effort indoors also tends to produce less power than outdoors. Chapter 18 has the numbers. None of it changes the shape of the week.

## Across a season

![An illustrative season for a rider building from 6 to 9.5 hours a week towards an event: three building weeks followed by a lighter one, two hard sessions in every full week, and a two-week taper that roughly halves the hours and keeps the hard sessions. Drawn for this book; not data, and the pattern of lighter weeks is convention, not tested.](figures/season.svg)

This is an example, with different evidence behind each part.

**Building the hours.** The rider adds about half an hour a week until the hours reach what life allows, then holds. No trial tells a cyclist how fast to add hours, and the 10% rule's one trial found no benefit from going slowly [@buist2008]. Going slowly costs little, though, and the jump to avoid is the sudden one.

**Lighter weeks.** Every fourth week in the figure is about 30% lighter, with one hard session instead of two. This is coaching convention. I searched for a trial in cyclists that compared a regular pattern of lighter weeks with steady training and found none. The nearest evidence is indirect. Unrelieved load leads to the stagnation and fatigue of overreaching [@meeusen2013], and fitness shows up better after volume is cut and intensity kept, which is what a taper is. Take a lighter week when the hard sessions stop going well, or when life makes one for you, and don't worry about the rhythm. {grade C}

**Blocks.** Some riders concentrate their hard sessions into a block of a week or two, followed by easier weeks. The 2019 meta-analysis of block periodisation found small advantages over traditional arrangements, an effect size of 0.40 for VO₂max and 0.28 for peak power [@molmen2019]. The studies behind it were rated as small and generally of low quality, with an average score of 3.7 out of 10 on its quality scale. A block is a reasonable way to use a free week, and the evidence doesn't make it necessary. {grade C}

**Order.** In the largest trial of cyclists, 63 riders did the same 24 interval sessions over 12 weeks in three different orders, with similar improvements {shadow @sylta2016}. There was no advantage to starting with either the long or the short intervals. {grade B}

**The taper.** The best evidence in this chapter is for the last two weeks. A 2007 meta-analysis of 27 studies in competitive athletes found the largest gains from a two-week taper. It cut training volume by 41% to 60%, without reducing the intensity or the number of sessions [@bosquet2007]. The figure's taper does that: the hours fall to about 6 and then about 4, and both hard sessions stay, shortened. The effects were measured as changes from before the taper to after it, without a group that kept training normally. So some of the gain is simply being rested for the event. That is the point. {grade B}

Not everyone has a target event. A rider who rides for fitness and the odd sportive can hold a steady week for months, take a lighter week when tired, and skip the taper. Kiely's 2012 review of periodisation argued that the classic models rest on assumptions that are "no longer scientifically justifiable" [@kiely2012]. Because responses to training vary so much between people, he argued, a plan should respond to how the rider is going rather than follow a fixed calendar.

## When the week goes wrong

The weeks in the figure have the advantage of never having been ridden. When life cuts into yours, protect the hard sessions by dropping easy hours first.

Drop easy riding first. Missing an easy hour costs very little.

Drop to one hard session when you are new to intervals, busy or travelling, or when two leave you flat. Hickson's volunteers kept their VO₂max for 15 weeks after cutting from six training days to two at the same intensity {shadow @hickson1981}. A later minimum-dose review reached a similar conclusion [@spiering2021]. I would use one hard session alongside reduced easy riding as a fallback for a busy period. As Chapter 12 explains, that exact mix is a practical inference, rather than a tested maintenance dose. {grade C}

Don't make up missed sessions by doubling up. The spacing matters before the number. The spacing rule exists to prevent two hard sessions on consecutive days, or a hard session on the day after the long ride. Easy days ridden too hard between hard sessions undo it in the same way.

After illness or a long break, come back on easy riding for a few days before the first hard session. Chapter 2 has my experience of how quickly it returns after a short illness, and Chapter 19 covers how much is lost in longer breaks.

## What to do with it

Two hard sessions, well spaced and well ridden, are the core, and they look much the same at 4 hours and at 12. The easy riding fills the rest, and it is where extra hours go. The long ride is for long events. The season adds shape, and of all the shapes on offer only the taper has good evidence behind it. The rest is convention, which is no reason not to use it and every reason not to argue about it. {grade B for the core of the week; grade C for the rest}
