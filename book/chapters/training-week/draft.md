# Chapter 17: The training week

Build the week in this order. Put in two hard sessions first, at least 48 hours apart and ideally 72 [@stanley2013]. Add a long ride if your events are long and the week has room for it. Fill the rest with easy riding near the top of Zone 2. Leave the remaining days empty.

The order matters because the pieces are not equally important. In the trials of Part III, the hard sessions were where most of the improvement in a few hours a week came from [@seiler2013], and their spacing decided whether they could be ridden well [@stanley2013]. The easy riding supports them, adds load, and becomes the larger share of the week as the hours grow. A week planned the other way round, with the easy hours decided first and the hard sessions squeezed in where they fit, tends to put them on tired legs or on consecutive days.

## The order to build it in

**The hard sessions.** Two a week is the best-supported default for a rider on six to ten hours, and the reasons are in Chapter 12: two sessions build about as much as three over a few weeks, one is enough to hold fitness for months, and the heart's nervous control takes 48 hours or more to recover from a hard session [@stanley2013]. One of the two should be long intervals of four to eight minutes per effort, adding up to about half an hour of hard work, because in recreational cyclists on about six hours a week, 4 × 8 minutes doubled the gain from 4 × 4 [@seiler2013]. The other can be 30/15s, more long intervals, or a controlled threshold session, which costs less to recover from and builds more of the fraction of VO₂max you can sustain than of VO₂max itself (Chapter 11). A third hard session belongs only in a short block of a week or two, followed by an easier week, and only when the rest of the week can be genuinely easy; beyond three the trials found no further gain, and the warning signs of overreaching came first (Chapter 12). {grade B for two sessions and their spacing; grade C for the choice of the second}

**Riding them.** Ride every session at the hardest effort you can sustain to the end, and judge it by power and effort, not heart rate, which can make short intervals look closer to the ceiling than they are (Chapter 11). Within four to eight minutes, Seiler's recreational cyclists suggest erring towards the longer end. Thirty-fifteens are a good second format, especially for a rider who finds long intervals hard to ride well, and sprints are for sprinting. When a session becomes comfortable, add a repetition before adding power, and keep the easy riding around it easy, because the trials that produced these gains surrounded the hard sessions with easy riding. {grade B for the sessions; grade C for the advice on progression}

**The long ride.** If your events last four or five hours, a weekly ride of three or four hours is a reasonable bet for durability, and Chapter 9 explains why it is a bet and not a finding. If your events are an hour, it is optional. Where the week is too short to hold it after the hard sessions, the hard sessions win.

**The easy riding.** Everything else is easy, and Chapter 7's rule sets how easy: as much work as the ride can carry without taking anything from the hard sessions, which for a rider on these hours means the top of Zone 2, a few beats under the ceiling. It is the part of the week that grows with the hours. A ride that drifts between the thresholds is not a better easy ride. It is an extra hard session, and it should be counted as one, because it costs about as much to recover from as an interval session does {shadow @seiler2007}.

**The rest.** Days off are whatever is left. No trial I found tested a rule for how many a week a rider needs, and the week's shape matters more than the count: a rest day or a short easy ride before each hard session is the way to arrive at it fresh. Riding regularly matters more than riding long. Four or five sessions a week is where the Norwegian analysis of 353 studies found the gains growing [@molmen2025], and Chapter 7 put my own winter's improvement down to regularity more than to anything about the intensity.

## Three weeks

![Three example weeks for a rider on 4, 8 and 12 hours, day by day. The hard sessions stay at two a week and about 50 to 60 minutes of work; the easy riding and the long ride carry the extra hours. My construction from the earlier chapters, not a tested programme.](figures/worked-weeks.svg)

The figure shows one way to lay out a week at 4, 8 and 12 hours. Each total is the time on the bike, including warm-ups and cool-downs.

At **4 hours**, the week is two hard sessions and two short easy rides: 4 × 8 minutes on Tuesday, an hour easy on Thursday, three sets of 30/15s on Saturday and 45 minutes easy on Sunday. The hard sessions are 96 and 72 hours apart. The work in them, the minutes actually spent in the efforts, comes to about 52 minutes, and that is 21% of the week's time. At this volume the hard sessions do most of the training. The easy rides are there to keep the riding regular and to let the legs recover without stopping. There is a case for making one of them a little longer and the other a rest day, and Chapter 7's point that 45-minute easy rides have the least to recommend them on mechanism applies here. There is also a case for keeping four rides, because a rider who rides four times a week is a rider who rides.

At **8 hours**, the hard sessions move to Tuesday and Friday, 72 hours apart, with an easy ride of an hour and a half on Wednesday and an hour on Thursday, and a three-hour long ride on Saturday. Sunday and Monday are rest. The hard work is the same 52 minutes, now 11% of the week.

At **12 hours**, the long intervals grow to 5 × 8 minutes, the long ride to four hours, and the easy days to two hours and an hour and a half, with another easy hour and a half on Sunday. The hard work comes to about an hour, 8% of the week.

The hard dose barely changes from 4 hours to 12. It sits in the range that worked in the trials of Chapter 12, about 40 minutes to an hour of hard work a week, and close to the champions' 1.3 hours a week above the first threshold [@tonnessen2014]. The extra hours are almost all easy. That is the same pattern Chapter 8 found in the training diaries of Olympic champions, who held their hard training steady through the year while their easy hours rose and fell, and it is the reason the share of hard training falls as the hours rise. Nobody needs to aim at a percentage. The percentage is what is left after the hard sessions are fixed and the easy hours are added. {grade C}

The days are movable. Put the hard sessions where you are fresh and have time to ride them properly, keep at least 48 hours between them [@stanley2013], and put the long ride where you have the hours, which for most people is the weekend. If a hard session follows the long ride, eat well on the long ride. If the long ride follows a hard session, it should be genuinely easy.

> **From the saddle.** My training week has massively changed since I started on this book. My mates used to tease me about how much Zone 2 I was doing. I've introduced structured intervals and seen a massive benefit within a few weeks, and it's still building. My Zone 2 output has notched up, and I'm feeling stronger. I now do two interval sessions a week, two Zone 2 sessions and a longer ride at the weekend. Now that I know what I'm doing, I'm confident I'm on the right track.

That is one rider over a few weeks, and it can't separate the intervals from anything else that changed at the same time.


### A planner, and where the book differs

When I wrote a week planner for my website, before this book, I built it on the same order: two hard sessions always, on Tuesday and Thursday, everything else easy and growing with the hours, an optional long ride on Saturday that goes in only if the week can hold it, never two hard days together, and at least one rest day. Writing Part III changed two of its details. The planner's hard sessions are 4 × 4 minutes and a threshold session. The book's evidence favours four to eight minutes per effort over four, for riders like the ones in Seiler's trial, and treats a threshold session as one reasonable choice for the second session rather than a rule. Neither the planner nor these weeks has been tested as a whole. They are arrangements of findings that were each tested in pieces.

## Building up

Most advice about adding training comes with a number. The best known is to add no more than 10% a week. It has been tested once, properly, and it made no difference [@buist2008].

### One study, up close: the 10% rule

In 2008 a group in Groningen in the Netherlands put the rule to a randomised trial [@buist2008].

::: trial
Hypothesis: A graded programme for novice runners would reduce the number of running-related injuries compared with a standard programme.
Design: Randomised trial in 532 novice runners preparing for a recreational 4-mile (6.7 km) event. Injuries were recorded in an online running log. An injury was any complaint of the lower limbs or back that stopped running for at least a week [@buist2008].
| | Graded programme | Standard programme
| Length | 13 weeks | 8 weeks
| How the training rose | By no more than 10% a week | As in a standard beginners' plan
-- Result
| Runners injured | 20.8% | 20.3%
~ No difference (P = .90).
Authors' conclusion: A graded 13-week programme applying the 10% rule did not reduce injuries compared with a standard 8-week programme.
:::

The trial was large, randomised, and set up to test exactly one thing. Five extra weeks of slower build-up bought nothing measurable, and about one runner in five was injured either way.

The runners were beginners, the outcome was injury and not fitness, and running hurts the body in a way cycling mostly doesn't. Each footstrike loads bones and tendons, and the bike, apart from crashes, spares them. So the trial doesn't say that a cyclist can add hours at any rate. It says that the most quoted number for safe progression has no evidence behind it where it was tested, and nothing better has replaced it. {grade B for the trial; grade C for what it means on a bike}

What does seem to matter, in the research that followed, is the size of sudden jumps rather than any steady rate. A widely read review in 2016 argued that athletes used to high loads get injured less often, and that "excessive and rapid increases" in training are behind many injuries [@gabbett2016]. The idea is plausible and fits what coaches see. The tool that came with it is another matter.

### Reading the research: a ratio that correlates with itself

The review proposed a number for spotting a dangerous jump: the acute:chronic workload ratio, this week's training load divided by the average weekly load over the last four weeks. Above a certain ratio, the risk of injury was said to rise. It became the most popular measure of its kind [@impellizzeri2020], and the review called it "a best practice predictor of training-related injuries" [@gabbett2016].

Look at how the conventional version is calculated. The four-week average includes this week. So the top and the bottom of the ratio share a quarter of their content. A week with a high load raises the top of the ratio fully and the bottom by a quarter as much, and the ratio rises whatever was happening to the rider. A week of 400 units after three weeks of 200 gives a ratio of 400 divided by 250, which is 1.6. The same 400 after three weeks of 400 gives exactly 1.0. The ratio is partly measuring this week twice.

That matters when the ratio is correlated with something else that depends on this week's load, such as injury. In 2019 a group of sport scientists published a short paper with the plain title "Mathematical coupling causes spurious correlation within the conventional acute-to-chronic workload ratio calculations" {shadow @lolli2019}. A ratio that shares a term with its own denominator will correlate with things that correlate with that term, even when the ratio itself adds nothing.

In 2020 a fuller critique concluded that "There is no evidence supporting the use of ACWR in training-load-management systems or for training recommendations aimed at reducing injury risk" [@impellizzeri2020]. No study had tried to estimate a causal effect properly. The ratio failed to normalise what it claimed to normalise, behaved differently depending on how it was calculated, and was not consistently related to injury. In the authors' words, it "adds noise and creates statistical artifacts".

A ratio, an index or a score built from other numbers can look like a new measurement and carry no new information, and when its parts overlap, it can manufacture a correlation by arithmetic. When a training tool offers a single number that predicts something, ask what it is made of, and whether the thing it predicts is already inside it.

For a cyclist, the useful part survives without the ratio. Don't double your training in one week after a quiet month. Rebuild gradually after a break, which Chapter 19 covers. Neither needs a number to two decimal places.

## Monotony

One older idea about weekly structure has a little evidence and a lot of sense. In 1998 Carl Foster followed 25 experienced athletes who logged every session as its rated effort multiplied by its duration [@foster1998]. He defined the week's monotony as the average daily load divided by its standard deviation: a week in which every day is much the same scores high, and a week of hard and easy days scores low. Illnesses, recorded as they came, clustered when athletes went over individual thresholds, mostly of strain, the week's load multiplied by its monotony.

It is one observational study of 25 people, with illness as reported by the athletes, and it shows association, not cause. But it points the same way as everything in Part III. A week of seven identical moderate rides is the monotonous week, and it is also the week that puts most of its riding between the thresholds. The hard–easy week this chapter recommends is low in monotony by construction. {grade C}

## Where the rest goes

The other things Part IV weighed fit around the riding, not into it.

**Strength.** Chapter 14's advice is heavy lifting for the legs twice a week for about three months, starting after the season ends, and once a week after that to keep it. In a week, that is one or two short sessions in the gym, and they compete with the hard rides for fresh legs. One arrangement, which no trial has tested, is to lift on the same day as a hard ride, after it or several hours later, so that the easy days stay easy and the legs are not heavy from squats at the start of an interval session. For the first two or three weeks of lifting, expect soreness and ride less. {grade C}

**Fuel.** Arrive at the hard sessions fuelled, because a hard session ridden on empty is a hard session wasted, and eat on anything longer than about 90 minutes. Chapter 13 has the amounts. The long ride is also the place to practise eating what you will eat in your events.

**Heat.** Chapter 15 found little for an amateur in deliberate heat training, apart from a week or two of riding in warm conditions before a hot event. A rider training indoors in summer is getting some of it anyway, and Chapter 18 covers what that does to heart rate.

**Indoors.** A trainer makes the hard sessions easier to ride well and the long ride harder to bear, and the same effort indoors tends to produce less power than outdoors. Chapter 18 has the numbers. None of it changes the shape of the week.

## Across a season

![An illustrative season for a rider building from 6 to 9.5 hours a week towards an event: three building weeks followed by a lighter one, two hard sessions in every full week, and a two-week taper that roughly halves the hours and keeps the hard sessions. Drawn for this book; not data, and the pattern of lighter weeks is convention, not tested.](figures/season.svg)

The season in the figure is drawn to show the shape, not to prescribe it. Each part of that shape has different evidence behind it.

**Building the hours.** The rider adds about half an hour a week until the hours reach what life allows, then holds. No trial tells a cyclist how fast to add hours, and the 10% rule's one trial found no benefit from going slowly [@buist2008]. Going slowly costs little, though, and the jump to avoid is the sudden one.

**Lighter weeks.** Every fourth week in the figure is about 30% lighter, with one hard session instead of two. This is coaching convention. I searched for a trial in cyclists that compared a regular pattern of lighter weeks with steady training and found none. The nearest evidence is indirect: unrelieved load leads to the stagnation and fatigue of overreaching [@meeusen2013], and fitness shows up better after a reduction in volume when the intensity is kept, which is what a taper is. Take a lighter week when the hard sessions stop going well, or when life makes one for you, and don't worry about the rhythm. {grade C}

**Blocks.** Some riders concentrate their hard sessions into a block of a week or two, followed by easier weeks. The 2019 meta-analysis of block periodisation found small advantages over traditional arrangements, an effect size of 0.40 for VO₂max and 0.28 for peak power, from studies it rated as small and generally of low quality, with an average score of 3.7 out of 10 on its quality scale [@molmen2019]. A block is a reasonable way to use a free week, and the evidence doesn't make it necessary. {grade C}

**Order.** In the largest trial of cyclists, 63 riders did the same 24 interval sessions over 12 weeks in three different orders, and all improved by similar amounts {shadow @sylta2016}. Whether the long intervals come before the short ones, or the other way round, doesn't matter. Doing them does. {grade B}

**The taper.** The best evidence in this chapter is for the last two weeks. A 2007 meta-analysis of 27 studies in competitive athletes found the largest gains from a taper of two weeks in which training volume was cut by 41% to 60%, without reducing the intensity or the number of sessions [@bosquet2007]. The figure's taper does that: the hours fall to about 6 and then about 4, and both hard sessions stay, shortened. The effects were measured as changes from before the taper to after it, without a group that kept training normally, so some of the gain is simply being rested for the event, which is the point. {grade B}

Not everyone has a target event. A rider who rides for fitness and the odd sportive can hold a steady week for months, take a lighter week when tired, and skip the taper. Kiely's 2012 review of periodisation argued that the classic models rest on assumptions that are "no longer scientifically justifiable", and that because responses to training vary so much between people, a plan should respond to how the rider is going rather than follow a fixed calendar [@kiely2012].

## When the week goes wrong

Weeks rarely go to plan. The three in the figure have the advantage of never having been ridden. The order of building the week is also the order of dropping things from it.

Drop easy riding first. Missing an easy hour costs very little.

Keep one hard session, and drop to one for a while whenever you are new to intervals, busy, ill or travelling, or when two leave you flat. Hickson's volunteers, who cut from six days a week to two at the same intensity, kept their VO₂max for 15 weeks {shadow @hickson1981}, and a 2021 review of the minimum dose found that endurance can be held for that long on as few as two sessions a week, provided the intensity is kept [@spiering2021]. Chapter 12 has the details. A busy fortnight with one good interval session in each week costs little.

Don't make up missed sessions by doubling up. The spacing matters before the number: two hard sessions on consecutive days, or a hard session added to the day after the long ride, is the arrangement the spacing rule exists to prevent, and easy days ridden too hard between hard sessions undo it in the same way.

After illness or a long break, come back on easy riding for a few days before the first hard session. Chapter 2 has my experience of how quickly it returns after a short illness, and Chapter 19 covers how much is lost in longer breaks.

## What to do with it

Two hard sessions, well spaced and well ridden, are the core, and they look much the same at 4 hours and at 12. The easy riding fills the rest, and it is where extra hours go. The long ride is for long events. The season adds shape, and of all the shapes on offer only the taper has good evidence behind it. The rest is convention, which is no reason not to use it and every reason not to argue about it. {grade B for the core of the week; grade C for the rest}
