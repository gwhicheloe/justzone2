# Chapter 11: Time near VO₂max

In 2000 Véronique Billat's group put eight long-distance runners on a 400 m track, carrying portable gas analysers, and had them run two hard sessions [@billat2000]. In one they alternated 30 seconds at the speed that just brought out their VO₂max with 30 seconds at half that speed. In the other they ran without stopping at a speed halfway between their threshold and that top speed. The two sessions left about the same lactate in their blood. During the stop-start session the runners spent 7 minutes 51 seconds at VO₂max, and during the continuous one 2 minutes 42 seconds.

Twenty-five years of interval research has been organised around that difference. The idea is that the strongest stimulus for raising VO₂max, the oxygen ceiling of Chapter 1, is time spent close to it, so the best interval session is the one that keeps you there longest at an effort you can bear. It is why 30/15 intervals exist, why coaches argue about 4-minute against 8-minute repeats, and why the next chapter's trials were designed as they were.

My reading is that time near VO₂max is the best working explanation for what a hard session does, and the most useful single number for comparing two sessions on the day. It has not been shown to be the reason intervals work. The one study that followed it through a whole training programme found that the riders who spent more of their sessions close to VO₂max improved more, but those riders also trained two hours a week more. Less-trained people raise their VO₂max with sprints and with steady riding between the thresholds, neither of which spends much time near it. And the same session gives very different amounts of it depending on how hard it is ridden. Use it to design and judge the hard sessions, and don't treat it as the only thing a session does.

## Where the idea came from

Intervals are older than any measurement of what they do. Chapter 8 told how the leading runners of the 1930s to the 1950s trained on repeated hard efforts, Emil Zátopek running dozens of 400 m repeats in a session, long before anyone could measure oxygen uptake during them {shadow @casado2023}. A 2001 review by Billat traced the method to the German physicians Hans Reindell and Helmut Roskamm and to Zátopek's example, and noted that coaches had set interval speeds from race paces, not from any physiological marker {shadow @billat2001}.

Measuring what a session does became practical only when gas analysers became light enough to carry. Billat's track study used them during intervals in the field [@billat2000], and by 2006 enough laboratory work had accumulated for a review to propose rules for the work and recovery periods that keep a runner longest at or near VO₂max [@midgley2006]. That review was explicit that its rules described what sessions do, and that experiments were needed before anyone could say what the sessions would produce over weeks. Most of the research since has been of the first kind.

## What happens near the ceiling

At the start of hard work, oxygen uptake does not jump to its new level. It rises over a minute or two, so a short effort ends before the body's oxygen use has caught up with the demand. In the severe domain, above critical power (Chapters 1 and 5), it then keeps creeping upward, an effect known as the slow component, until it reaches VO₂max or the rider stops. That is how Billat's continuous runners reached VO₂max in more than half the attempts despite running below the speed that would bring it out straight away [@billat2000].

Close to VO₂max the heart is pumping as much blood as it can and nearly every muscle fibre in the legs is working. The usual argument is that this is the strongest signal the heart and the muscles receive, and a widely cited review describes the optimal stimulus as "at least several minutes per session" above 90% of VO₂max [@buchheit2013]. In a 2007 trial from Trondheim, the two interval programmes raised the amount of blood the heart pumped per beat by about 10% [@helgerud2007]. That is the mechanism as its proponents describe it. Nobody has shown that time near VO₂max is what produces the adaptation, rather than something that goes with it, such as the total amount of hard work.

The muscles are part of the argument too. Chapter 7 described how an easy ride is carried mostly by the slow, fatigue-resistant fibres, with the fast fibres joining in only as the effort rises or the ride goes on. Close to VO₂max nearly all of them are working, and a fibre that is not used receives no signal to adapt. In a 2017 Canadian study, ten active men trained one leg with intervals and the other with continuous cycling, doing the same total work with each, for six sessions over two weeks. Citrate synthase, an enzyme used as a marker of mitochondrial content, rose further in the interval leg, to 10.2 against 8.4 in the units the study used {shadow @macinnis2017}. Using each person as their own control removes most of the differences between people that cloud other comparisons, although ten men and two weeks is a small and short test.

The 90% line is a convention. An earlier review used 95% [@midgley2006], and the most careful recent study found that the average share of VO₂max across the work intervals said more than the minutes above any line [@odden2024].

## Why short intervals keep you there

Oxygen uptake also falls slowly when the effort stops. Fifteen seconds of easy pedalling is too short for it to drop far, so a session of 30 seconds hard and 15 easy holds oxygen uptake high through the recoveries as well as the efforts. A 2006 review of running studies recommended work and recovery periods of 15 to 30 seconds for exactly this reason [@midgley2006].

The cycling studies agree, at matched power. When 13 well-trained cyclists rode intervals to exhaustion at the power that brings out VO₂max, work periods of 30 seconds gave longer above 90% than longer work periods at the same power {shadow @ronnestad2016}. In 2024 the same Norwegian group had 11 women and 8 men ride three sessions of 6 × 8 minutes at the same average power, their best for 40 minutes: steady, alternating 60 seconds harder and easier, or 30/15. The 30/15 version gave 1,123 seconds above 90% of VO₂max, against 879 for the steady one and 917 for the 60/60, and the women responded as the men did [@urianstad2024]. Varying the power within long intervals helps too. Fourteen cyclists spent 410 seconds above 90% when each work interval mixed harder and easier sections, against 286 seconds when the power was steady, at the same average power and with no difference in how hard the session felt [@bossi2020].

## The catch is pacing

Every one of those comparisons fixed the power. Riders on the road do not ride at a fixed power, and the difference turns out to matter more than the format.

In 2025 twelve well-trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 × 13 × 30/15 and 6 × 5 minutes twice each: once at prescribed powers, and once self-paced and as hard as they could manage [@appelhans2025]. At prescribed powers the 30/15 session won easily, with 420 seconds above 90% of VO₂max against 178. Ridden all-out, both sessions produced far more, 1,130 seconds and 1,016, and the difference between them was no longer statistically significant.

In runners the short format can lose outright. Twelve highly trained middle-distance runners spent 201 seconds above 90% of VO₂max in a session of 24 × 30 seconds and 328 seconds in 4 × 3 minutes, even though the 30-second efforts were run faster [@fleckenstein2024]. Their heart rates said the opposite: 820 seconds above 90% of maximum heart rate in the short session against 545 in the long one. In short intervals heart rate and oxygen uptake do not move together, and heart rate can make a session look closer to the ceiling than it is.

Perceived effort is steadier. When twelve well-trained runners chose their own pace for sessions of 1-, 2-, 4- and 6-minute repeats, they rated every one at about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, very hard, and the 2- to 6-minute repeats all took them to a peak oxygen uptake of about 92% of VO₂max, against 82% for the 1-minute repeats {shadow @seiler2004}. Six of the twelve reached their highest oxygen uptake in the 4-minute session.

So the format of a session changes its time near VO₂max less than the effort a rider is willing to put into it. A 30/15 session makes a large dose easier to reach at a power that feels manageable, and that is a real advantage. A rider who paces long intervals well can get much the same. {grade B}

### One study, up close: 22 cyclists, 21 sessions

Until 2024 nobody had measured oxygen uptake in every session of a training programme and then asked whether it predicted the result [@odden2024].

The study did something nobody had done, and did it carefully. The riders were tested for VO₂max twice during the programme as well as before and after, so each session's oxygen uptake was compared with a current VO₂max rather than one that the training had already left behind. The share of VO₂max proved more repeatable from session to session than the share of maximum heart rate, and it tracked the improvements better [@odden2024].

What the study cannot show is that reaching a higher share of VO₂max caused the bigger improvements, and the table shows why.

### Reading the research: a correlation inside a trial

Every rider rode the same session at the same share of their own 40-minute power. Whether a rider reached 86% of VO₂max or 80% was therefore a property of the rider, not of the session. Riders whose 40-minute power sits high relative to their VO₂max work closer to the ceiling at that power, and the paper reports that riders with a higher power at a lactate of 4 mmol/L and a higher 15-minute power before the programme reached a higher share during it [@odden2024]. The two halves were different groups of people before the training began.

They also trained differently while it ran. The higher half rode two hours a week more, most of the difference in the lowest zone, and they started with a lower VO₂max, which leaves more room to improve. The authors acknowledge that the extra volume "could have affected the results", and judge that the share of VO₂max was "most likely" responsible, because the lower half did slightly more training in the two hardest zones [@odden2024]. That is a reasonable judgement. It is a judgement, not a result.

The skill is to notice when a trial has stopped being a trial. Assigning riders to sessions at random lets a study compare the sessions. Splitting them afterwards by something they did, or something about them, turns the comparison into an observational one, with all the confounding of Chapter 10's cohorts. A correlation of this kind is a strong reason to run the trial that would test it, in which riders are assigned to sessions that differ only in how close to VO₂max they keep them. As far as I can find, nobody has run it. {grade C}

## Responders and doses

Chapter 2 described the HERITAGE study, in which 481 sedentary adults did the same 20 weeks of training, gained about 400 millilitres a minute of VO₂max on average, and varied from more than a litre to almost nothing. It also described the Zurich experiment in which giving people more sessions a week made the apparent non-responders disappear {shadow @monterolundby2017}.

In 2013 a group that included Michael Joyner, whose model of endurance opened Chapter 1, asked the same question of interval training [@bacon2013]. They gathered 37 studies in which sedentary or recreationally active people under 45 did at least ten minutes of hard work per session, three or more times a week, for six to thirteen weeks. The average gain was 0.51 litres a minute, more than HERITAGE's in a fraction of the time. The nine studies with the largest gains averaged 0.87 litres and the nine with the smallest 0.27, and the larger gains came from programmes that ran longer, 9.7 weeks against 6.9, and, though the difference was not significant, had more training each week, 209 minutes against 123. In the studies with the longest intervals, the authors reported marked gains in almost every participant. They also found signs that small studies with small effects had gone unpublished, and correcting for that lowered the average to 0.37 litres a minute.

The argument they drew from it is that much apparent non-response is under-dosing: programmes too short, too easy or too sparse to move VO₂max in everyone. That fits the Zurich result, and it fits the idea of time near VO₂max, because the programmes that worked best spent more of it. It does not separate time near VO₂max from the other things those programmes also had more of, which were hard minutes, weeks and sessions.

## What the training trials show

Comparisons of whole programmes support a narrower claim: that intervals raise VO₂max more than continuous training, by a modest margin. A 2015 analysis of 28 controlled studies in 723 healthy adults found that both raised it substantially against no exercise, by 4.9 mL/kg/min for continuous training and 5.5 for intervals, and that intervals beat continuous training by 1.2 mL/kg/min on average, with the gap larger in less fit people and in longer programmes [@milanovic2015]. A difference of that size, about 3% for a recreational rider, is real and modest. A pooled analysis of 334 sedentary or recreationally active people found an average rise of 0.51 litres a minute after 6 to 13 weeks of interval training, and of 0.8 to 0.9 litres in the nine studies with the longest intervals, in which every participant responded [@bacon2013]. The Trondheim trial of 2007 found 7.2% after 4 × 4-minute intervals and 5.5% after 15/15s, more than after continuous training matched for work [@helgerud2007]. {grade B}

The same evidence shows other routes to the same place. In 2024 a Canadian group randomised 84 young adults to six weeks of training in one of four intensity domains, defined from each person's own thresholds, or to no training [@inglis2024]. Continuous riding in the upper part of the band between the thresholds raised VO₂max by 5.4 mL/kg/min, nearly as much as intervals in the severe domain at 6.2, and all-out sprints raised it by 4.7. Riding below the first threshold, matched for work, raised it by 1.8, which was not significantly different from doing nothing. Because each person's domains were set from their own tests, everyone in a group received a similar physiological stimulus, which is the authors' main point: prescribing intensity from a person's thresholds, not from a percentage of their maximum, made the training more uniform. Sprint intervals, repeated 30-second efforts with long rests, keep oxygen uptake near VO₂max only briefly, yet across 16 trials in young people they raised it by about 8%, as much as continuous training {shadow @gist2014}.

And closest is not automatically best. In the trial that opens the next chapter, recreational cyclists who rode 4 × 8 minutes at about 90% of peak heart rate improved twice as much as those who rode 4 × 4 at 94%, although the shorter intervals were ridden at a higher heart rate and felt harder {shadow @seiler2013}. The longer session gave twice the work at a slightly lower intensity, and the amount of work seems to have mattered at least as much as how close to VO₂max it was done.

In people who are not very fit, then, almost any hard enough training raises VO₂max, and time near it is one route among several. In trained riders the claim is harder to test, because they improve slowly and trials are short, and the reviews that argue that trained athletes need time near VO₂max say "may" [@midgley2006]. The trials in trained cyclists that improved VO₂max mostly used intervals close to it, which is consistent with the idea without proving it. {grade C}

## What to take from it

Time near VO₂max is best used as a way to compare sessions. Between two sessions of the same length and effort, the one that keeps you closer to the ceiling for longer is probably the stronger stimulus for the heart and the oxygen system, and 30/15s, or long intervals with surges built into them, are reliable ways to get more of it at a power that feels tolerable. The best sessions in the figure gave 15 to 19 minutes above 90% of VO₂max, and they were sizeable: 6 × 8 minutes, 6 × 5 minutes or three sets of 13 × 30/15, each with 20 to 50 minutes of hard work in it. The often-quoted "several minutes" near VO₂max is a floor, not a target [@buchheit2013]. A rider who does two hard sessions a week should expect each to contain roughly half an hour of hard work, and the more of that spent close to the ceiling, the stronger the session is likely to be.

For a rider without a gas analyser, the guide is effort and power, not heart rate. The trials that worked used the hardest effort a rider could sustain for the whole set, which in self-paced studies comes out at about 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, and heart rate is a poor guide to short intervals.

It is a poor guide to everything else a hard session does. Neither VO₂max nor time near it is the whole of performance, and in the one study that measured both, the riders who got more of it also did more of everything else. Chapter 12 turns to the sessions themselves, and Chapter 13 to how many of them a week can do their work.
