# Chapter 1: What limits endurance

In 1923 the physiologist Archibald Hill and his colleague Hartley Lupton described something that every endurance athlete now takes for granted. As exercise gets harder, the body takes up more oxygen, until it reaches a ceiling. Beyond that point the work can go on for a while, but the extra energy has to be made without oxygen, which the body can do only briefly {shadow @hill1923}. That ceiling became known as maximal oxygen uptake, or VO₂max. For most of the following century it was the first number physiologists reached for to explain why one athlete was faster than another.

Two riders can have the same VO₂max and finish an hour-long time trial minutes apart. VO₂max is only one part of what cyclists call a rider's engine. In this book the engine means the whole aerobic system: how much oxygen you can use, how much of that capacity you can sustain, and how efficiently you turn it into power.

## The question

Strip away tactics, aerodynamics and the weather, and endurance performance comes down to one thing: how much power you can sustain for the length of the event. For a 40-minute effort or a two-hour climb, that power comes almost entirely from aerobic metabolism, the process in which muscle cells use oxygen to release energy from fat and carbohydrate.

So the real question is what limits how fast you can make energy with oxygen, and how much of that energy ends up turning the pedals.

In 2008 the physiologists Michael Joyner and Edward Coyle set out the answer that is still the standard one [@joynercoyle2008]. It has three parts:

1. **VO₂max**, the maximum rate at which you can take up and use oxygen.
2. **The fraction of VO₂max you can sustain**, which depends on where your lactate threshold sits.
3. **Efficiency**, the share of the energy you produce that ends up as power at the pedals.

Multiply the first two and you get what Joyner and Coyle call your *performance VO₂*, the rate of oxygen use you can hold for the duration of a race. Multiply that by your efficiency and you have your sustainable power.

::: formula
*performance VO₂* = VO₂max × fraction sustained
sustainable power = *performance VO₂* × efficiency
:::

## How it works: from air to muscle

You breathe oxygen into your lungs, where it crosses into the blood and binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Your heart pumps that blood out to the body. The amount it pumps each minute is the *cardiac output*: your heart rate multiplied by the volume of blood pushed out with each beat, the *stroke volume*. In the working leg muscles, blood flows through a dense mesh of tiny vessels called capillaries, and oxygen moves out of the blood into the muscle fibres. Inside each fibre it reaches the mitochondria, small structures that use it to break down fuel and make ATP, the molecule that powers muscle contraction.

![Oxygen's route from the air to the working muscle. The first three steps deliver it and the last two use it. Drawn for this book, following Bassett and Howley's review and Joyner and Coyle's model [@bassetthowley2000; @joynercoyle2008].](figures/oxygen-route.svg)

The ceiling depends mostly on delivery: how much oxygen the heart and blood can bring to the muscle. Bassett and Howley's review shows that when oxygen delivery changes, through blood doping, thin air at altitude or drugs that slow the heart, VO₂max changes with it [@bassetthowley2000]. When people train, the rise in VO₂max comes mainly from a bigger maximum cardiac output, not from muscles extracting more oxygen from each litre of blood. And a small muscle group given more blood than usual can use oxygen far faster than it does during whole-body exercise. The muscle could use more oxygen if it were delivered. {grade A}

Joyner and Coyle name stroke volume as the biggest single difference between champions and everyone else. Champions also have more blood, and more capillaries and mitochondria in their trained muscles [@joynercoyle2008]. Those last two matter for a different reason: they don't raise the ceiling much, but they change how close to it you can ride.

## VO₂max: the ceiling

Champion endurance athletes have VO₂max values of roughly 70 to 85 millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute, about 50 to 100% higher than healthy, untrained young adults [@joynercoyle2008]. Elite women's values tend to be about 10% lower than elite men's, which Joyner and Coyle attribute mainly to lower haemoglobin concentration and a higher proportion of body fat.

A high VO₂max is close to a requirement for elite endurance performance. You won't find a Tour de France winner with an average one. But among athletes who are already good, VO₂max becomes a poor predictor of who wins, because the riders in a professional peloton all have high values. Once the ceiling is high enough, the other two factors decide most of the differences that remain.

VO₂max is also only partly trainable. It rises quickly in untrained people and much more slowly in those who have trained for years, and part of it is set by genetics.

## The fraction you can sustain

Nobody can ride at 100% of their VO₂max for long. The question is how close they can get for an hour. The answer depends on what happens to lactate as effort rises. Lactate is a molecule that muscle makes from glucose all the time and that other tissues burn as fuel {shadow @brooks2018}.

At low intensities, blood lactate stays close to its resting level. As intensity rises, lactate reaches a first point where it starts to build up, and above a second, higher point it keeps rising until the effort has to stop. The first of these points is the lactate threshold that Chapter 5 explores in detail. In untrained people, lactate begins to rise noticeably at around 60% of VO₂max. In elite endurance athletes it can sit at 75 to 90% [@joynercoyle2008]. {grade A}

That shift is one of the largest effects of endurance training, and it has little to do with the heart. It comes mainly from changes in the muscle: more mitochondria, more of the enzymes that use oxygen and a better capacity to burn fat, so the muscle relies less on making lactate at a given workload [@bassetthowley2000]. Bassett and Howley argue that the lactate threshold is the best single physiological predictor of endurance performance. It combines all three factors in one number: how high your VO₂max is, how much of it you can use, and how economically you use it.

This is where the case for large volumes of easy riding begins. If easy training builds mitochondria and capillaries, it should raise the fraction of VO₂max you can sustain, even if it does relatively little for VO₂max itself. That's a mechanism argument.

## Efficiency: turning oxygen into watts

The third factor is the easiest to overlook. Of the energy your muscles release from oxygen and fuel, only a fraction ends up as power at the cranks. The rest becomes heat, which is why nobody rides a turbo trainer without a fan more than once. In cyclists this fraction, called *gross efficiency*, typically ranges from about 18.5% to 23.5% at 300 watts [@joynercoyle2008]. Joyner and Coyle estimate that efficiency varies by 20 to 30% between cyclists, which is a large range for something that rarely appears in training discussions.

Taking the two ends of that range, a rider with a gross efficiency of 23% produces about a quarter more power from the same oxygen than a rider at 18.5% [@joynercoyle2008]. No training block raises VO₂max by a quarter in a trained cyclist.

The best-known evidence on why some riders are more efficient than others comes from Edward Coyle's lab in 1992. In 19 highly trained cyclists, the proportion of slow-twitch (type I) fibres in the thigh muscle ranged from 32% to 76%. Riders with more of them were markedly more efficient: the correlation between type I fibre percentage and one measure of efficiency was 0.85 [@coyle1992]. Type I fibres are the ones built for sustained, aerobic work, and they seem to produce force with less energy wasted as heat. {grade B}

Whether training can increase efficiency much, and how quickly, is less clear. Joyner and Coyle suggest it may improve by 1 to 3% a year with continued endurance training [@joynercoyle2008].

### Reading the research: what one athlete can and can't tell us

In 2005 Coyle published a paper following one cyclist from age 21 to 28 [@coyle2005]. Over those seven years, the paper reported, the rider's VO₂max stayed at about 6 litres a minute, but his muscular efficiency rose by 8%. Combined with the body fat he lost before each race season, his power per kilogram at a given rate of oxygen use rose by 18%. The paper suggested the improvement probably reflected changes in muscle proteins after years of training intensely for three to six hours on most days. The rider was described as a six-time consecutive champion of the Tour de France. He was Lance Armstrong.

The paper was widely cited as evidence that efficiency improves with years of training. It also drew immediate challenges. In the same journal and the same year, a published exchange asked whether Armstrong's efficiency had improved at all [@martin2005]. In 2008, a letter from four researchers was published under the blunt title "Delta efficiency calculation in Tour de France champion is wrong" [@gore2008]. And in 2012 the United States Anti-Doping Agency found that Armstrong had doped, and his Tour de France results from 1999 onwards were disqualified [@usada2012].

A case study follows one person. It can't separate the effect of training from everything else that changed in that person's life over the same period: equipment, testing methods, illness, weight, and, in this case, drugs that alter blood and muscle. Measurements taken years apart, on different occasions, may not be comparable at all.

When you see a training claim resting on one athlete, however famous, treat it as grade C at best. Efficiency may well improve over years of training. This paper can't show it.

### One study, up close: when efficiency makes up for a lower VO₂max

In 2002 Alejandro Lucía and colleagues in Madrid tested 11 world-class professional road cyclists [@lucia2002]. Their average VO₂max was 72 mL/kg/min, firmly in the elite range. Each rode a ramp test to find their VO₂max, then 20 minutes at a steady power equal to 80% of it, which averaged 385 watts. From the second test, the researchers calculated each rider's efficiency, which averaged 24.5%.

Riders with a higher VO₂max tended to be *less* efficient, and riders with a lower VO₂max tended to be more efficient. The correlations were around −0.65 to −0.72, depending on how VO₂max was scaled for body size. The authors concluded that, at the top of the sport, high efficiency seems to compensate for a relatively low VO₂max.

It's a small study, and it describes riders who had already reached the top. If you only study successful professionals, you only see combinations that worked. A rider with a modest VO₂max and modest efficiency wouldn't be in the sample, because they wouldn't be a world-class professional. The study can't tell us that training trades one factor for the other, and it doesn't claim to. What it shows is that there is more than one route to the same performance, and that VO₂max alone would have ranked these riders wrongly. {grade B}

## Putting the three together

Because the three factors multiply, a change in any of them changes sustainable power in proportion. Take two riders, as an illustration. Both have the same VO₂max. One can sustain 80% of it for an hour and the other 75%. All else equal, the first rider's sustainable power is about 7% higher, simply because 80 is about 7% more than 75. Now make the second rider 7% more efficient, and the two are level again.

A training block can therefore make you faster without changing your VO₂max, if it raises your threshold or efficiency. When people argue about whether easy riding or hard intervals are "better", they are often talking about different factors. The case for high-intensity training rests heavily on VO₂max; the case for large amounts of easy training rests on the fraction you can sustain and, possibly, on efficiency.

Joyner and Coyle described elite performance as the product of these factors working together, which is why an elite cyclist can hold around 400 watts for an extended effort [@joynercoyle2008]. They also warned that this physiological picture leaves things out: the nervous system's role in fatigue, motivation and the realities of racing. Chapter 3 takes up what it leaves out.

## The power–duration curve and critical power

There's another way to describe what limits endurance, and it's the one that power meters have made familiar. Ask a rider to produce as much power as possible for 10 seconds, then for a minute, five minutes, twenty and an hour. Plot the results, and you get a curve that falls steeply at first and then flattens out. Riders who use training software know it as their power curve.

In 1965 the French physiologists Hugues Monod and Jean Scherrer found that shape in experiments on single groups of muscles in the arm and leg {shadow @monod1965; @poole2016}. For all-out efforts lasting from about 2 to 15 minutes, power and the time it can be held follow a simple curve, a hyperbola, which flattens towards a particular power. They called that power the *critical power*, and described it as a power that could be kept up "for a very long time without fatigue" {shadow @poole2016}.

That description didn't survive testing: riders asked to ride at their measured critical power typically last only 20 to 30 minutes {shadow @jones2019}. The promise rests on the maths. Extend the hyperbola and the time an effort can last grows without limit as power nears critical power. But the curve is drawn from efforts of 15 minutes at most, and it ignores the slower kinds of fatigue that end longer rides {shadow @poole2016}. The name has also been borrowed, and some training software calls the whole power curve a critical power curve. Physiologists now use a more precise definition, and it is the one this book uses.

The idea itself has held up remarkably well. A 2016 review led by David Poole and Andrew Jones found the same curve across species, types of exercise and individual muscles {shadow @poole2016}. For cycling it comes down to two numbers. Critical power is the power the curve flattens towards but never quite reaches. It is estimated from a handful of all-out efforts in that 2-to-15-minute range, or from a single three-minute all-out test. It marks a boundary: the highest power at which oxygen uptake, lactate and the body's other responses can still settle at a steady level. Above it they keep rising, and the effort will end. The second number, known as W′ (pronounced W prime), is the fixed amount of work you can do above critical power before that happens. Ride 50 watts above your critical power and you'll use up W′ in a predictable time. Ride 100 watts above it and you'll use it up roughly twice as fast. Not everyone agrees that measured critical power sits exactly on that boundary; Chapter 5 has the argument.

![The power–duration curve for a rider with a critical power of 250 W and a W′ of 20 kJ, with the three intensity domains. Above critical power the time an effort can last is set by how fast W′ runs out; below it, other limits take over. Drawn for illustration, not data; the first threshold's position is also illustrative.](figures/power-duration.svg)

Critical power also divides exercise into the intensity domains that will come up throughout this book. Below the lactate threshold is the *moderate* domain. Between the lactate threshold and critical power is the *heavy* domain. Above critical power is the *severe* domain. Martin Burnley and Andrew Jones showed that fatigue behaves differently in each {shadow @burnleyjones2018}. In the moderate domain it develops slowly and seems to come mainly from the nervous system. In the heavy domain, both the nervous system and the muscles fatigue, often in step with falling muscle glycogen. In the severe domain, the muscle's internal chemistry is progressively disrupted until it can't continue, and that happens faster the further above critical power you ride. They also make a point every cyclist has felt: the range of power you can actually sustain is remarkably narrow. {grade A for the shape of the curve; grade B for the details of fatigue in each domain}

Critical power isn't a fourth factor alongside the other three. It's a different view of the same physiology. Where critical power sits depends on all three: how much oxygen you can use, how much of it you can sustain without losing control of your muscle chemistry, and how efficiently you turn it into power. Its advantage is practical. You can estimate it from a power meter, and it gives researchers a way to measure how the whole system holds up when you're tired.

## The fourth factor: durability

These measurements usually come from rested riders, tested after an easy day. They may describe a rider poorly by the end of a race.

In 2021 a group of physiologists led by Ed Maunder argued that this was a serious gap. The numbers measured in routine testing aren't fixed. They shift during long exercise, and models that ignore this will misjudge how athletes perform and how they should pace {shadow @maunder2021}. They proposed measuring *durability*: when, and by how much, those numbers get worse as exercise goes on.

In a 2019 study from Andrew Jones's group, 16 people did a three-minute all-out test fresh, and again after 40 minutes, 80 minutes and two hours of hard cycling. Their critical power held up for the first 80 minutes, but after two hours it was 9% lower {shadow @clark2019a}. In a 2024 review, Jones described critical power falling by about 10% on average after two hours of heavy-intensity cycling, with individual changes ranging from less than 1% to about 32% {shadow @jones2024}. He argued that this resilience to fatigue should be treated as a fourth dimension of endurance performance, alongside the three in Joyner and Coyle's model. {grade B}

Two riders who look identical in a fresh lab test could be almost a third apart after two hours. For anyone training for long events, that may matter more than a few per cent of VO₂max. Chapter 9 looks at durability in detail: how it's measured, what the professional peloton shows, and what, if anything, trains it.

## What to do with it

The model gives every training question in this book the same shape. A session can make you faster by raising your VO₂max, by raising the fraction of it you can sustain, by improving your efficiency, by making all three hold up better late in a ride, or by some combination. Arguments about training methods usually turn out to be arguments about which of these a method changes, and by how much.

The claims made for Zone 2 sit on two of the four. Easy riding is said to build the mitochondria and capillaries that let a rider hold a larger fraction of VO₂max, and to help all three hold up late in a long ride. Chapter 7 weighs the first claim and Chapter 9 the second.
