# Glossary

The terms the book uses most, as it uses them. The chapter in brackets explains each one more fully.

**Aerobic threshold.** Another name for the first threshold. (Chapter 5)

**AMPK.** An enzyme that reads the cell's fuel gauge, the ratio of AMP to ATP, and switches on when energy demand outruns supply. It is one of the sensors through which exercise sets off the building of new mitochondria. (Chapter 2)

**Anaerobic threshold.** A name used for both the first and the second threshold. Where you meet it, find out which is meant. (Chapter 5)

**ATP (adenosine triphosphate).** The molecule every cell uses to carry energy from the fuel it burns to the work it does, muscle contraction included. A muscle stores only enough for a few seconds, so it rebuilds ATP continuously. (Chapter 2)

**Between the thresholds.** Intensity above the first threshold and below the second: the physiologists' heavy domain, and zone 2 of the three-zone model. Riders call parts of it tempo, sweet spot and threshold. (Chapter 5)

**Biological passport.** A running record of a professional rider's blood values, against which each new sample is compared. Professional riders have had one since 2008. (Chapter 16)

**Capillaries.** The tiny blood vessels running between muscle fibres, where oxygen passes from the blood into the muscle. Training adds more of them. (Chapter 1)

**Cardiac output.** The amount of blood the heart pumps each minute: heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Training raises VO₂max mainly by raising the maximum cardiac output. (Chapter 1)

**Central governor.** Tim Noakes's idea that the brain regulates exercise in advance, setting how much muscle it will recruit so that the effort ends before anything is damaged, and using the feeling of fatigue to enforce that decision. (Chapter 3)

**Cohort study.** A study that follows a large group of people for years and compares those who differ in something, such as how active they are. It shows what goes together, not what causes what. (Chapter 10)

**Confidence interval.** The range within which the true effect plausibly lies, given the data. A wide one means the study could not pin the effect down. (Chapter 4)

**Control group.** Volunteers who are tested like the others but do not get the treatment, so that its effect can be separated from practice, the season and chance. (Chapter 4)

**Critical power.** The highest power at which the body's responses, including oxygen uptake and lactate, can still settle to a steady state. Above it every effort has a time limit, set by W′. One of the measures of the second threshold. (Chapter 1)

**Crossover trial.** A trial in which every volunteer does each of the treatments in turn, so that each is compared with themselves. (Chapter 9)

**Decoupling.** Heart rate drifting upwards, relative to power or pace, during a long steady effort. The more it drifts, the more the rider is fading. (Chapter 19)

**DFA alpha 1.** An index of the pattern in the gaps between heartbeats, calculated from a chest strap. It sits around 1.0 in very light exercise and falls towards 0.5 at high intensity. A 2021 proposal took 0.75 as the first threshold, and free phone apps calculate the index live. It is less reliable than the apps make it look. (Chapter 6)

**Double-blind.** Describes a trial in which neither the volunteers nor the researchers testing them know who received which treatment. (Chapter 4)

**Durability.** When, and by how much, a rider's power, efficiency and thresholds deteriorate as a long effort goes on. Also called physiological resilience, and proposed as a fourth factor in endurance alongside VO₂max, the fraction of it a rider can sustain, and efficiency. (Chapters 1 and 9)

**Easy.** In this book, any intensity below the first threshold: the physiologists' moderate domain and zone 1 of the three-zone model. Zone 2 is its upper part. (Chapter 5)

**Effect size.** A difference between groups divided by how much people vary, so that results measured in different units can be compared. (Chapters 12 and 17)

**Efficiency.** The share of the energy a rider produces that ends up as power at the pedals; in cycling, usually gross efficiency. The third factor in the standard model of endurance. (Chapter 1)

**EPO (erythropoietin).** The hormone, made by the kidney, that controls how many red blood cells the body makes. Given as a drug it raises the blood's capacity to carry oxygen, and it is banned in sport. (Chapter 16)

**Evidence grade.** The letter this book gives its main claims. A: several randomised trials, or a good pooled analysis of them, pointing the same way. B: consistent evidence from trials that are small or short or from a single laboratory, or from one well-designed trial. C: observation, mechanism, case studies or expert opinion. (Introduction and Chapter 4)

**Fat burning.** The use of fat as fuel, also called fat oxidation. Training shifts the fuel mix towards fat at any given power; as effort rises, more of the energy comes from carbohydrate. Peak fat burning, or fat max, is the intensity at which the most fat is burned per minute, and it is lower after eating. (Chapter 7)

**First threshold (LT1).** In this book, the intensity at which blood lactate first rises above the rider's own baseline, taken as a rise of 0.5 mmol/L in a test with stages of at least four minutes. It marks the top of Zone 2. Breathing changes at about the same point, the first ventilatory threshold (VT1). (Chapters 5 and 6)

**Fitness–fatigue model.** A model, first published by Eric Banister and colleagues in 1975, in which each session adds to a fitness that builds and fades slowly and a fatigue that builds and fades quickly. Performance is fitness minus fatigue. Training software draws its charts of fitness, fatigue and form from it. (Chapter 19)

**FTP (functional threshold power).** Andrew Coggan's term for the highest power a rider can hold in a quasi-steady state for about an hour, usually estimated as 95% of the average power in a 20-minute test ridden as hard as possible. The measure of the second threshold that most cyclists meet. (Chapters 5 and 6)

**Glycogen.** The form in which the body stores carbohydrate, in the muscles and the liver. Hours of riding run it down, even below the first threshold, and a long effort ends when the muscles have little left. (Chapters 5 and 13)

**Gut training.** Practising taking carbohydrate while riding so that the gut tolerates more of it. In the studies so far, two weeks of repeated feeding reduced gut discomfort. (Chapter 13)

**Haematocrit.** The percentage of the blood's volume taken up by red cells. (Chapter 16)

**Haemoglobin mass.** The total amount of haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Training raises it, and so did heat training in some trials, although performance did not always follow. (Chapters 2 and 15)

**Hard.** In this book, any intensity above the second threshold: the severe domain. (Chapter 5)

**Heart rate variability (HRV).** The variation in the gaps between heartbeats. After a session it shows how far the nervous control of the heart has been disturbed, and how quickly it recovers. (Chapter 6)

**Heat training.** Repeated sessions of exercise in the heat, which bring heat acclimation: more dilute sweat, a lower heart rate in the heat and longer endurance in a hot test. Whether it helps in cool conditions is disputed, and its effect on the blood fades within weeks of stopping. (Chapter 15)

**Insulin resistance.** A weakened response of the body's cells to insulin, which makes blood sugar harder to control. Both steady training and intervals reduce it, intervals by a small to moderate margin more. (Chapter 10)

**Intensity domains.** The physiologists' division of exercise into moderate (below the first threshold), heavy (between the first threshold and critical power) and severe (above critical power). Fatigue develops differently in each. (Chapters 1 and 5)

**kJ/kg.** The work done on a ride, in kilojoules, the figure a bike computer shows, per kilogram of body weight. Studies of durability use it to say how much riding came before an effort. (Chapter 9)

**Lactate.** A molecule that muscle makes from glucose all the time and that other tissues burn as fuel. Its level in the blood, measured in millimoles per litre (mmol/L), sits around 1 mmol/L at rest in most people and rises when it is made faster than it is cleared, which is how the thresholds are found. (Chapters 1 and 5)

**Maximal lactate steady state (MLSS).** The highest constant power at which blood lactate stays steady, found from 30-minute rides at different powers on four or five separate days. A laboratory measure of the second threshold. (Chapter 5)

**Meta-analysis.** A study that pools the results of earlier studies into one overall estimate. It is only as good as the studies it pools, and an early one can inherit their exaggeration. (Chapter 8)

**Mitochondria.** The structures in muscle cells that use oxygen to make energy, in the form of ATP. Steady riding and intervals both add to them. The building of new mitochondria is called mitochondrial biogenesis. (Chapters 2 and 7)

**Neuromuscular system.** The chain that turns an intention into a pedal stroke: the motor cortex in the brain, the spinal cord, the motor neurons and the muscle fibres each one controls. (Chapter 3)

**Non-responder.** Someone who trains and does not improve. In a Zurich study, everyone who failed to improve on one to three hours a week improved once they trained more. (Chapter 2)

**Observational study.** A study that measures people as they are, without assigning a treatment. It can show that two things go together but not which causes which. Studies of professionals are almost always observational. (Chapter 4)

**Overreaching.** A drop in performance from training harder than the body can absorb. Functional overreaching is short and turns into improvement after rest; non-functional overreaching lasts longer and does not. (Chapter 12)

**Overtraining syndrome.** A prolonged failure to adapt to training, from which recovery takes months to years. (Chapter 12)

**p-value.** The probability of a result at least as large as the one observed if there were really no effect. By convention, a result is statistically significant when the p-value is below 0.05. (Chapter 4)

**p-hacking.** Choosing how to analyse the data after seeing it, and settling, often without any dishonesty, on the choices that give the more interesting result. It makes false positives far more common than the p-value promises. (Chapter 4)

**Pain threshold and pain tolerance.** The pain threshold is the point at which a stimulus, such as pressure or cold, is first felt as painful. Pain tolerance is how long, or how much of it, a person will bear before asking for it to stop. (Chapter 3)

**Periodisation.** Arranging training in phases over weeks and months. Block periodisation concentrates the hard sessions into a block of a week or two, followed by easier weeks. (Chapter 17)

**PGC-1α.** A protein that switches on the genes for building mitochondria, often called the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis. Exercise activates it through sensors such as AMPK, and a hard ride raises its message more than an easy one. (Chapters 2 and 7)

**Placebo.** A dummy treatment made to look like the real one, so that volunteers' expectations affect both groups alike. (Chapter 4)

**Plasma volume.** The volume of the fluid part of the blood. It expands within the first two to four weeks of training, and it is most of what two weeks off the bike costs. (Chapter 2)

**Polarised training.** A training distribution with more time above the second threshold than between the thresholds, on top of a large amount of easy riding. (Chapter 8)

**Power–duration curve.** The best power a rider can hold for each length of effort, from seconds to hours. It falls steeply at first and then flattens towards critical power. (Chapter 1)

**Pyramidal training.** A training distribution with more time between the thresholds than above the second, on top of a large amount of easy riding. (Chapter 8)

**Ramp test.** A test in which power rises every minute or so until the rider cannot continue. It finds VO₂max and peak power, but its stages are too short to find the first threshold. (Chapters 1 and 6)

**Randomised controlled trial.** A study in which volunteers are allocated at random to a treatment or to a comparison, so that the groups differ only by chance and by the treatment. The strongest form of evidence for cause and effect. (Chapter 4)

**Rating of perceived exertion (RPE).** A rider's score for how hard an effort feels, usually on Gunnar Borg's scale, which runs from 6 to 20. (Chapter 3)

**Replication crisis.** The finding, first on a large scale in psychology in 2015, that many published results shrink or disappear when other groups repeat the studies. (Chapter 4)

**Second threshold.** In this book, the highest intensity at which the body can still reach a steady state. Critical power, maximal lactate steady state and FTP all estimate it, and they are not interchangeable. Also called LT2 and VT2. (Chapter 5)

**Slow-twitch fibres.** See Type I fibres.

**Sprint intervals.** Repeated efforts of about 30 seconds, all-out, with three or four minutes of rest between them. (Chapter 11)

**Statistically significant.** Describes a result whose p-value is below 0.05. It says nothing about how large or important an effect is, and a real effect measured in a small study often fails to reach it. (Chapter 4)

**Step test.** A test in which power rises in stages of several minutes, with a drop of blood or the breathing gases measured at the end of each, used to find the thresholds. (Chapters 5 and 6)

**Stroke volume.** The volume of blood the heart pumps with each beat. Joyner and Coyle identify it as the most important single difference between champions and everyone else. (Chapter 1)

**Systematic review.** A review that looks for every study on a question by a stated method, rather than choosing the ones that suit an argument. It is often combined with a meta-analysis. (Chapter 7)

**Talk test.** A way of finding the first threshold by speech: in a test of rising intensity, the rider recites a set passage at the end of each stage and says whether they could speak comfortably. (Chapter 6)

**Taper.** A planned cut in training before an event, reducing the hours while keeping the hard sessions, so that the rider arrives fresh. (Chapter 17)

**Time near VO₂max.** How long a session keeps a rider close to their maximum oxygen uptake, the usual way of comparing interval sessions. Nobody has shown that it is what produces the adaptation. (Chapter 11)

**Type I fibres.** Slow-twitch muscle fibres, built for sustained aerobic work. They seem to produce force with less energy wasted as heat. (Chapter 1)

**Ventilatory thresholds (VT1 and VT2).** The first and second thresholds as found from breathing rather than blood lactate. At VT1, breathing first rises out of proportion to oxygen uptake. (Chapter 5)

**VO₂max.** The maximum rate at which the body can take up and use oxygen, usually given in millilitres per kilogram of body weight per minute. The first factor in the standard model of endurance. VO₂peak is the highest value reached in a particular test. (Chapter 1)

**W′ (W prime).** The fixed amount of work a rider can do above critical power before having to stop, measured in kilojoules. (Chapter 1)

**Zone 2.** In this book, the upper part of the easy range, immediately below the first threshold, as an expert panel defined it in 2025. In the three-zone model used in research, zone 2 means between the thresholds. (Chapter 5)

**Zones.** Bands of intensity. The three-zone model puts zone 1 below the first threshold, zone 2 between the thresholds and zone 3 above the second. Coaching scales, such as the Norwegian federation's, use five or more. (Chapters 5 and 8)
